To fix Exact P-Values

What we found

We found 1 imprecise p value out of 6 detected p values.

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text
p > 0.05 The 33 included isolates did not differ significantly from the 35 not included in patient age, sex, geographic origin, MGIT-based phenotypic profiles, or mutation patterns detected by line probe assays (p > 0.05; Supplementary Table 1).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

American Psychological Association (2020). Publication manual of the American Psychological Association, 7 edition. American Psychological Association.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a leading infectious cause of death, with drug-resistant TB (DR-TB) challenging disease control. Brazil has a high TB burden, and Rio Grande do Sul state reports persistently high incidence and drug-resistance rates. The drivers shaping the regional DR-TB burden remain poorly characterised.

We conducted genomic surveillance of confirmed DR-TB cases in Rio Grande do Sul, Brazil, from January 2023 to September 2024. Clinical isolates underwent genotypic (gDST) and phenotypic drug susceptibility testing (pDST) and whole-genome sequencing (WGS). We analyzed 33 newly sequenced genomes and 267 historical Mycobacterium tuberculosis genomes collected in 2011-2019 to characterize resistance, transmission, and spatiotemporal patterns and estimate the contributions of transmitted and acquired rifampicin resistance.

Molecular testing detected rifampicin resistance missed by pDST, including borderline rpoB mutations in 18.2% and emerging resistance-associated variants in 3% of the 33 analyzed isolates. Conversely, pDST detected baseline bedaquiline resistance missed by WGS in one newly diagnosed case. Among 300 genomes, 181 (60.3%) clustered into 32 genomic clusters at a 5-SNP threshold, including 12 with long-term circulation and several spanning prisons and communities. Sublineage frequencies remained stable over time, whereas we observed marked geographic structuring and localized clonal expansion.

Among 178 rifampicin-resistant isolates, transmission accounted for an estimated 79.2-88.2% of resistance, corresponding to 11.8-20.8% of cases arising through de novo acquisition during treatment.

Our findings identify active transmission as the main driver of rifampicin-resistant TB in Rio Grande do Sul, with long-term transmission chains extending across both incarcerated and community settings. These results underscore the urgent need to strengthen timely TB case detection and resistance testing, enhance contact investigations, and implement targeted interventions aiming to interrupt persistent transmission chains.

INTRODUCTION

Tuberculosis (TB) is the leading cause of death from a single infectious agent worldwide, with an estimated 10.6 million new cases and 1.3 million deaths in 2025. Brazil is among the 30 countries with the highest TB burden worldwide, with an estimated incidence of 39.5 cases per https://paperpile.com/c/TunZXb/bwL500,000 population 1 . In Brazil, Rio Grande do Sul is a particularly important setting for TB prevention and care. Located at the country's southernmost border, the state reported a TB incidence of 51.1 cases per 100,000 population in 2025 and over 1,000 new drug-resistant TB (DR-TB) cases between 2015 and 2025 2.

Drug resistance is one of the greatest challenges to global TB prevention and care, compromising treatment effectiveness, prolonging infectiousness, and increasing mortality and healthcare costs. The emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR-TB) and extensively drug-resistant TB (XDR-TB) has further complicated care, particularly where access to rapid diagnostics remains limited 3 . DR-TB arises through two pathways: transmission of already resistant strains, or de novo acquisition of resistance-conferring mutations during treatment, driven by inadequate regimens, poor adherence, or subtherapeutic drug exposure 4.

Distinguishing between them is essential, because transmitted resistance requires active case-finding and infection prevention and control to interrupt transmission chains, whereas acquired resistance requires effective regimens, adequate drug exposure, and patient-centered adherence support.

Since 2019, World Health Organization (WHO) has prioritized shorter, all-oral regimens including bedaquiline, delamanid, pretomanid, and linezolid 5 . In Brazil, bedaquiline and delamanid were incorporated into the Unified Health System (SUS) in 2020 and became available for routine use in 2021 6 . Nevertheless, data on resistance to these recently introduced drugs and its molecular mechanisms remain scarce in Brazil, where genomic surveillance of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M. tuberculosis) remains underutilized despite its resolution for characterizing resistance mutations, identifying lineages, and distinguishing transmitted from acquired resistance 7.

We therefore used whole-genome sequencing (WGS), integrated with phenotypic and epidemiological data, to characterize the genomic determinants of resistance among DR-TB isolates circulating in Rio Grande do Sul, southern Brazil, estimate the relative contribution of transmitted versus acquired resistance, and identify major genomic clusters and their geographic distribution across the state.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population and drug susceptibility testing

Between January 2023 and September 2024, the Central Public Health Laboratory of Rio Grande do Sul (LACEN-RS), the state reference laboratory for mycobacterial culture and drug susceptibility testing, identified 68 culture-positive M. tuberculosis isolates from unique patients resistant to rifampicin and/or isoniazid by at least one genotypic or phenotypic test. Samples were submitted by healthcare facilities across the state following suspicion of drug resistance. Isolates were classified according to WHO definitions: MDR-TB as resistance to at least isoniazid and rifampicin; pre-XDR-TB as MDR/RR-TB (rifampicin-resistant TB) with additional resistance to any fluoroquinolone; and XDR-TB as MDR/RR-TB with fluoroquinolone resistance plus resistance to bedaquiline or linezolid. rpoB mutations previously reported to confer low-level rifampicin resistance that may be missed by pDST were classified as borderline mutations 8, 9.

Whole-genome sequencing

A convenience sample of 36 of the 68 available cultures was selected for WGS, based on sequencing cost constraints and aiming to include different geographic regions of the state. DNA was extracted from solid cultures using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method 10 . Libraries were prepared with the Illumina DNA Prep kit (Illumina, San Diego, USA) and sequenced on a MiSeq platform with V3 600-cycle chemistry (2×150 bp). TB-Profiler (v6.3.0) 11 was used to assign SNP-based lineages and identify fixed and low-frequency (heteroresistant) resistance-associated mutations, using a minimum allele frequency of 10% and a minimum read depth of 10×, against the WHO Catalogue of Mutations in M. tuberculosis 12 . We also implemented an in-house pipeline: Trimmomatic v0.39 13 for adapter removal and quality trimming 13 , FastQC for read-quality verification, BWA-MEM v0.7.19 14 for alignment to the M. tuberculosis H37Rv reference genome (NC_000962.3), and Qualimap v2.2.1 15 to confirm a minimum average depth of 30×. SNPs and small indels were called with both SAMtools/BCFtools v1.2.1 16 and GATK HaplotypeCaller v4.6.1.0 17 , retaining only concordant variants 18 . Sites and isolates with more than 10% missing data were excluded, and PE/PPE genes and resistance-associated loci were masked in the whole-genome SNP alignment. Three genomes below the 30× mean-depth threshold were excluded, leaving 33 sequences for downstream analyses.

Phylogenetic and clustering analysis

To enable temporal comparisons of transmission dynamics and resistance acquisition, we combined the 33 newly sequenced genomes with 267 historical isolates collected in Rio Grande do Sul between 2011 and 2019, comprising previously published sequences 19, 20 and unpublished data from our group, yielding 300 genomes for phylogenetic reconstruction, phylodynamic inference, and genomic cluster identification.

ModelFinder 21 selected the TVM+F nucleotide substitution model under the Akaike Information Criterion, and a maximum-likelihood phylogeny was reconstructed with IQ-TREE2 22 , using 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates and SH-aLRT, annotated with ggtree. Genomic clusters were defined using a 5-SNP threshold 23, 24 , with hierarchical clustering (hclust, R package ape). Patient addresses, georeferenced at the neighborhood level, were mapped using ggplot2, sf, and geobr.

Relative contribution of transmitted versus acquired rifampicin resistance

Rifampicin resistance was assessed based on the presence of resistance-associated mutations in rpoB. Patients with no prior history of TB treatment were classified as having transmitted rifampicin resistance, as the absence of previous drug exposure precludes treatment-mediated selection. For previously treated patients, the origin of resistance was determined using a combined clinical and phylogenetic approach implemented in the TB-SIMEX pipeline (https://github.com/RuanSpies21/TB-SIMEX) 25 , mapping resistance mutations onto the phylogeny and applying two complementary approaches (N and N-1) to define the plausible range of resistance origins. Complete parameters and scripts are available in Supplementary Methods.

Temporal, spatial, and diversity patterns of M. tuberculosis sublineages

Lineage assignments, collection years, and municipality of origin were analyzed in R (dplyr, tidyr, ggplot2). Sublineages represented by fewer than five isolates were excluded from spatial inferential analyses, geographic association tests were restricted to the ten municipalities with the most sampled isolates, and sublineages were aggregated at the third lineage level for temporal analyses. Within-municipality sublineage diversity was quantified using Simpson's Diversity Index (1 -D), ranging from 0 (complete dominance of a single sublineage) to 1 (high diversity). Fisher's exact test was used to calculate exact odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between dominant sublineages and their respective municipalities. Temporal and geographic structuring of sublineages was evaluated using Pearson's Chi-square test of independence. Because many cells had expected frequencies below five, p-values were obtained by Monte Carlo simulation with 10,000 permutations. When a significant overall geographic association was detected, post-hoc analysis of standardized residuals identified the municipality-sublineage combinations contributing most strongly to it, with absolute values ≥ 1.96 considered significant (p < 0.05).

RESULTS

In 2023 and 2024, Rio Grande do Sul reported 152 new cases of DR-TB. Over the 21-month study period, 68 isolates were referred to LACEN-RS. Of these, 36 were selected for WGS, and 33 passed bioinformatic quality control, the three excluded had mean coverage below 30×. The 33 included isolates did not differ significantly from the 35 not included in patient age, sex, geographic origin, MGIT-based phenotypic profiles, or mutation patterns detected by line probe assays (p > 0.05; Supplementary Table 1). Patients came from 14 municipalities, most frequently Porto Alegre (n=10), Canoas (n=7), and Alvorada (n=3). The remaining municipalities contributed one or two isolates each. Patients were predominantly male (78.8%), aged 19 to 71 years (mean 37.2), and seven (21.2%) were incarcerated at the time of diagnosis. Based on pDST and available molecular testing (Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra and LPA), the 33 isolates were classified as 15 MDR, 13 isoniazid-resistant, two pre-XDR-TB, two RR-TB, and one resistant to both isoniazid and bedaquiline.

Isoniazid resistance was detected by pDST in 31/33 isolates (93.9%), of which 26 (83.9%) were also resistant by LPA (Figure 1A), mainly through the katG MUT1 (Ser315Thr; n = 21) and inhA MUT1 (-15C>T; n = 4) probes. Of the five pDST-resistant isolates without LPA-detected mutations, WGS identified katG Asp735Ala, outside the LPA target region, in one (a pre-XDR-TB isolate). No resistance-associated mutation was found in the other four or in the two pDST-susceptible isolates. For rifampicin, 11/33 isolates (33.3%) were resistant by pDST (Figure 1B), Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra and LPA detected 10 of these (90.9%), mostly through the MUT3 probe (rpoB Ser450Leu; n = 6). The only isolate missed by both assays carried a 12-nucleotide rpoB insertion associated with a genomic cluster endemic to Rio Grande do Sul 26 . Conversely, 9 of the 22 pDST-susceptible isolates (40.9%) were resistant by at least one molecular method. WGS explained seven of these discordances with borderline rpoB mutations (Asp435Tyr, n = 6; and Leu430Pro, n = 1, at 42% of reads, consistent with heteroresistance).

Two isolates (2/33; 6.06%) were fluoroquinolone-resistant by both pDST and LPA, with gyrA Asp94Asn and gyrA Ser91Pro confirmed by WGS, both were also resistant to rifampicin and isoniazid. One isolate from a newly diagnosed TB patient was phenotypically resistant to both bedaquiline and isoniazid (1/33; 3.03%), although WGS identified no known bedaquiline resistance-associated mutation. The complete pDST, gDST, and wgsDST data are available in Supplementary Table All 33 sequenced isolates belonged to Lineage 4. The most prevalent sublineages were 4.1.2 (13/33; 39.39%), 4.1.2.1 (6/33; 18.18%), and 4.3.3 (6/33; 18.18%), followed by 4.3.2, 4.3.4.1, and 4.3.4.2 (2/33 each; 6.06%) and 4.8 and 4.4.1.1 (1/33 each; 3.03%), the latter two resistant exclusively to isoniazid and detected only by pDST. Among the 27 isolates with isoniazid resistance-associated mutations, katG Ser315Thr predominated (21/27; 77.78%), followed by the fabG1-inhA operon -15C>T promoter mutation (4/27; 14.81%), with katG Ser315Asn and Asp735Ala in one isolate each. Rifampicin resistance-associated mutations were identified in 17 isolates (51.5%), most frequently the WHO-defined borderline mutation rpoB Asp435Tyr (8/17; 47.06%; one also harboring Leu430Pro) and rpoB Ser450Leu (6/17; 35.30%); rpoB Leu430Pro alone, rpoB His445Tyr, and a 12-nucleotide insertion at codon 435 of rpoB occurred in one isolate each (1/17; 5.88%). Streptomycin resistance-associated mutations were identified in 10/33 isolates (30.30%), most frequently gid 351delG (8/10; 80.0%), with gid Pro84Leu and a premature stop-gain mutation (gid Gln127*) in one isolate each. Ethambutol resistance-associated mutations were found in 4/33 isolates (12.12%), mainly embB Met306Val (3/4; 75%), with one embA -12C>T promoter mutation. Fluoroquinolone resistance-associated mutations were found in 2/33 isolates (6.06%), both in gyrA.

We next analyzed the 33 genomes sequenced in this study together with 267 genomes collected between 2011 and 2019, comprising previously published data 20, 26 and unpublished sequences generated by our research group, totalling 300 genomes from 48 municipalities collected between 2011 and 2024. Most isolates originated from the state capital, Porto Alegre (n=107), followed by the metropolitan municipalities of Alvorada (n=28), Canoas (n=19), Viamão (n=17), and Cachoeirinha (n=11). A total of 181 of the 300 isolates (60.33%) were grouped into 32 genomic clusters. We defined persistence as the presence within the same cluster of at least one isolate from the initial surveillance period (2011-2014) and at least one from a later period (2018-2024), indicating continuous circulation. Twelve clusters (37.50%) met this criterion, demonstrating sustained transmission throughout the study period (Figure 2A).

Of the 300 isolates, 133 (44.33%) were from newly diagnosed patients with no previous anti-TB treatment, accounting for 43.70% (52/119) of non-clustered and 44.75% (81/181) of clustered isolates, with no significant difference (p = 0.906; OR = 1.04, 95% CI 0.64-1.71). Porto Alegre accounted for the largest proportion of clustered isolates (33.15%), followed by Alvorada (12.71%), Canoas (8.29%), and Viamão (6.08%), with substantial clusters also in the regional centers of Caxias do Sul (5.52%) and Santa Cruz do Sul (4.97%). The largest cluster (GC1) comprised 32 isolates of sublineage 4.1.2, including 10 from the current study; 26 (81.25%) originated from Porto Alegre and its metropolitan region and six were from incarcerated individuals, all collected from 2018 onwards. The second-largest cluster (GC2) comprised 20 isolates of sublineage 4.1.2.1, 16 (80%) from Porto Alegre and its metropolitan region; nine were from incarcerated individuals, whereas all isolates collected from 2013 onward were from community settings, indicating a temporal shift. Two GC2 isolates from 2018 were pre-XDR.

GC3 comprised 16 isolates of sublineage 4.3.3 RD115, 15 assigned to spoligotype SIT 863/PINI2.

Eleven harbored a 12-nucleotide insertion at codon 435 of rpoB, identified in 2011, 2018, and 2023, indicating its persistence within the cluster; isolates without the insertion collected in 2023 and 2024 fell within the same cluster, indicating co-circulation and ongoing transmission of both variants. Most isolates were from incarcerated individuals in different prison establishments, particularly in Porto Alegre and Charqueadas. GC4, GC5, and GC6 comprised 13, 10, and 9 isolates, respectively, all from community settings, whereas smaller clusters varied in their association with incarceration: four of nine isolates in GC7, one of seven in GC8, five of six in GC9 (the remaining isolate from a person experiencing homelessness), and one of five in GC10 (Figure 2B). All six GC9 isolates originated from Santa Cruz do Sul. Complete information on each genomic cluster is provided in Supplementary Table 3.

We found no significant temporal change in sublineage distribution over the 14-year surveillance period (p = 0.144), indicating stable relative frequencies of the main circulating sublineages. In contrast, sublineage distribution differed significantly among the ten municipalities with the most sampled isolates (p < 0.001). Post-hoc standardized residuals indicated enrichment of sublineage 4.3.4.2 in Santa Cruz do Sul (z = 6.13) and 4.1.1.1 in Viamão (z = 6.03), both of which remained significant under the more conservative Bonferroni correction, together with 4.7 in Cachoeirinha (z = 3.67), 4.1.2 in Alvorada (z = 3.35), and 4.3.3 in Caxias do Sul (z = 3.14). No municipality showed a significant depletion of any sublineage, and Porto Alegre, Canoas, São Leopoldo, Gravataí, and Charqueadas showed no significant enrichment, consistent with a more heterogeneous distribution of circulating strains (Figure 3).

Within-municipality diversity (Simpson's Diversity Index, 1 -D) further highlighted marked spatial heterogeneity. Viamão (0.85), Porto Alegre (0.84), and Cachoeirinha (0.84) exhibited the highest diversity, whereas Santa Cruz do Sul showed extreme monoclonality (1 -D = 0.18), with 90% (9/10) of isolates belonging to sublineage 4.3.4.2, indicating localized clonal expansion. These nine isolates were grouped into two distinct clusters: GC9, linking five incarcerated individuals and one person experiencing homelessness, and GC4, grouping three residents of the same neighborhood. Among the 300 genomes, 178 were classified as rifampicin-resistant based on resistance-associated rpoB mutations. Of these, 80 (44.94%) were from patients receiving their first anti-TB treatment, of which 59 (73.75%) were clustered. The remaining 98 (55.06%) were from previously treated patients (61, 62.24%, treatment failures; 37, 37.76%, losses to follow-up), of which 74 (75.51%) were clustered. Because resistance profiles at first diagnosis were unavailable for previously treated patients, the origin of rifampicin resistance in this group was assessed using the phylogenetic approach 25 . At the observed sampling fraction, transmission accounted for 88.2% (95% CI, 83.8-91.9%) of RR-TB under the N approach and 79.2% (77.2-85.4%) under the N-1 approach, with de novo acquisition accounting for 11.8% (8.1-16.2%) and 20.8% (14.6-22.8%), respectively (Figure 4), giving a plausible range of 79.2-88.2% for transmission. Extrapolating to complete sampling with SIMEX, transmission accounted for 95.2% (80.6-99.0%) and 94.7% (70.2-99.3%) under the N and N-1 approaches, respectively.

DISCUSSION

Rio Grande do Sul, in southern Brazil, is among the five Brazilian states with the highest number of DR-TB cases, making it a priority setting for TB care and transmission control. By integrating WGS with epidemiological and phenotypic data, we characterized the genomic determinants of resistance and demonstrated that sustained transmission of resistant strains is the primary driver of the current RR-TB burden.

Our molecular-based resistance prediction demonstrated the importance of molecular assays for detecting first-and second-line drug resistance, particularly rifampicin resistance caused by borderline rpoB mutations that pDST often misses: approximately 41% of isolates classified as rifampicin-susceptible by pDST were potentially misclassified, most harboring these mutations. These confer clinically relevant resistance comparable to the canonical Ser450Leu substitution, despite reduced in vitro growth that may lead to false susceptibility in pDST 8, 9 . Emerging variants harboring unusual resistance-conferring mutations can also be missed by molecular tools; the 12-nucleotide rpoB insertion, detected in one isolate here, belongs to an emerging endemic strain associated with an important genomic cluster in the state 20, 26 . These findings support Brazil's integrated approach to drug resistance diagnosis, in which LPAs, introduced into the SUS in 2021, complement pDST, which remains important for detecting resistance that molecular assays may miss https://paperpile.com/c/TunZXb/xVTWO.

Our findings also underscore the value of molecular testing directly from sputum: Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra alone identified two isolates as rifampicin-resistant, and WGS identified heteroresistance in one, enabling earlier and more accurate treatment decisions 28 . Because the frequency of borderline rpoB mutations and other emerging variants varies across regions, WGS-based surveillance is essential to inform diagnostic algorithms and treatment policies in TB programs. Conversely, we identified by pDST one case of resistance to bedaquiline, recently introduced in Brazil, that WGS did not detect, in a newly diagnosed patient with no previous exposure to the drug, indicating baseline resistance. Baseline bedaquiline resistance appears to vary geographically, and its mechanisms remain largely unclear 29 ; surveillance combining pDST and WGS is therefore essential at the regional level to preserve the effectiveness of this drug 30.

Clustering analysis of 300 drug-resistant isolates collected between 2011 and 2024 revealed a clustering rate of 60.33% across 32 genomic clusters, most concentrated in the Porto Alegre metropolitan area. The persistence of several of these clusters, which likely mirror transmission chains, indicates they remained active and uncontrolled for more than a decade in both prison and community settings, with evidence of dispersal from the capital toward the state interior.

Sublineage composition remained temporally stable over the 14 years, with sustained circulation of multiple established sublineages. The marked geographic structuring observed among municipalities suggests that spatial processes played a greater role than temporal turnover in shaping population structure, consistent with localized transmission dynamics and heterogeneous population connectivity. The high diversity in metropolitan municipalities likely reflects greater population mobility and connectivity. Conversely, the predominance of sublineage 4.3.4.2 in Santa Cruz do Sul, where 90% of sampled isolates belonged to this sublineage, suggests localized expansion of a restricted genetic background; their identification in two independent genomic clusters indicates sustained circulation of different, closely related transmission chains rather than a single chain. In contrast, GC4, GC5, and GC6 comprised exclusively community-associated isolates, mostly from Porto Alegre and its metropolitan region but with genetically related isolates in other regions of the state, suggesting that some transmission chains extend beyond their main areas of concentration.

The genomic clusters identified further illustrate how transmission may be structured by social and epidemiological settings. GC1 was predominantly community-associated, whereas GC2 and GC3 showed substantial representation of isolates from incarcerated individuals, highlighting prisons as important environments for maintaining and amplifying transmission chains 31 . The predominance of prison-associated isolates in more recent years in GC1 and the shift from prison-to community-associated isolates in GC2 indicate transmission connectivity between prison facilities and the wider community. In GC3, isolates carrying and lacking the rpoB codon 435 insertion co-circulated across sampling years, demonstrating persistence within a sustained transmission chain rather than recent emergence of a single resistant genotype 26.

Taken together, these findings indicate sustained transmission of M. tuberculosis across both prison and community settings, showing that potentially uncontrolled transmission is not restricted to prisons. Consistently, transmission was the predominant determinant of RR-TB in our analyses: in addition to the substantial proportion of RR-TB cases among patients with no previous history of TB treatment, phylogenetic-based inference attributed 79.2-88.2% of RR-TB cases to transmission. Similar estimates have been reported in Viet Nam (72-87%) using the same approach 25 , and in clustering analyses from South Africa (approximately 70%) and China (approximately 61%) 32, 33.

Several operational indicators from the TB prevention and care program may help explain the sustained transmission observed. In 2024, only 43.5% of newly diagnosed pulmonary TB cases in the state underwent rapid molecular testing (64% in Porto Alegre), culture was performed in 23% of new cases, and drug susceptibility testing in only 56% of retreatment cases with positive cultures; about 65% of identified contacts were examined, with no specific data for contacts of DR-TB cases. These indicators broadly follow the national pattern and reflect broader operational and structural challenges faced by TB programs 34 , including a high number of diagnosed cases, budgetary constraints, and laboratory capacity centralized in a small number of reference or regional laboratories. Although they do not establish a causal relationship with the transmission patterns observed, they highlight gaps in diagnostic coverage, resistance detection, and contact investigation that may facilitate the continued circulation of drug-resistant strains in both community and prison settings.

Our study has important limitations. We sequenced only a subset (52.9%; n = 36) of the 68 DR-TB isolates identified during the study period, with 33 genomes retained after quality filtering, which may not fully capture the genomic diversity of circulating strains and may have missed rare or emerging resistance variants. Furthermore, the historical dataset did not have homogeneous representation of resistant cases across the state over time, representing approximately 25% of registered cases during the period. Despite implementing the TB-SIMEX framework and simulation-extrapolation, limited sampling may still have introduced bias, as the method assumes that unsampled cases are missing at random.

In conclusion, the DR-TB burden in Rio Grande do Sul appears to result largely from uncontrolled transmission, and we found evidence of baseline resistance to the recently introduced bedaquiline, threatening the sustainability of this drug. These findings point to an urgent need for population-level interventions that prioritize resistance detection among new cases through molecular and phenotypic testing, improve contact investigation, and target persistent transmission chains, particularly in prison and community settings.
