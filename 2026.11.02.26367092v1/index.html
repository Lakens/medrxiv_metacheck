How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. This module retrieves information from repositories.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Abstract

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) produces heterogeneous electrical phenotypes whose continuous organization, longitudinal evolution and clinical relevance remain poorly defined. We developed complementary variationalautoencoder representations to map HCM ECG morphology relative to physiological variation and characterize within-subject change. Using 5,194 normalreference ECGs and 2,594 longitudinal visits from 650 individuals with HCM, we found that HCM overlapped the physiological manifold but extended towards its periphery, while an HCM-oriented manifold largely reorganized shared electrical modes. Mixed-effects models identified longitudinal drift in selected phenotypic axes. A lateral/frontal QRS-force coordinate remained time-dependent after censoring septal reduction therapy, atrial fibrillation and pacing, and lower values were associated with subsequent heart-failure risk in baseline and time-updated analyses. A time-conditioned change VAE identified four interpretable within-subject modes aligned with longer-term trajectories. Sarcomeric genotype was associated with phenotype position but not trajectory rate.

1. Introduction

Understanding how disease expression evolves over time is a central challenge in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM). The condition encompasses heterogeneous genetic substrates, electrical phenotypes and clinical trajectories [6, 1, 21], with some individuals remaining stable for decades, whereas others developing heart failure or life-threatening arrhythmias. Although pathogenic sarcomeric variants contribute to this heterogeneity, they do not fully determine an individual's disease course [20, 22]. Relating phenotypic variation to longitudinal evolution therefore requires representations that capture both a patient's current electrical state and its trajectory over time.

The 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) provides an accessible, repeatable measurement of cardiac electrical expression. In HCM, depolarization and repolarization abnormalities relate to structural phenotype and myocardial fibrosis and can precede overt hypertrophy in sarcomeric variant carriers [8, 12]. Computational methods have extended this information beyond conventional measurements, enabling HCM detection, identification of electrical subgroups and extraction of interpretable waveform representations [18, 15, 17]. Supervised ECG models have also tracked treatment-related changes and longitudinal variation in echocardiographic risk features [14, 7, 26, 28, 24, 5]. While these approaches demonstrate the rich clinical information contained in serial ECGs, having been optimized for predefined targets, they do not directly describe the multiple morphological directions along which electrical phenotypes evolve.

To address this limitation, we learn the geometry of ECG variation directly from waveform morphology using variational autoencoders (VAEs), without supervision from genotype, clinical severity or outcomes. In a learned manifold, a low-dimensional representation organizing waveform variation, each recording defines an electrical phenotype, and successive recordings trace its trajectory. This formulation allows us to ask whether structure learned from ECG morphology contains information about subsequent clinical course that was not supplied during training, while distinguishing departure from normal electrical variation, heterogeneity within HCM, and change within an individual. We investigate this possibility using baseline and serial ECGs from the Sarcomeric Human Cardiomyopathy Registry (SHaRe) [11], together with an independent reference set of normal ECGs from PTB-XL [27] and an external HCM cohort from MIMIC-IV-ECG [9]. Variational autoencoders learn separate manifolds of normal ECG morphol-2 ogy and of variation within HCM, while MIMIC-IV ECGs are projected into the frozen representations to assess external reproducibility. Serial recordings are projected into these fixed latent spaces to characterize longitudinal trajectories. A complementary time-conditioned model learns waveform changes between consecutive visits while accounting for elapsed time. Genotype, clinical events and outcomes are introduced exclusively after representation learning, enabling their relationships with the resulting electrical phenotypes to be assessed without defining those phenotypes in advance (Fig. 1). Our aim is to determine whether unsupervised ECG representations can characterize HCM electrical phenotype across complementary levels, including deviation from physiological variation, heterogeneity within established HCM and longitudinal within-patient change. We further examine whether these representations identify interpretable and reproducible electrical trajectories, how these trajectories relate to major clinical transitions and sarcomeric genotype, and whether selected phenotypes are associated with subsequent clinical outcomes. More broadly, we ask whether serial ECGs can provide a state-and-change representation of HCM without optimizing the representation for predefined diagnostic or prognostic targets.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1. Study design and HCM cohort harmonization

The HCM cohort was derived from the Sarcomeric Human Cardiomyopathy Registry (SHaRe). Methods regarding SHaRe data collection have been previously described [11]. The HCM dataset initially contained 2,705 raw ECG identifiers. Identity reconciliation and removal of 107 redundant ECG identifiers yielded 2,598 unique visits from 650 coherent subjects. Of these, 649 subjects contributed an eligible baseline ECG for median-beat morphological analysis, with one baseline record excluded because it did not meet the prespecified minimum requirement of at least two usable aligned beats. Longitudinal visits were chronologically ordered within each coherent analysis subject. Four visits failed the minimum quality requirement for latent projection, leaving 2,594 valid visits. The primary longitudinal cohort required at least two valid visits at distinct time points and contained 493 subjects and 2,437 visits; the three-or-more-visit sensitivity cohort contained 376 subjects and 2,203 visits. Clinical characterization was restricted to the 623 subjects with an exact non-ambiguous ECG-to-SHaRe mapping. Baseline was defined by the first valid waveform ECG. Clinical-history variables were referenced to this baseline when event timing was available, and echocardiographic measurements were obtained from the examination nearest to the baseline ECG within a ±7-day window.

2.2. Normal-reference cohort

The physiological reference cohort was derived from PTB-XL [27]. Eligible ECGs were required to have the SCP-ECG code NORM with likelihood 100, with no additional diagnostic SCP codes other than the physiological rhythm codes SR, SBRAD, STACH or SARRH. Subjects were additionally restricted to 18-90 years of age, and only ECGs marked as human-validated in PTB-XL were retained. These criteria identified 5,417 eligible ECG records from 5,194 unique subjects. To avoid repeated observations from the same individual in the physiological reference model, one ECG was selected per subject, using the earliest available recording when multiple eligible records were present, yielding 5, 194 Normal-reference ECGs. The Normal cohort was used to train 4 and evaluate the Normal-anchored VAE and was not used to train either the HCM-oriented static VAE or the longitudinal Delta-VAE.

2.3. ECG harmonization and median-beat construction

ECGs were analysed using a 4-s segment corresponding to the first 2,000 samples of each record, featuring a sampling rate of 500 Hz. Each lead was filtered using a fourth-order Butterworth band-pass filter from 0.5 to 40 Hz with zero-phase application. Common multilead QRS fiducials were detected across the 12 leads. Beat windows extended from -350 ms to +650 ms relative to each detected multilead QRS fiducial, producing 500 samples per lead. A minimum of two usable beats was required for longitudinal latent projection. Median-beat morphology was analysed separately from rhythm and beat-to-beat descriptors. Consequently, latent morphology coordinates should not be interpreted as intrinsic heart-rate or rhythm representations even when empirical associations with timing-related features are observed.

2.4. Variational autoencoder framework

2.5. Normal-anchored β-VAE

The encoder of the Normal-anchored β-VAE consisted of four one-dimensional convolutional blocks with channel progression 12-32-64-96-128 and temporal downsampling from 500 to 31 samples, followed by a 96-unit fully connected layer producing the posterior mean and log-variance; the decoder mirrored this architecture using transposed convolutions. Hidden convolutional blocks used GroupNorm and SiLU activations. The VAE was trained exclusively on Normal-reference median beats. Median-beat amplitudes were standardized using a single global mean and standard deviation estimated exclusively from the Normal training set. The same transformation was subsequently applied to Normal validation and test ECGs and to all HCM ECGs. No per-record or per-lead amplitude normalization was applied, thereby preserving relative voltage information and cross-lead amplitude relationships. The input shape was 12×500, the latent dimensionality was 16 and the model contained 939,116 trainable parameters. A β-VAE objective was used, with β linearly increased during the first 30 epochs to a target value of 0.01. Model selection based on the Normal validation set began once the target β was reached. HCM ECGs were projected after training with all model weights frozen. The latent posterior mean was used as the deterministic representation for downstream analyses. A Mahalanobis distance d N was computed relative to the Normal-training posterior-mean distribution using a Ledoit-Wolf covariance estimate. Physical reconstruction mean squared error was calculated after inversion of the training-set scaling transformation. Because Normal and HCM data originated from different source datasets, HCM-versus-Normal reconstruction differences and any descriptive discrimination metrics were not interpreted as diagnostic validation.

2.6. HCM-oriented static β-VAE

The encoder of the HCM-oriented static βVAE consisted of four onedimensional convolutional blocks with channel progression 12-16-32-48-64 and temporal downsampling from 500 to 31 samples, followed by a 48-unit fully connected layer producing the posterior mean and log-variance; the decoder mirrored this architecture using transposed convolutions. Hidden convolutional blocks used GroupNorm and SiLU activations. The model was trained on one baseline median-beat ECG per eligible HCM subject. Medianbeat amplitudes were standardized using a single global mean and standard deviation estimated exclusively from the HCM training set and applied unchanged to the validation and test sets. No per-record or per-lead amplitude 6 normalization was performed. Subject-wise HCM train, validation and test partitions contained 454, 97 and 98 ECGs, respectively. Normal-reference ECGs were not used for training or model selection. The input shape was 12 × 500, the latent dimensionality was 8 and the model contained 236,988 trainable parameters. A β-VAE objective was used, with β linearly increased during the first 30 epochs to a target value of 0.01. Model selection based on the HCM validation set began once the target β was reached. The purpose of this model was to characterize continuous electrical heterogeneity within HCM rather than to discriminate HCM from Normal ECGs. A Mahalanobis distance d HCM was computed relative to the HCM-training posterior-mean distribution. Physiological interpretation of HCM latent axes used decoder traversals and associations with ECG morphology features extracted from real median beats.

2.7. Cross-manifold integration

To determine whether HCM-oriented coordinates represented morphology independent of the Normal-anchored space, each frozen HCM latent coordinate was predicted from the 16-dimensional Normal-anchored latent vector using ridge regression with held-out evaluation. High cross-manifold predictability was interpreted as evidence of HCM-oriented reparameterization or recombination of electrical modes present in the physiological reference space. As a descriptive measure of relative reconstruction burden, we additionally calculated ∆RE = RE N -RE HCM , where RE N and RE HCM denote physical reconstruction errors from the frozen Normal-anchored and HCM-oriented VAEs, respectively. Associations between HCM latent coordinates and ∆RE were used to identify phenotypes linked to morphology that was relatively less well represented by the Normal-anchored model. Because the two autoencoders differed in training population and model capacity, ∆RE was interpreted descriptively and not as a calibrated measure of disease specificity. Residual components were therefore treated as off-Normal or not-explained-by-Normal-representation components and were not labelled as disease-specific without additional evidence.

2.8. Longitudinal projection through frozen static manifolds

All valid HCM longitudinal median beats were projected through both frozen static VAEs without additional representation learning. For each visit we retained the Normal-anchored and HCM-oriented posterior-mean latent 7 coordinates, d N , d HCM , physical reconstruction errors and selected regionspecific residual metrics. Baseline projection consistency was checked against the static baseline analyses before longitudinal modelling.

2.9. Mixed-effects analysis of longitudinal electrical trajectories

For each prespecified primary outcome, we fitted a linear mixed-effects model where t ij was time in years from the subject's first valid ECG, β 1 was the population-average annual change and b 1i was the subject-specific deviation from that slope.

Models were fitted by restricted maximum likelihood with random intercept and random slope; a random-intercept-only fallback was allowed only when the random-slope model failed and was explicitly flagged. The 28 primary variables comprised 16 Normal-anchored latent coordinates, eight HCM-oriented latent coordinates, d N , d HCM and the two model-specific physical reconstruction errors. Patient-specific BLUP slopes were exported for successfully fitted random-slope models. Independent subject-wise ordinaryleast-squares (OLS) slopes and first-to-last annualized changes were calculated as model-independent descriptive complements. A sensitivity analysis repeated the primary mixed-effects analysis in subjects with at least three visits. To assess whether longitudinal electrical trajectories were sensitive to major clinical transitions capable of modifying ECG morphology, we performed event-censoring sensitivity analyses using clinical data from the SHaRe subset available to the present study through the SMASH-HCM project. Analyses were restricted to subjects with an exact ECG-to-SHaRe mapping. Septal reduction therapy (SRT) was defined as surgical myectomy or alcohol septal ablation. Separate sensitivity cohorts were constructed by censoring ECG observations occurring at or after the recorded onset of SRT, atrial fibrillation (AF) or pacemaker implantation, and by jointly censoring at the earliest of these three events. Subjects with an event recorded but unavailable event timing were excluded from the corresponding analysis, and only subjects retaining at least two valid ECGs at distinct time points after censoring were analysed. For each sensitivity cohort, the original longitudinal mixed-effects models were refitted without retraining either latent representation, using time from the first valid ECG as the fixed effect 8 and subject-specific random intercepts and slopes. The selected trajectorysensitive outcomes were z HCM 01 , z HCM 05 , z HCM 06 and distance from the Normal manifold (d N ). Benjamini-Hochberg correction was applied across the four variables within each sensitivity analysis.

Clinical-risk association of the longitudinally robust z HCM 06 phenotype To investigate the clinical relevance of the HCM-oriented coordinate showing the most robust longitudinal trajectory, we performed secondary timeto-event analyses focused on z HCM 06 . Analyses were restricted to subjects with an exact ECG-to-SHaRe mapping, a valid baseline z HCM 06 value and sufficient timing information to establish whether each clinical event occurred before or after the first valid ECG. The first valid ECG defined baseline and time zero, and subjects with the event of interest occurring before or at baseline were excluded from the corresponding risk set. Follow-up ended at the first occurrence of the endpoint or at the last available clinical follow-up in subjects without the event. The principal endpoint of this secondary clinical-risk analysis was the SHaRe heart-failure composite, defined as the first occurrence of left ventricular ejection fraction < 35%, New York Heart Association functional class III/IV symptoms, cardiac transplantation or left ventricular assist-device implantation. Baseline analyses additionally evaluated atrial fibrillation, unplanned cardiovascular hospitalization and the overall composite endpoint, with Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate correction applied across these four clinical outcomes. The ventricular-arrhythmic composite, defined as sudden cardiac death, resuscitated cardiac arrest or appropriate implantable cardioverter-defibrillator therapy, was analysed separately as an exploratory endpoint because of the limited number of incident events. For baseline analyses, z HCM 06 was standardized using the standard deviation of baseline z HCM 06 in the full exact-mapped cohort, and Cox proportional-hazards models were fitted both unadjusted and adjusted for baseline age and sex. Hazard ratios therefore represent the relative change in event hazard per 1-SD increase in z HCM 06 . To determine whether serially available phenotype values retained prognostic information, we additionally fitted time-updated Cox models using a start-stop formulation in which the most recent observed z HCM 06 value was carried forward from each ECG until the subsequent ECG, event or censoring time. Only ECG information available before each event contributed to the corresponding risk interval. In a 30-day lag sensitivity analysis, each ECG-derived phenotype value became active 30 days after its acquisition, with follow-up correspondingly left-truncated at 30 days. This 9 prevented phenotype measurements obtained within 30 days before an event from contributing to the corresponding risk interval. Robustness of the timeupdated estimates was further assessed using 2,000 subject-level bootstrap resamples. To explore residual risk after septal reduction therapy (SRT), landmark analyses were performed using the first eligible ECG obtained 30-180 days after SRT, with a secondary 30-365-day window. Only subjects free of the endpoint at the landmark entered the corresponding risk set, and subsequent events were counted from the landmark ECG. These post-SRT analyses were considered exploratory because of the small number of eligible subjects and subsequent events.

2.12. Physiological interpretation of longitudinal change modes

Classical ECG features were not extracted directly from ∆X because a difference waveform is not itself a physiological ECG. Instead, features were extracted independently from X(t 1 ) and X(t 2 ) and transformed into observed feature changes ∆f = f (t 2 ) -f (t 1 ). Spearman associations between each Delta-VAE posterior-mean coordinate and each observed feature change were calculated separately in the training, validation and held-out test subsets. Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate correction was applied globally across all latent-feature associations within each subset, with the held-out test associations used as the primary basis for physiological interpretation. Decoder-based interpretation was performed by varying one latent coordinate from -3 to +3 empirical training-set standard deviations while fixing all remaining latent coordinates at zero. The inter-visit interval was fixed at the median ∆t of the training pairs. Decoded waveform changes were added to real HCM reference beats drawn from the training set, and ECG features were remeasured on the resulting synthetic beats. For each traversal level, feature changes were quantified relative to the corresponding zero-latent decoded trajectory at the same ∆t and summarized across reference beats. A latent-feature relationship was considered a strong internal 11 interpretation when the held-out test association had |ρ| ≥ 0.20 and global FDR q < 0.05, the decoder traversal showed R² ≥ 0.80, and the directions of the observed and traversal-based effects were concordant.

2.13. Cluster-aware robustness of physiological anchors

Because some subjects contributed multiple consecutive ECG pairs, four physiological anchor associations identified in the preceding interpretation analysis, one for each Delta-VAE latent mode, were further assessed using analyses that accounted for within-subject clustering. All analyses were restricted to the held-out Delta-VAE test subset. Pair-level Spearman correlations were retained as descriptive effect estimates, whereas inference was based on three complementary subject-aware procedures: 10,000 subjectcluster bootstrap resamples retaining all pairs from each resampled subject; subject-level aggregation using the median latent coordinate and median feature change for each subject, with 20,000 permutation tests and Benjamini-Hochberg correction across the four selected anchor associations; and 10,000 Monte Carlo repetitions in which one pair was randomly selected per subject. An anchor was considered robust when the subject-cluster bootstrap 95% confidence interval excluded zero, the subject-level association preserved the expected direction with FDR q < 0.05, and at least 90% of one-pair-persubject repetitions preserved the expected sign. Because these four anchors were selected from the preceding physiological interpretation analysis, these analyses were considered cluster-aware robustness assessments rather than statistically independent validation.

2.14. Interval-normalized rate sensitivity analysis

As a secondary sensitivity analysis, a separately trained four-dimensional VAE was fitted to interval-normalized waveform change ∆X/∆t. To reduce amplification of measurement and alignment noise at short inter-visit intervals, pairs separated by less than 0.25 years were excluded. The rate model was not conditioned on ∆t and therefore represented annualized waveform change directly rather than learning an explicit dependence on elapsed time. The resulting analysis included 1,775 consecutive ECG pairs from 488 HCM subjects. Physiological interpretation used annualized feature changes ∆f /∆t and the same held-out association and decoder-traversal framework used for the primary Delta-VAE. Decoder traversals were interpreted on a one-year-equivalent scale. Because independently trained VAE coordinates may permute, invert or rotate, the sensitivity analysis was evaluated for recovery of the same broad physiological change domains rather than for one-to-one correspondence of latent indices.

Integration of Delta-VAE change modes with patient-specific trajectories

For each subject, the signed median of each Delta-VAE coordinate across all consecutive ECG pairs was used as a subject-level summary of typical change-mode expression. These four summaries were compared with patientspecific BLUP slopes for the selected longitudinal targets z HCM 01 , z HCM 05 , z HCM 06 and d N , yielding 16 subject-level associations. Spearman correlation was used, subject-level bootstrap 95% confidence intervals were calculated, and Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction was applied across the 16 associations. The primary integration analysis included 491 subjects. Two sensitivity analyses were performed. First, the same associations were evaluated in the 73 subjects belonging to the held-out Delta-VAE test split to assess whether the observed alignment extended beyond subjects used for model training. Second, BLUP slopes were replaced by empirical subject-wise OLS slopes among 376 subjects with at least three valid ECG visits, providing a modelindependent sensitivity analysis to BLUP shrinkage. These analyses quantify alignment between complementary longitudinal representations and are not considered statistically independent validation because the Delta-VAE summaries and trajectory slopes are derived from overlapping serial ECG observations.

2.16. External evaluation in MIMIC-IV

An independent HCM cohort was derived from MIMIC-IV and processed using the same median-beat representation in the primary SHaRe analysis. 3.1. Study population and analysis framework

The HCM cohort was derived from the Sarcomeric Human Cardiomyopathy Registry (SHaRe) [11]. After identity harmonization and removal of redundant ECG identifiers, the dataset comprised 2,598 unique visits from 650 coherent subject (Extended Data Table 4). Of these, 649 subjects contributed an eligible baseline ECG for median-beat morphological analysis, with one baseline record excluded because it did not meet the prespecified minimum requirement of at least two usable aligned beats. Overall, 2,594 15 visits passed the longitudinal projection quality criteria; 493 subjects contributed at least two valid visits and 376 contributed at least three. Of the 649 waveform subjects in the SHaRe cohort, 623 had an exact non-ambiguous ECG-to-SHaRe mapping and constituted the clinical-characterization cohort. The strict Normal reference comprised 5,194 subjects selected from PTB-XL [27]. Static analyses used one baseline ECG per subject, whereas longitudinal analyses retained all eligible HCM visits. The analysis framework separated physiological reference modelling, within-HCM static phenotyping, longitudinal projection through frozen manifolds and direct modelling of within-subject waveform change, followed by clinical and genetic interrogation of the resulting electrical phenotypes. For external evaluation, an independent HCM cohort derived from MIMIC-IV [9] comprised 5,706 ECG records from 335 subjects, of which 5,576 satisfied the prespecified medianbeat quality criterion and were retained for projection through the frozen Normal-anchored and HCM-oriented manifolds.

3.2. A Normal-anchored ECG manifold defines physiological morphological variation

The Normal-anchored VAE was trained exclusively on Normal-reference median beats and used a 16-dimensional latent representation. All 16 dimensions were active, with an effective participation-ratio dimension of approximately 14.5. Reconstruction error was substantially larger for HCM than for held-out Normal ECGs: the median physical reconstruction mean squared error was 0.00692 mV 2 in HCM and 0.000801 mV 2 in the Normal test set. These differences are interpreted as descriptive evidence that HCM morphology is less well represented by the physiological reference manifold, not as diagnostic performance because the HCM and Normal cohorts originate from different data sources. Latent traversal and feature association analyses linked individual Normal-anchored coordinates to interpretable electrical domains, including LV-oriented depolarization forces, ventricular activation geometry, coupled depolarization-repolarization morphology and frontal repolarization orientation (Extended Data Fig. 4. HCM ECGs were not confined to a separate remote cluster; instead, their latent distribution overlapped the Normal manifold while being shifted and more dispersed towards its tails and periphery (Fig. 2). Representative 12-lead median-beat morphologies from Normal-reference and HCM subjects are shown in Extended Data Fig. 4.

3.3. The HCM-oriented manifold reorganizes largely shared electrical variation

A separate eight-dimensional VAE was trained on baseline HCM median beats to characterize within-HCM electrical heterogeneity. All eight 17 dimensions were active, with an effective participation-ratio dimension of 7.00. Physiological interpretation based on associations with ECG features measured in real HCM median beats and concordant decoder traversals identified continuous axes dominated by precordial QRS-force redistribution, precordial R/S transition balance, precordial repolarization, precordial depolarization force, global QRS magnitude, lateral/LV-oriented depolarization force, lateral/frontal QRS-force redistribution and mixed precordial repolarization-activation patterns (Extended Data Table 4)). Crossmanifold analysis showed that the HCM-oriented latent coordinates were substantially predictable from the Normal-anchored representation. Heldout ridge-regression R² values ranged from 0.63 to 0.86 across the eight HCM latent axes, indicating that the HCM-oriented manifold is not well described as an orthogonal disease-only subspace. Instead, it predominantly reparameterizes and recombines electrical modes already represented within physiological ECG variation. Nevertheless, some HCM-oriented coordinates remained associated with morphology that was relatively poorly represented by the Normal-anchored model. In particular, the precordial repolarization coordinate z HCM 02 showed the strongest association with relative off-Normal reconstruction burden (ρ = -0.560 across all baseline HCM ECGs and ρ = -0.588 in the held-out HCM test set), despite being highly predictable from the Normal latent representation (R² test = 0.834). Thus, substantial cross-manifold predictability and relative off-Normal morphology are not mutually exclusive, but characterize complementary aspects of the HCM electrical phenotype (Fig. 3).

3.4. HCM electrical phenotypes show longitudinal population-level trajectories

All valid longitudinal HCM ECGs were projected through the frozen Normal-anchored and HCM-oriented VAEs. Linear mixed-effects models with subject-specific random intercepts and slopes were fitted to 493 subjects with at least two valid ECGs, comprising 2,437 visits. Thirteen of 28 prespecified primary longitudinal outcomes were significant after falsediscovery-rate correction. Within the HCM-oriented space, three coordinates showed significant population-average longitudinal change. The precordial R/S transition-balance coordinate z HCM 01 decreased by 0.0329 latent units per year (FDR q = 7.57 × 10 -5 ), the lateral/LV-oriented depolarization-force coordinate z HCM 05 decreased by 0.0272 units per year (q = 9.16 × 10 -4 ), and the lateral/frontal QRS-force coordinate z HCM 06 showed the strongest change, decreasing by 0.0531 units per year (q = 4.81 × 10 -12 ). In contrast, the repolarization coordinate z HCM 02 showed no detectable linear population trend. Distance from the Normal manifold (d N ) increased by 0.0361 units per year (q = 0.00117), whereas distance within the HCM distribution remained broadly stable. Secondary residual analyses localized much of the longitu-19 dinal change to QRS/depolarization morphology rather than ST-T residual structure. A sensitivity analysis restricted to subjects with at least three visits preserved the direction of 26 of 28 primary slopes (Fig. 4). We next examined whether the principal longitudinal trajectories were sensitive to major clinical transitions capable of modifying ECG morphology. Of the 493 subjects in the primary longitudinal cohort, 475 had an exact ECG-to-SHaRe mapping, contributing 2,369 valid ECG visits; restricting the analysis to these subjects yielded estimates closely similar to the primary analysis. Censoring ECGs at septal reduction therapy (SRT) attenuated the annual slopes of z HCM 01 , z HCM 05 and d N , whereas z HCM 06 remained strongly associated with time (β = -0.0332 year -1 , 95% CI -0.0464 to -0.0200, q = 3. 3 4 5 6). In the most conservative analysis, censoring at the earliest occurrence of SRT, atrial fibrillation or pacemaker implantation, z HCM 06 remained significantly time-dependent (β = -0.0311 year -1 , 95% CI -0.0453 to -0.0168, q = 7.7 × 10 -5 ), whereas the other three selected trajectories were attenuated and no longer significant after multiple-testing correction (Table 1). Longitudinal change along z HCM 06 was particularly robust to censoring of SRT, atrial fibrillation and pacing, whereas changes in z HCM 01 , z HCM 05 and d N were partly attenuated. These population-level trajectories do not imply deterministic disease progression in individual patients.

3.6. An HCM-only longitudinal change manifold identifies four interpretable

within-subject modes To model within-subject change directly, we trained a four-dimensional conditional Delta-VAE based on the same VAE framework, applied to signed differences between consecutive HCM ECGs. The input was the signed, non-rate-normalized median-beat difference ∆X = X(t 2 ) -X(t 1 ), while the elapsed inter-visit interval ∆t entered as an explicit conditioning variable through standardized log(1 + ∆t). Subject-wise train/validation/test splitting prevented leakage between pairs from the same individual. The model used 1,916 consecutive pairs from 491 subjects; all four latent dimensions were active, with an effective latent dimension of 3.93. On held-out test pairs, 23 median reconstruction MSE was 0.00808 mV 2 and median relative MSE was 0.728. Physiological interpretation combined real feature changes between the two observed ECGs with decoder traversals reconstructed on top of real HCM reference beats. Four recurrent change modes emerged (Extended Data Table 4). D z00 represented precordial/lateral repolarization change and was most strongly associated with change in V4 T-wave amplitude (ρ = -0.510, global FDR q = 1.81 × 10 -17 ; traversal R² = 0.981). D z01 represented coupled precordial depolarization-ST/T change and was anchored by V3 net QRS-area change (ρ = -0.694, q = 3.27 × 10 -38 ; traversal R² = 0.894). D z02 represented lateral/LV-oriented depolarization-force change and was associated with V5 net QRS-area change (ρ = 0.381, q = 1.39 × 10 -9 ; traversal R² = 0.991). D z03 represented precordial activation redistribution/R-S balance change and was anchored by V3 net QRS-area change (ρ = -0.529, q = 4.95 × 10 -19 ; traversal R² = 0.872). Their selected physiological anchor associations showed consistent direction across training, validation and test splits and were concordant with decoder traversals (Fig. 5).

Because multiple consecutive pairs can arise from one subject, the four selected anchor associations were additionally assessed using subject-cluster bootstrap, subject-level median associations with permutation testing, and repeated one-pair-per-subject resampling. All four retained 95% clusterbootstrap confidence intervals excluding zero, FDR-adjusted subject-level permutation q < 0.05, and 100% one-pair-per-subject sign consistency. These analyses are cluster-aware robustness analyses of selected physiological anchors rather than independent external validation. As a sensitivity analysis, a separately trained interval-normalized rate VAE recovered related depolarization and repolarization change domains despite the expected permutation, inversion or rotation of independently learned latent axes. Reconstruction of held-out waveform change was less efficient than with the primary timeconditioned Delta-VAE (median relative MSE 0.857 versus 0.728), supporting the use of the signed, non-rate-normalized waveform difference ∆X with explicit ∆t conditioning as the primary longitudinal formulation.

3.7. Change modes align with patient-specific longer-term manifold trajectories

We next integrated the pairwise Delta-VAE representation with patientspecific slopes from the frozen-manifold mixed-effects analysis. For each subject, the signed median of each Delta-VAE latent coordinate across consecutive pairs was compared with patient-specific longitudinal slopes estimated as best linear unbiased predictors (BLUPs) for z HCM 01 , z HCM 05 , z HCM 06 and d N . Six of 16 tested associations survived FDR correction across 491 overlapping subjects. The strongest association linked the repolarization-change mode D z00 to the z HCM 05 trajectory (ρ = 0.330, FDR q = 9.68 × 10 -13 ). The lateral depolarization-force mode D z02 was also aligned with z HCM 05 (ρ = 0.235, FDR 25 q = 1.13 × 10 -6 ). The coupled precordial depolarization-ST/T mode D z01 was negatively associated with the z HCM 06 trajectory, whereas the activationredistribution mode D z03 was associated with both the z HCM 06 trajectory and increasing distance from the Normal manifold (d N : ρ = 0.162, FDR q = 0.00166). All six primary associations preserved their direction in the modelindependent OLS sensitivity analysis among 376 subjects with at least three visits, in which six of 16 associations were again FDR significant.

The held-out Delta-VAE test subset contained 73 subjects and did not yield FDR-significant associations after correction across 16 tests, although the dominant effects preserved similar directions and magnitudes. For example, the D z00 -z HCM 05 association was ρ = 0.324 in held-out subjects. We therefore interpret this analysis as evidence of alignment between complementary longitudinal representations, not as statistically independent validation because both summaries derive from overlapping longitudinal ECG information (Fig. 6).

3.9. External evaluation in MIMIC-IV replicates HCM-manifold transport

and longitudinal z HCM 06 dynamics We next evaluated the frozen SHaRE representations in the independent HCM cohort derived from MIMIC-IV (Extended Data Table 4). 4. Discussion and conclusions

We developed an ECG-first latent-manifold framework that separates deviation from physiological ECG morphology, heterogeneity within established HCM and within-subject electrical change over time. HCM ECGs overlapped the physiological reference manifold while extending towards its periphery (see Fig. 2), whereas the independently learned HCM-oriented manifold organized this heterogeneity along interpretable morphological axes that largely recombined physiological modes (see Fig. 3; Extended Data Table 4). Serial ECGs revealed distinct population-level trajectories, with z HCM 06 showing the strongest and most robust longitudinal change, including after censoring major clinical transitions (see Fig. 4; Table 1). Lower z HCM 06 was also associated with subsequent heart-failure risk (Table 2), while a complementary Delta-VAE identified recurrent modes of within-subject waveform change that aligned with longer-term manifold trajectories (see Figs. 5 and 6; Extended Data Table 4). Finally, the HCM-oriented representation and the negative longitudinal z HCM 06 trajectory were reproduced in an independent MIMIC-IV cohort (see Extended Data Table 4). Because all latent representations were learned without supervision from genotype, clinical severity or outcomes, these findings support their interpretation as electrical phenotypes rather than proxies explicitly optimized for external clinical targets.

Previous computational work has already established that HCM contains heterogeneous electrical phenotypes. Computational QRS and T-wave biomarkers combined with unsupervised clustering have been used to identify discrete HCM ECG subgroups and to relate these groups to patterns of hypertrophy and arrhythmic-risk markers [16]. More recently, supervised deep-learning approaches have shown that 12-lead ECG morphology can also distinguish predefined anatomical HCM phenotypes, including septal and apical hypertrophic patterns [28]. Our findings extend these approaches in two directions. First, rather than optimizing ECG representations for predefined anatomical classes or partitioning patients into discrete phenotypic groups, the HCM-oriented manifold represents variation continuously along interpretable morphological axes learned directly from the multilead waveform. Second, this continuous representation provides a natural coordinate system for longitudinal analysis, allowing serial ECGs from the same patient to be represented as trajectories through electrical phenotype space rather than as transitions between predefined classes or clusters.

The cross-manifold analysis further places HCM electrical heterogeneity 29 in relation to physiological variation. Latent representations are increasingly being used as quantitative biomedical phenotypes [13]. In ECG analysis, latent-space decoding has been used to interpret features associated with predefined high-risk HCM phenotypes [3], while VAE-derived embeddings have been incorporated into supervised models for left ventricular hypertrophy classification [10]. Our framework instead treats latent representations as unsupervised electrical phenotype spaces and directly compares an HCMoriented geometry with a separately learned physiological reference. The high cross-manifold predictability therefore suggests that much of HCM electrical heterogeneity reflects reorganization of physiological modes rather than a wholly distinct disease-specific latent space. Previous AI-ECG studies have shown that supervised HCM scores can track treatment response and longitudinal changes in clinically defined disease features. HCM probability scores decreased during mavacamten treatment and correlated with haemodynamic and biomarker changes [25], while probabilities trained to detect high-risk echocardiographic features followed changes in structural-risk status over time [19]. Our framework instead represents longitudinal electrical evolution without defining the expected clinical target in advance: frozen-manifold trajectories describe longer-term movement through phenotype space, whereas the Delta-VAE identifies recurrent modes of waveform change between consecutive visits. This distinction is particularly evident in the longitudinal HCM-oriented coordinates. The three population-level trajectories were not interchangeable, and the eventcensoring analyses indicate that they have different sensitivities to clinically important transitions. Longitudinal change along the lateral/frontal QRSforce coordinate z HCM 06 remained significant after censoring septal reduction therapy, atrial fibrillation and pacemaker implantation, including when these transitions were considered jointly. By contrast, changes in z HCM 01 , z HCM 05 and particularly distance from the Normal manifold were attenuated after censoring septal reduction therapy. This observation is biologically plausible because septal reduction interventions themselves can substantially modify ventricular activation: both myectomy and alcohol septal ablation are known to produce major QRS and conduction changes, including frequent new bundle-branch block [23]. Event censoring cannot distinguish intervention effects from the disease state that led to intervention and also changes the amount and composition of follow-up. These analyses should therefore not be interpreted causally. Instead, they indicate that different electrical coordinates capture distinct components of longitudinal evolution, arguing 30 against reduction of serial ECG change to a single global progression score.

Prognostic information from the conventional ECG in HCM has been recognized previously, with specific QRS and repolarization abnormalities associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes [2]. Our findings extend this concept to a continuous multilead electrical phenotype learned without outcome supervision and identified initially through its longitudinal behaviour. Lower z HCM 06 was associated with subsequent heart-failure risk both at baseline and as a time-updated exposure, suggesting that clinically relevant information can emerge from the unsupervised organization of ECG morphology rather than from predefined ECG abnormalities alone. Thus, the lateral/frontal QRS-force phenotype represented by z HCM 06 is not only longitudinally robust but also carries information about subsequent heartfailure risk. Importantly, the consistency between baseline and time-updated analyses indicates that serially available z HCM 06 values retain risk information, but does not establish that within-patient decline in z HCM 06 itself causes or independently predicts increasing risk. The association with ventriculararrhythmic events was directionally consistent but should remain exploratory because of the limited number of events. The available data did not establish residual post-treatment risk. Landmark analyses after septal reduction therapy contained too few subsequent events for reliable inference, despite substantial peri-procedural variation in z HCM 06 . Accordingly, the present results support z HCM 06 as a risk-associated electrical phenotype, particularly for heart failure, rather than as a validated post-treatment risk marker. Larger cohorts with denser post-intervention ECG follow-up will be required to determine whether residual or evolving z HCM 06 provides prognostic information after septal reduction therapy.

The Delta-VAE provides a complementary description of this evolution at shorter temporal scale. Its four modes captured recurrent changes in repolarization, coupled depolarization-ST/T morphology, lateral depolarization forces and precordial activation redistribution. Physiological associations derived from real ECG pairs were concordant with decoder traversals and remained supported when repeated observations within subjects were explicitly accounted for. Related depolarization and repolarization domains were also recovered by an independently initialized interval-normalized rate VAE, despite the expected permutation, inversion or rotation of independently learned latent axes. The less efficient reconstruction of the rate-normalized model supports the primary formulation in which the signed waveform difference ∆X is represented directly while elapsed time is supplied separately as a 31 conditioning variable. Together with the alignment between Delta-VAE summaries and patient-specific manifold slopes, these results suggest that local waveform change and longer-term electrical drift are different temporal views of an underlying evolving phenotype rather than unrelated representations.

The genetic findings should also be interpreted in the context of established HCM biology. Electrical abnormalities can precede overt hypertrophy in sarcomere-mutation carriers [15], and ECG-based deep-learning models have shown that sarcomeric genotype can be partially inferred from electrical morphology [4]. Large SHaRe analyses have further shown that pathogenic sarcomeric variants are associated with earlier disease expression and substantially greater lifetime clinical burden [11]. Against this background, our finding that sarcomeric genotype was associated with selected baseline electrical coordinates but did not significantly modify the average longitudinal slopes is not evidence that genotype is unrelated to disease progression. Rather, it suggests that genotype-associated differences in electrical phenotype position and the subsequent rate of movement through the ECG manifold are separable quantities. Genetic substrate may influence when and where an electrical phenotype becomes expressed without necessarily producing a detectable uniform difference in annual ECG trajectory over the follow-up interval and sample size available here.

External validation provides a complementary form of evidence. Supervised AI-ECG models for HCM detection have shown substantial generalizability across independent international cohorts [26], including external validation in MIMIC-IV [24]. Here, external evaluation addressed a different question: whether a frozen unsupervised HCM phenotype representation remains applicable across cohorts and whether its longitudinal behaviour is reproducible. The preserved manifold coverage and independent replication of the negative z HCM 06 trajectory in MIMIC-IV support transportability of both phenotype position and temporal evolution.

This study has limitations. The Normal and HCM cohorts originated from different source datasets, so between-source reconstruction differences and descriptive separation metrics cannot be interpreted as diagnostic performance. External evaluation in MIMIC-IV supported transport of the frozen HCM-oriented representation and independently replicated the longitudinal z HCM 06 trajectory, but did not extend to all components of the framework. In particular, the clinical-risk association of z HCM 06 , genotype relationships and the Delta-VAE change modes were not externally validated. The Delta-VAE represents unsupervised waveform change rather than a clinically validated 32 progression score. Patient-specific BLUP slopes and Delta-VAE summaries are derived from overlapping serial ECG observations; their association therefore demonstrates alignment between complementary representations rather than statistically independent validation. The clinical-risk analyses of z HCM were secondary and were adjusted for age and sex but not for the full set of established structural, haemodynamic and clinical risk markers; they therefore demonstrate risk association rather than independent or incremental predictive value. Similarly, the time-updated analyses show that serially available z HCM 06 values retain information about subsequent risk, but do not establish that within-patient decline in z HCM 06 itself causes or independently predicts increasing risk. Ventricular-arrhythmic analyses were limited by the small number of events, and post-SRT landmark analyses were underpowered. Event-censoring analyses reduce available follow-up and alter cohort composition and cannot distinguish effects of clinical interventions from the disease states that prompted those interventions. Finally, the median-beat representation intentionally emphasizes morphology and does not represent rhythm or beat-to-beat variability as primary latent information.

Together, these results position the serial ECG as more than a binary marker of HCM presence. Established electrical abnormalities can be organized within a continuous geometry referenced to physiological variation, and individual patients can be followed as they move through this electrical phenotype space. Within this representation, z HCM 06 emerged as a longitudinally robust electrical phenotype whose lower values were associated with subsequent heart-failure risk, providing an initial link between unsupervised electrical evolution and clinical outcome. By separating physiological reference position, within-HCM phenotype organization, within-subject change and subsequent clinical association, the framework provides a state-and-change representation that complements supervised AI-ECG detection and monitoring approaches. Future work should determine the structural substrates of these electrical trajectories and whether they provide incremental prognostic information beyond established imaging, haemodynamic, genetic and clinical risk markers.
