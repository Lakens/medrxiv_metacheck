Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

p > 0.05 Differences by income were not statistically significant in both unadjusted or adjusted models (p > 0.05).

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. The COVID-19 pandemic substantially altered how people live and work, highlighting the need for up-to-date and sub-national estimates of social contact patterns to inform disease modeling and public health planning.

conducted an opt-in social contact survey of upper Midwest residents, with about 60% of participants drawn from Minnesota. Participants reported all interpersonal contacts during the previous day, including the age of their contacts allowing for the analysis of mixing patterns. Detailed demographic information about the participants was also collected, allowing for additional subgroup analyses of contact patterns.

Results: Participants in our sample had an average of 6.8 contacts per day (IQR: 2.0 -8.0), which is within the range of recent post-pandemic US national and European estimates. 1 Background

Understanding how people interact and come into contact with each other is vital to understanding infectious disease epidemiology. Erroneous assumptions about contact rates can lead to inaccuracies in mathematical model predictions, resulting in sub-optimal policies and interventions [1] [2] [3]. This was highlighted during the height of the COVID-19 pandemic, in which researchers and public health professionals had to advise on an effective response to the spread of a newly emergent, highly infectious, respiratory illness, but without localized information about population contact patterns or the extent to which policies like business closures and stay-at-home orders would modify those patterns [4].

Social contact patterns often vary by local context due to differences in sociodemographic features, such as age structure, racial and ethnic composition, household structure, work-force composition, as well as local policies and climate [2, 5, 6]. As a result, sub-national social contact surveys have proven to be essential to accurately measure local contact patterns, enabling more precise modeling of local infectious disease transmission dynamics and evaluation of control measures [6].

There is growing evidence that the COVID-19 pandemic has had long-lasting impacts on everyday life, including how people interact. The pandemic catalyzed sustained increases in work-from-home arrangements and hybrid work models have become the norm for many organizations [7]. Changes in where and how people work can have substantial impacts on social contact patterns, as workplace contacts historically have accounted for a large share of individuals' total contacts [2, 8]. For instance, post pandemic contacts in the United Kingdom were 20% lower than pre-pandemic estimates [9]; and social contact changes in Europe were associated with a 20 to 50 % decline in R0 for a respiratory infectious disease compared to pre-pandemic period [8].

The Midwest Analytics and Disease Modeling Center's Social Contact Survey (MADMC SCS) was developed to measure contemporary, post-pandemic contact patterns in the upper Midwest, addressing both the lack of sub-national contact data in the United States and the need for updated contact data to reflect post-pandemic behaviors. The survey collects information about contacts in household, work, school, and community settings, and combines detailed and aggregate reporting to better capture contact patterns of participants with both small and large numbers of contacts. We use these data to describe sociodemographic variation in daily number of contacts and to generate setting-specific, age-structured contact matrices. We also assess to what extent Upper Midwest contact patterns differ from national contact data and implication for infectious disease transmission.

2 Methods

2.1 Study population and sampling

The survey was administered online to an opt-in sample recruited by Ipsos, a well-established market research firm with extensive experience conducting opt-in health and behavioral surveys [10] [11] [12] [13]. The study's target population consisted of non-institutionalized individuals of all ages residing in Minnesota and neighboring states (North and South Dakota, Iowa, and Wisconsin). The survey was fielded online, in English, between September 14 and October 12, 2025. Survey respondents who provided a zip code located outside of these states were excluded from the study. Respondents were also excluded if they took longer than 48 hours to complete the survey or longer than 24 hours and crossing from weekday to weekend or vice versa. Participants were only allowed to complete the survey once.

Post-stratification weights were provided by the survey vendor to account for study design and to ensure sample representativeness of the upper Midwest region. Weights were generated to adjust survey responses to match upper Midwest region-specific population distribution by sex, age, race, state of residence, metropolitan status (as defined by the ACS/Office of Budget Management), education level, household income, and employment status, using an iterative proportional fitting (raking) procedure. Weights also adjusted for days of the week to weight each day of the week equally. Population targets were obtained from the 2023 American Community Survey [14]. To reduce the variance of the sample and the influence of outliers, weights were also trimmed and scaled to sum to the unweighted sample size of all qualified respondents.

2.2 Ethics statement

Participation in the MADMC SCS was voluntary. Consent was obtained from the participant or from the parent or legal guardian of participants under the age of 18 years. The consenting adult completed the survey on behalf of children 12 years and under, while children aged 13-17 years provided their assent and completed the contact modules of the survey independently after parent/legal guardian consent. The study was determined to be exempt from human subjects review by the University of Minnesota Institutional Review Board [ID: STUDY0024205].

2.3 Survey Design

The survey instrument can be found in Appendix A. We first asked participants about themselves and their household, including questions about age, sex, race/ethnicity, employment, level of education, household income, household size, marital status, and state of residence. Metropolitan status was determined from participant-reported residential zip code.

Participants were then asked about contacts they had on the previous day. A contact was defined as a person with whom the participant had an in-person conversation of at least five words or any physical contact (such as a handshake, hug, kiss, or contact sports). This definition was used to align with other social contact studies [15]. Contact information was obtained separately for four specific settings: household, work, school, and community.

Participants were first asked about their household contacts. For each household contact, detailed information was collected, including but not limited to the contact's age, sex, and race.

For work, school, and community contacts, participants were first asked to report the total number of contacts in each setting and then asked to list (using pseudonyms or nicknames) up to a maximum of 40 contacts in each setting. If a participant reported fewer than 10 contacts in a given setting, they were asked to provide detailed information for all of their listed contacts using questions similar to those for household members. For those reporting more than 10 contacts, detailed information was collected for a random sample of 10 of their listed contacts. Detailed information was limited to 10 contacts per setting to reduce survey fatigue.

To ensure contact characteristics were still captured for individuals with large numbers of contacts, aggregate information was also collected by asking participants how their remaining contacts were distributed across age groups, allowing for potential imputation of other demographic characteristics. Community contacts also included a question about how the participant's remaining contacts were distributed across different locations. Note that setting definitions were based on the participant's relationship to that setting; a participant who worked in a school would report contacts with students and colleagues as work.

2.4 Statistical analyses

We first conducted descriptive analyses of the total number of contacts reported by participants (across all settings). Participant responses were top-coded (an upward bound to the total number of contacts) at 40 total contacts, similar to other social contact surveys [2, 16, 17], to reduce the influence of outliers on analysis results. We examined the distribution of the total number of contacts to assess whether there was evidence of heaping at multiples of 5 or 10, which could indicate participants were loosely estimating rather than re-calling exact number of contacts. We evaluated how the number of reported contacts varied by participant characteristics, such as sex, age, household size, school attendance (in-person, online, no school), work type (in-person vs. not in-person), household income, residence in a metro area, and day of the week contacts were reported for (weekend vs. weekday). We used negative binomial regression models to estimate contact rate ratios (RRs) associated with these factors, in both univariate (unadjusted) and multivariate (adjusted) analyses to identify statistically significant differences [13, 16]. All analyses were conducted using sample weights. In sensitivity analysis, we ran regression models without top-coding contacts, and we also evaluated the performance of Zero Inflated Poisson and Zero Inflated Negative Binomial regression models in fitting contact data.

2.4.1 Mixing patterns

Mixing matrices are used to describe how different groups interact with one another and the extent to which contacts are concentrated within groups (homophily or assortativity) versus across groups. We constructed an overall age-structured mixing matrix, as well as separate, setting-specific age-structured matrices for household, work, school, and community. In the contact data, the maximum number of contacts a participant could have was capped at 40 by location.

2.4.2 Comparing Mixing Patterns and Implications for Disease Transmission

We compared the MADMC SCS age-structured contact matrices with the recently collected and nationally representative Epistorm-Mix study [13]. 3 Results

3.1 Survey sample characteristics

The MADMC SCS analytic sample consisted of 1,009 individuals reporting 5,806 unique contacts (when the maximum number of contacts was top-coded at 40). Sixty percent (n = 612) of the participants resided in Minnesota and the remaining 40% resided in surrounding states.

Slightly more than half (53%) of our sample was female, and the majority were from metro areas (78%). Our sample was primarily White (90%); therefore, we did not stratify results by race and ethnicity. All days of the week were represented in the survey; before applying weights, approximately 23% of participants reported contacts that occurred on a Saturday or Sunday.

3.2 Number of contacts

The distribution of reported total contacts (across all settings) was right-skewed (Figure 1) and did not show evidence of heaping at multiples of 5 and 10. In the sample, the mean number of contacts was 6.84 and the median was 4.00 (IQR: 2.0-8.0). Without top-coding total contacts at 40, the mean shifted to 8.68 while the median remained at 4.00 (IQR: 2.0-8.0).

3.3 Correlates of contacts

Table 1 summarizes the number of reported contacts across sociodemographic groups, including the mean, median, and interquartile range. Table 2 presents unadjusted and adjusted rate ratios from negative binomial regression models with reference categories as indicated in the table.

Men reported more contacts than women (RR = 1.28) in the unadjusted results, with a mean of 7.7 (IQR: 2.0-9.0) contacts per day compared to 6.0 (IQR: 1.0-7.0) among women.

This difference was marginally statistically significant in the unadjusted model. The men in the sample were also more likely to work in person than the women, suggesting that workrelated exposure may partly explain the observed difference. After adjusting for covariates, men had a lower estimated contact rate than women (RR = 0.91), but this difference was not statistically significant.

The average number of contacts reported per day varied substantially by age. Contacts were highest among adolescents and young adults, peaking at a mean of 16. For participants aged 5 -19, most reported contacts took place at school. After adjustments for covariates, including school attendance and in-person work, age group differences were attenuated. Participants aged 15-19 remained the most connected (RR = 1.16), followed by those aged 25-30 (Table 2). In contrast, most other age groups converged to a similar mean number of contacts per day, and the pronounced decline in contact rates at older ages was no longer observed.

Reported contacts increased with household size. Participants living alone averaged 4.4 contacts per day, while those in two-or three-person households averaged 5.3 to 5.8 contacts per day. Contact rates were higher in larger households, rising to 8.6 contacts per day among participants in four-person households and 13.8 among those in households with six or more members. Relative to participants living alone, the difference was statistically significant only for households with more than four members. After controlling for covariates, only those in households with six or more people had statistically significantly more contacts than those living alone (RR = 1.92).

Participants in households with annual incomes above $100,000 reported more contacts (mean = 7.8-9.3) than those in lower-income households, particularly those earning less than $25,000 (mean = 5.1; IQR: 0.0-5.0) or between $50,000 and $75,000 (mean = 4.2; IQR: 1.0-5.0). Contact rates did not increase monotonically with household income. Differences by income were not statistically significant in both unadjusted or adjusted models (p > 0.05). Contact rates were somewhat lower among participants in non-metro areas than among those in metro areas. Non-metro participants reported an average of 6.2 contacts per day (IQR: 2.0-9.0), compared with 7.1 contacts per day among metro participants (IQR: 2.0-8.8). This difference was not statistically significant in the unadjusted model (RR = 0.87), and controlling for covariates did not materially change the results (RR = 0.94). Because metro participants were more likely to report more than 40 contacts, the metro-non-metro difference appeared larger when contact counts were analyzed without top-coding (see Supple-mental

Materials Appendix B).

Reported contacts were similar on weekdays and weekends. Participants reported an average of 6.9 contacts per day on weekdays (IQR: 2.0-9.0) and 6.8 contacts per day on weekends (IQR: 2.0-6.0). When contact counts were analyzed without top-coding, weekend contacts appeared much higher than weekday contacts, however, this difference was no longer statistically significant after adjusting for covariates.

3.4 Age-Structured Mixing Patterns

Figure 2 presents the overall age-structured contact matrix (10-year age groups), along with setting-specific contact matrices and the corresponding age-assortativity (Q-index) for each.

We also calculated the share of age-specific contacts in each cell reported to be with household members (Figure 2F).

Across most age groups, the greatest amount of mixing took place between participants and similarly aged contacts. The exception was middle aged participants (40-49) being slightly more likely to interact with children aged 10-19. Children and young adults exhibited the highest levels of age-assortative mixing, averaging 4 to 7 same age contacts per day.

Among participants younger than 19, these contacts occurred primarily in school (Figure 2D) and in community settings (Figure 2E). In contrast, among young adults, most assortative contacts took place at work (Figure 2C). Participants aged 50 and above had about one contact per day within their age group.

In the home setting, children and adolescents primarily mixed with siblings, while adults mixed with their similarly aged partners or other household members. However, the highest number of home contacts reflected inter-generational mixing, particularly between parents aged 30-49 and children aged 0-19. The lower diagonal represents parents interacting with children, while the upper diagonal represents children interacting with their parents (Figure 2B).

Most workplace contacts occurred among adults aged 20-69, which is consistent with the working age population. The levels of workplace mixing occurred within the 20-29 and 30-39 age groups and between these two adjacent age groups (Figure 2C). The overall workplace Q-index is low (0.09) suggesting homogeneous mixing; we also see evidence of homogeneous mixing when we exclude children and elderly from the analysis (Q-index = 0.14). In contrast, although the overall Q-index for school is also very low (Q-index = 0.09), restricting the analysis to children under age of 19 results in a very high level of homophily (Q-index = 0.83) (Figure 2D). At school, children averaged 4-5 contacts per day with other children.

Community contacts also exhibited a strong age assortative profile (Figure 2E). It is in community settings that the elderly mixed with other age groups the most, especially with adjacent age groups. Children under 10 had very few community contacts with other age groups.

3.5 Comparison with Epistorm-Mix Matrices

The Epistorm-Mix data we use had 980 participants with 7,492 contacts. The maximum number of contacts contributed by a single participant was similar across the two datasets (MADMC SCS: 77; Epistorm-Mix: 67), suggesting that extreme values were unlikely to substantially influence the age-structured contact matrices. In Epistorm-Mix, 28 participants reported 40 or more contacts, including 9 who reported more than 49 con-tacts.

In comparison, MADMC SCS included 16 participants with 40 or more contacts, of whom only 3 reported more than 49 contacts. We found a strong statistical correspondence between the national Epistorm-Mix matrix and the MADMC SCS contact matrix (Pearson r= 0.92; Spearman ρ= 0.90; R² = 0.84). The rank relationship was even stronger when focusing exclusively on the diagonal elements which capture age-assortative mixing (Pearson r= 0.92; Spearman ρ= 0.97). Despite the overall similarity, the Epistorm-Mix matrix generally exhibited higher contact rates than the MADMC SCS matrix across most age-group pairings.

The primary exception was among younger age groups (<30 years), where MADMC SCS participants reported substantially higher rates of contact within their own age group, reflected in the stronger age-assortative mixing along the matrix diagonal (Figure 3).

The leading eigenvalue of the MADMC SCS contact matrix was 8.84, compared with 8.28 for the Epistorm matrix. The resulting relative R0 (MADMC/Epistorm) was 1.067, indicating that the MADMC SCS contact matrix yielded an R0 6.7% higher than that obtained using the Epistorm contact matrix, holding all other transmission parameters constant.

4 Discussion and Conclusion

In the fall of 2025, the MADMC Social Contact Survey recorded an average of 6.8 contacts per day (IQR: 2.0-8.0) among participants in Minnesota and surrounding upper Midwest states. When we remove the upward bound on number of contacts, the reported mean was 8.7 contacts per day (IQR: 2.0-8.0). Both estimates fall within the range of recent post-pandemic European estimates of mean daily contacts, including 6.0 contacts per day in Switzerland, 6.5 contacts per day in the United Kingdom, 6.7 contacts per day in Belgium, and 9.9 in the Netherlands [8]. Our top-coded MADMC SCS estimates were lower than the national US-focused Epistorm-Mix study collected in fall 2024, which estimated 7.4 contacts per day (IQR: 2.0-9.0) nationally and 8.0 contacts per day (IQR: 3.0-10.0) in their Midwestern regional subsample [13]. However, after removing the top-coding our estimate were slightly higher.

Despite lower total daily contacts than contemporary US national estimates, the Midwest sub-national mixing patterns by age exhibited greater within-age-group transmission potential among children, adolescents, and young adults. These highly connected age groups can form a transmission "core" that can disproportionately influence epidemic dynamics.

Correspondingly, this difference in age-based mixing resulted in an estimated 6.7% higher R0 than that implied by national contact patterns.

Although modest in relative terms, a difference of this magnitude may still be epidemiologically important when transmission is near the epidemic threshold, where relatively small changes in R0 can substantially alter epi-demic growth rates. Furthermore, the 6.7% relative difference in R0 is not fixed and could increase or decrease based on interaction between mixing patterns and specific infectious pathogens age pattern of susceptibility and infectiousness. The observed differences may reflect underlying geographic variation as well as differences in the study period and sampling methodology, particularly the contrast between the probability-based Epistorm-Mix sample and the MADMC SCS opt-in sample. There were also differences in how MADMC SCS and Epistorm-Mix treated participants with large number of contacts. As additional national and sub-national data become available, it will be important to assess whether these patterns persist.

Although comparable pre-pandemic estimates of social contact patterns are not avail-able for the Upper Midwest or the United States nationally, MADMC contact rates were approximately half of those reported in historical European studies [2]. This difference is consistent with broader evidence that the pandemic produced persistent changes in daily routines, including increased work-from-home arrangements.

School and work settings were the primary drivers of social contact patterns. Consistent with prior research, participants attending school or working in person reported substantially more daily contacts than those who did not, and age groups most engaged in these activities (15-19 and 24-29 years) had the highest contact rates [13]. Although individuals in online school also reported high contact rates, most were employed adults whose contacts occurred predominantly in workplaces (Appendix D1). These findings suggest that continued changes in work arrangements, particularly shifts toward or away from in-person work, could meaningfully affect population-level contact rates and transmission potential. Higher household income was also associated with greater contact rates, a pattern largely explained by more school-related contacts, including daycare, camp, and after-school care, further underscoring the central role of schools in shaping social mixing (Appendix D2). At the same time, substantial intergenerational mixing in community settings, particularly between older adults and younger age groups, suggests that community-based interventions may be important for reducing exposure among susceptible older adults.

Additional research is needed to investigate the potential impact of sampling approaches and survey instrument design. The MADMC social contact survey used an opt-in sample, providing a source of potential bias in the study population. The post-stratification weights ensured that our sample aligned with key demographic characteristics of the population, but some unobserved differences may still remain. We were reassured that our findings were consistent with known mixing patterns and that our results were largely consistent with data from Epistorm-Mix, the nationally representative survey that utilized probability sampling.

A second limitation is the reliance on self-reported contact data, which can be subject to reporting bias. Standard contact survey instruments can be lengthy and burdensome to complete, particularly for individuals with many contacts, and survey fatigue may lead some respondents to under report their true number of interactions. To assess one potential source of reporting bias, we examined the distribution of reported contacts across all contact modules (home, school, work, and other) for evidence of "heaping" at multiples of 5 and 10. The resulting histograms showed little evidence of systematic rounding, suggesting that this source of reporting bias was limited (Appendix E). Nevertheless, further work is needed to identify methods that improve contact ascertainment and reduce under reporting, particularly among highly connected individuals. Such advances would support more accurate estimates of contact patterns and strengthen inferences about their implications for disease transmission.

In conclusion, contact patterns from the Upper Midwest were similar to national estimates. However, there were key differences such as higher rates of assortative mixing among participants younger than 30 years of age in the Upper Midwest. These data are important for better understanding post-pandemic social contact behaviors and potential regional differences that exist.
