How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 51 papers with 87 statements, this module correctly identified 38 statements (true positives) and incorrectly flagged 22 statements (false positives). It failed to detect 27 statements. Thus, among all statements flagged by the module, 63% were genuine cases (positive predictive value). However, the module missed 42% of all true statements (false negative rate).

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. Abstract

During infectious disease outbreaks, characteristics of reported cases are routinely collected. These give information on becoming infected but not on infecting others. We assess whether linking infectees to infectors, together with their characteristics, can help understand transmission.

From the start of the COVID-19 pandemic in the Netherlands, reported cases were asked to identify their most probable infector in routine surveillance, enabling the linking of cases. We assess for the period 27 February 2020 -11 April 2022 whether the infectees of these transmission pairs are representative of all reported cases, whether the transmission pairs yield verifiable estimates of epidemiological characteristics (here the serial interval), and whether they provide additional information on transmission.

Of 8,003,008 reported cases, 678,482 (8.5%) could be linked to their most probable infector. These infectees were largely representative of the reported cases regarding age group, sex, and geographical location. The mean serial interval of 3.6 days (sd 3.4 days) from transmission pairs aligns with literature. Transmissions between age groups largely follow known contact patterns. Most transmissions in September 2021 occurred between persons who were not (fully) vaccinated, indicating the effectiveness of the vaccine, and relatively few between persons with different vaccination status, suggesting assortative mixing in vaccination status. Conclusion .

Introduction

When a completely new or new variant of an infectious disease pathogen emerges, its characteristics are initially unknown. Basic epidemiological variables, such as the incubation period or age-specific transmission risks, are needed to inform an appropriate public health response. Knowledge about the transmission dynamics can help design targeted interventions to mitigate the outbreak as effectively as possible.

Studies that determine who infected whom are invaluable to gain insight into the transmission dynamics. Detailed reports of transmission events at the start of an epidemic can provide the first estimates of epidemiological characteristics, as was shown for COVID-19 in 2019 [1] [2] [3] [4] and Mpox in 2022 [5]. Information on infectees and their infectors can also be obtained from household studies where all household members are monitored over time, from which for instance age-dependent transmission characteristics for influenza [6, 7] and COVID-19 [8] [9] [10] were obtained. Another data source from which transmission pairs can be constructed is contact tracing, where contacts of reported cases are followed over time, as was done for COVID-19 [11, 12] and pandemic influenza in 2009 [13]. Genomic sequencing can provide additional evidence as to whether viral genomes of an infector and infectee are sufficiently similar to support a transmission link without inferring its direction. These types of studies have in common that they require time-intensive and costly collection methods. As a result, they typically provide reliable data, but only for a limited number of cases and over a limited time span.

An alternative method to collect transmission pairs was introduced at the start of the COVID-19 pandemic in the Netherlands. In the routine surveillance questionnaire that was filled out for all reported cases [14], a question was included to identify the most probable infector. If the indicated infector was also a reported case in the surveillance system, the two cases and all their characteristics, such as age, location and symptom onset date, were linked as a transmission pair. The identification of the infector relied on the assessment of the infectee with the help of a public health officer and was not confirmed by genomic sequencing or other methods. Transmission links were available in large numbers over the entire period of the COVID-19 pandemic, without substantial additional costs to the routine surveillance system. These routinely collected transmission pairs were used to study the role of children in SARS-CoV-2 transmission [15], the interaction between households and schools [16], the serial intervals of the Delta and Omicron variants [17], and the transmission depending on the vaccination status of infector and infectee [18].

In this study we aim to assess whether these routinely collected transmission pairs can provide additional information on transmission processes that could complement other data sources, such as contact tracing data or household studies. For this we first established that the infectees with an identified infector were a representative subset of all reported cases. Second, we verified that the linking of cases yielded plausible estimates for epidemiological characteristics, by comparing them to literature. With these two requirements met, we investigated the frequency of transmission events within and between groups of cases stratified by age group, transmission setting and vaccination status. Identification of characteristics that are associated with onward transmission within and between groups can suggest possible interventions to reduce transmission in the population.

Methods

Data

All laboratory-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infections in the Netherlands were centrally registered in the OSIRIS database [14] using a unique identification number. These reported COVID-19 cases were coupled to their demographic characteristics, such as age, sex and postal code of the place of residence, as registered in the Personal Records Database [19]. Epidemiological characteristics, such as symptom onset date and transmission setting, were ascertained and registered by the public health officer. The public health officer would also ask the case by whom they thought they were infected. If this person could be identified and if this person was also present in the database as a previously reported case, its unique identification number was stored in the reported case file as "most probable infector". By linking the identification numbers of the reported case and its most probable infector, transmission pairs were constructed with known characteristics for both infector and infectee. These characteristics include age, sex, postal code, residential province, date of report, date of symptom onset, date of testing positive for SARS-CoV-2 (i.e. date of laboratory confirmation), vaccination status and transmission setting.

We included data in the period from 27 February 2020 when the first case was reported until 11 April 2022, after which date a positive self test did not need to be confirmed anymore by the public health service [20] and the number of reported cases dropped. We completely excluded impossible transmission chains, such as self infections (of type A→A), reciprocal infections (of type A→B→A) and triangular infection chains (of type as A→B→C→A). The dates of testing positive were generally provided by automated systems of testing laboratories, whereas the dates of symptom onset were entered manually. Therefore we deemed dates of testing positive to be more trustworthy than symptom onset dates. For this reason, transmission pairs with an unrealistic period between testing positive were excluded from the data set, while pairs with an unrealistic period between symptom onset were only excluded from the serial interval analysis. We set these unrealistic periods between testing positive and between symptom onsets to be less than -10 days or more than 30 days, which is a bit broader than the reported serial interval distributions of smaller data sets [1, 21].

The transmission setting (of the infectee) could be one or more settings from a list of options. They were assigned a single setting using the hierarchy: "Household", "School", "Work", "Visit", or "Other". An exception was made for cases infected at nursing homes or long-term care facilities, who were infected at home, but were assigned the setting "Other". The postal codes of infector and infectee were used to correct the settings in two ways. First, infectees who reported to have been infected at home by an infector with a postal code that differed from the infectee's postal code, were assigned a "Visit" setting. Second, transmission pairs with unknown setting but with identical postal codes were classified as having a "Household" setting. It should be noted here that when an infector and infectee had identical postal codes they were not necessarily household members, but a validation study showed they were in 97% of the cases [22]. When using the term "Household" setting throughout the manuscript, it should be kept in mind that some of these transmission pairs might have occurred between neighbours.

Analysis

To evaluate whether the reported cases with identified infector are representative of all reported cases, we calculated the number of infectees as a fraction of the total number of reported cases [23], stratified by 10-year age group, sex, residential province, calendar week, and whether the symptom onset date is known. Comparable fractions in the strata imply comparable representation. We also calculated the post-stratification weights. Poststratification weights above one imply underrepresentation of the transmission pairs in these strata, and vice versa.

To check whether the transmission pairs were plausible, we compared the serial interval to values reported in literature [24]. The serial interval is defined as the time between symptom onset dates of infector and infectee. We calculated the mean and standard deviation of the serial intervals from all pairs with known symptom onset dates. For a comparison to values reported in literature, we calculated the mean serial interval by period with different dominant SARS-CoV-2 variants. The symptom onset date of the infector was used to assign each transmission pair to a period where the ancestral lineage (until 14 Feb 2021), the Alpha variant ( 15 Feb 2021 -27 Jun 2021), the Delta variant (28 Jun 2021 -26 Dec 2021) or the Omicron variant (27 Dec 2021 onwards) constituted the majority of tested samples [25].

To demonstrate how the transmission pair data can provide insights in transmission, we examined the transmission pairs by age groups of infectors and infectees. We first made a distinction by transmission setting, plotting the numbers of transmission pairs by 5-year age groups (up to 95+ years of age) and setting over the full study period. Next, we stratified the transmission pairs by vaccination status of infector and infectee, in 10-year age groups (up to 70+ years of age) to align with available vaccination coverage data [26, 27]. We used data of infectees testing positive in September 2021 when the Delta variant was dominant. Before this month all persons of 12 years and older had the chance to get vaccinated, and after this month the effect of vaccine waning and the autumn booster campaign may impact the results. Persons who finished their vaccination series (i.e. receiving their second mRNA vaccine dose or their first vector vaccine dose) at least two weeks before testing positive were considered to be fully vaccinated [28, 29]; all others -including partially vaccinated persons -were classified as not (fully) vaccinated. We note that the proportion of partially vaccinated persons was relatively small in September 2021 as the vaccination campaign was nearing its end [26]. We tested the number of transmission pairs in each combination of infector and infectee age group against the hypothesis that vaccination did not affect infection nor transmission and that mixing is independent of vaccination status, using a likelihood ratio test [30]. In this test, we compared the observed number of transmission pairs to the expected number under the null hypothesis based on the vaccination coverage in the general population (see Suppl S1). As a sensitivity analysis we repeated the analysis for the longer autumn period Sep -Nov 2021.

Results

On 11 April 2022, in total 8,003,008 test-positive COVID-19 cases were reported in the OSIRIS database. For 713,222 of these cases, the most probable infector was identified. After removing 11,286 pairs with implausible intervals between dates of testing positive, 5,178 self infections, 18,204 reciprocal infections, and 72 triangular infection chains, the data consisted of 678,482 plausible transmission pairs, i.e., 8.5% of all reported cases. The transmission setting of 54,029 pairs (8% of all pairs) reported at home but between persons with a different postal codes were classified as "Visit", and 24,714 pairs (3.6% of all pairs) with a missing transmission setting between persons with an identical postal code were assigned a "Household" setting. We assessed these pairs by evaluating their representativeness, serial interval and transmission patterns.

Representativeness of transmission pairs

Stratifying the 678,482 infectees of the transmission pairs by age group, we found that all age groups are similarly represented, with post-stratification weights between 0.80 for 50-69 year olds and 1.21 for 30-39 year olds (Tab. 1). Infectees were proportionally divided over the sexes (Tab 1), also within age groups (Fig. 1A). There were only 6 infectees with a missing value for age and 18 with a missing value for sex. The distribution of cases with identified infector over the Dutch provinces reasonably corresponded to that of all reported cases (Tab 1 and Fig. 1B).

The vast majority (86.3%) of transmission pairs was reported in the household or during visits (Tab 1), which were settings where infector and infectee were well acquainted. The symptom onset date of cases with identified infector was more often known than for all reported cases (Tab 1). The identification of infectors fluctuated heavily over time (Fig. 1C). Identification percentages were low at the start of the pandemic when testing for SARS-CoV-2 was not available for the general population and at the end of the pandemic when case numbers were high during the Omicron waves in the Netherlands. The percentages of identified infectors were highest during early 2021 when the Alpha variant emerged. * The transmission settings are only given for the number of cases with identified infector, as they were corrected with the use of postal code information and cannot be compared with the transmission settings of all reported cases.

Serial interval of transmission pairs

For 80% of the transmission pairs the symptom onset date was known for both infector and infectee. For each of these pairs, we calculate the time between these symptom onset dates, i.e., the serial interval. Of all serial intervals 666 (0.1%) were was discarded because they were lower than -10 days or longer than 30 days. The serial interval distribution peaked at 3 days (Fig. 2), with a mean of 3.6 days, a median of 3 days, and a standard deviation of 3.4 days. A fraction of 5% of the serial intervals was negative, indicating presymptomatic transmission. The mean serial interval was 3.7 days (sd 3.6 days) in the period dominated by the ancestral lineage, 3.6 days (sd 3.3 days) in the Alpha-dominated period, 3.6 days (sd 3.1 days) in the Deltadominated period, and 3.4 days (sd 3.2 days) in the Omicron-dominated period.

Transmission patterns

The transmission pair data allowed us to study transmission between groups with different characteristics. As an illustration, we evaluate transmission patterns between different age groups, stratified by setting or vaccination status.

Household transmissions showed a clear pattern along the diagonal and two off-diagonal parallels when depicted as a transmission matrix by 5-year age groups of infectors and infectees in households (Fig. 3). These household transmissions occurred between partners, flatmates or siblings (diagonal), from parent to child (upper parallel), or from child to parent (lower parallel). The transmission pattern in the "Visit" setting contained mainly transmissions between 15-24 year olds, but also showed intergenerational transmissions. Compared to the "Household" setting, these were shifted to higher ages where children and parents do not generally share a household. In the "School" setting many transmissions between children were reported. School transmissions from adults to children were more common than from children to adults, but it should be noted that an adult infected at school could have reported this with a "Work" setting.

Indeed, in the "Work" setting some child to adult transmissions are reported. The "Work" setting was the only setting where transmissions occurred reasonably homogeneously between working age groups.

The size of the circles denotes the number of transmission pairs. Combinations of transmission setting, infector age group and infectee age group that contain less than 5 transmission pairs are not shown.

Stratifying the transmission pairs by vaccination status revealed a distinct transmission pattern, as illustrated here for September 2021 with in total 13,072 transmission pairs (Fig. 4). In 52 of the 64 age group combinations, the number of transmission pairs stratified by vaccination status differed significantly (p value < 0.001) from the number that would be expected if vaccination would not have any effect and if mixing were independent of vaccination status (see Tab. S1 in Supplement for values). Most transmission pairs were reported between persons who are not (fully) vaccinated, and the numbers were always larger than the expectation, as shown by the positive residual deviances (in red) in the lower left quadrant of Fig. 4. In the opposite quadrant transmissions between fully vaccinated persons were less common (in blue) than would be expected from the vaccination coverage alone.

The results in both quadrants could be explained by the effect of vaccination, but this could be modified to an extent by assortative mixing [31]. Of all transmission pairs, 76% occur between persons of identical vaccination status, which indeed suggests assortative mixing. This could partly explain why the observed number of transmission pairs is higher than the expectation among not (fully) vaccinated persons. Similarly, the lower than expected numbers in the lower right and upper left quadrants could be partly explained by persons preferring not to mix with persons of opposing vaccination status. A notable exception in the lower right quadrant is that transmissions on the lower off-diagonal occurred more often than expected. These were most likely household transmissions from not (fully) vaccinated children to fully vaccinated parents, suggestive of a "leaky" rather than "all or nothing" protection by the vaccine.

An interesting group to consider are the 0-9 year olds because they were all unvaccinated. This age group more often infects not (fully) vaccinated than fully vaccinated other age groups (bottom row of Fig. 4), indicating vaccination protects against infection. In the other direction an even larger discrepancy shows that 0-9 year olds are more often infected by not (fully) vaccinated than fully vaccinated other age groups (left column of Fig. 4). This would suggest that vaccination has an additional effect on onward transmission. Assortativity may also play a role here but would probably not be the only explanation for the observed effects. As a sensitivity analysis we repeated the analysis over a longer period including September, October and November 2021 with in total 56,206 transmission pairs. The general transmission pattern (see Fig. S1) is similar to the pattern shown in Fig. 4 with one striking difference: the number of observed transmission pairs among fully vaccinated persons in the oldest age groups is here higher than expected. This could be explained by a waning vaccine effect, as the oldest age groups were vaccinated first. Another explanation could be the assortative mixing effect, because also among the not (fully) vaccinated persons in the oldest age groups, the observations are higher than expected. A combination of both explanations is likely.

Discussion

Routine surveillance of COVID-19 cases in the Netherlands allowed identifying a large number of transmission pairs, where the reported case could be linked to its most probable infector. The infectees of the transmission pairs were largely representative of all reported cases with regard to 10-year age group, sex and residential province. Because of this representativeness and because the transmission pairs were likely real transmission events (evidenced by the plausible serial interval distribution), we could use the observed transmission pairs as a proxy for all transmissions to study transmission patterns.

The transmission pair data were not representative for transmission events to reported cases over time. The fraction of cases with identified infector was low in some periods, especially during the first wave at the start of 2020 and after the emergence of Omicron at the end of 2021. The largest number of transmission pairs was collected during the period when the ancestral lineage or the Alpha variant were dominant. The household and visit transmission settings were likely overrepresented, because in these settings infectees could most probably identify their infector. On the other hand, transmissions from and to asymptomatic cases and other underreported cases are largely absent. These limitations are however also present in contact tracing data.

As plausibility check of the transmission pair data, we found a serial interval with an overall mean of 3.6 days, composed of mean serial intervals of 3.7 days, 3.6 days, 3.6 days and 3.4 days for the ancestral lineage, Alpha, Delta and Omicron variants respectively. These values were in line with a meta analysis of reported values [24] that found a pooled mean serial interval of 4.8 days (range of reported means 1.5 -7.5 days), 3.5 days (2.4 -4.5 days), 3.6 days (2.3 -6.7 days), and 3.2 days (2.4 -4.2 days) for the ancestral lineage, Alpha, Delta and Omicron BA.1 variants respectively. At the start of the pandemic long serial intervals were reported [2, 32], but after the first pandemic peak when interventions were put in place the serial interval was found to be shorter [33] [34] [35]. Most of our transmission pairs were observed when interventions were in effect, explaining the relatively short serial interval for the ancestral lineage. We found 5% of the serial intervals to be negative. Other studies report higher negative fractions between 5.8% and 13% [1, 12, 33, 36] or no negative fractions [2, 32]. Negative serial intervals are an indication of presymptomatic transmission as was demonstrated for COVID-19 [37, 38].

As the transmission pairs were largely self-reported and unconfirmed by genomic sequencing or field epidemiological investigation, they could have been misclassified in two ways. First, the identified infector was not necessarily the true infector. For confirmation additional testing of infector and infectee is required. In a previous study [17], we studied a period of two weeks with cocirculation of the Delta and Omicron BA.1 variants, that could be distinguished by S gene target failure (SGTF). Of the 1143 transmission pairs where both infector and infectee were tested, 27 pairs (2.4%) had conflicting results. This fraction is a lower bound to the true misclassification of infectors. Second, the direction of transmission could have been misclassified, where the identified infector was in fact the infectee. This could occur when infectees have a tendency to identify an infector with an earlier symptom onset. Such a tendency would lead to a discontinuity in the serial interval distribution at a serial interval of 0 days. As we did not observe such a discontinuity in our data (Fig. 2), we conclude there is no systemic misclassification in the transmission direction.

The added value of the transmission pair data lies in the adequate representativeness and the large size of the data set, which can reveal general transmission patterns. This was illustrated by studying transmissions between different age groups: • Age-specific patterns in transmission pairs stratified by setting (Fig. 3) are similar to agespecific patterns in self-reported contacts by setting [39, 40].

The similarity provides evidence favouring the 'social contact hypothesis' that proposes conversational contacts as a proxy for events at risk of transmission [41].

• Transmission pairs stratified by vaccination status compared to the vaccination coverage (Fig. 4) reveal that not (fully) vaccinated persons were more often infected than expected, which can be explained by a combination of the effect of vaccination and assortative mixing of persons with identical vaccination status.

• Fully vaccinated persons were less likely to be infected by the completely unvaccinated youngest age group, suggesting that the vaccine was effective against infection. Once infected, fully vaccinated persons were less likely to infect the youngest age group, suggesting that the vaccine was effective against onward transmission. These two types of vaccine effectiveness have been demonstrated in household studies [42], but the transmission pair data provided evidence from other settings and in a higher resolution.

• Our data suggested that persons preferably mix with persons who had an identical vaccination status, which has also been observed [43, 44]. This assortativity introduces heterogeneity in the population structure that reduces the overall impact of vaccination [43, 45].

The effects of vaccination (on infection and onward transmission) and assortativity could be estimated simultaneously [31], although it should be reminded that the not (fully) vaccinated population consists of unvaccinated and partially vaccinated persons. This would most likely underestimate both the effects of vaccination and assortativity.

Of all reported cases 8.5% could be linked to a most probable infector. This percentage was even higher than 30% from December 2020 till June 2021 during a crucial period of the pandemic when the Alpha variant emerged and large-scale vaccination was rolled out. A reported case would lack an infector when they did not know by whom they were infected (even if they did know the time and setting), when the case was not asked by or did not reply to the public health services for follow-up questions, or when the identified infector was not a reported case itself. This latter requisite for the linking of a transmission pair sets this system apart from contact tracing and has implications for its applicability. First, in order to improve chances to find a transmission pair, all cases should be centrally reported. This only applies to notifiable diseases. Second, the symptomatic rate should be considerable. Even if a reported case were able to identify an asymptomatic infector, asymptomatic cases are nearly always unreported and would not be in the data. Third, the number of cases should be (or at least have the potential to become) considerable. When the number of cases is small, most transmission links will be found through contact tracing. When the number of cases increases, the required contact tracing may exceed capacity, at which point the linking of transmission pairs from a database of reported cases can complement the data or provide a cheaper alternative. For these reasons this system would be most suited for (re)emerging pathogens with pandemic potential.

Transmission pair data can be used for a range of other research topics. The serial interval distribution can inform models to estimate the generation time distribution and the proportion of presymptomatic infections [37, 46]. Building on the social contact hypothesis, the transmission pair data can be combined with contact data to infer age-specific epidemiological characteristics. The relative susceptibility of infectees and the relative infectiousness of infectors can be estimated simultaneously because the data contains the direction of transmission [47]. Also the spatial information of the transmission pairs could be a valuable data source, for instance, to study whether non-pharmaceutical interventions affect the distance between non-household transmission pairs. Aggregating the transmission pair data by infector yields the offspring distribution of the number of secondary infections per infector, which can be used to study superspreading potential [48, 49] or to provide parametrisation of network models.

Conclusions

Collecting transmission pair data is a valuable addition to routine surveillance. By identifying and linking infectors and infectees, detailed information can be generated about infection characteristics and transmission patterns of (re)emerging pathogens with pandemic potential. Although the implementation requires some adaptation of registration systems and some additional effort from public health officers, the resulting data set is an important resource for understanding how diseases spread. Furthermore, engaging citizens in the identification process can raise public awareness about transmission routes and prevention measures, strengthening community involvement in disease control [50]. Most importantly, timely collection and analysis of transmission pair data can provide crucial evidence to guide public health policy and interventions. Establishing such procedures now will enhance preparedness and response capacity for future pandemics, making it a worthwhile investment for public health systems.
