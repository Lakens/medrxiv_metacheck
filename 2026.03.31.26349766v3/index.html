To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
p = 1.00 With hidden semi-Markov model (HSMM) filtering, adding sensors to bleeding, position and symptoms improved CatBoost macro F1 by +1.09 points (nominal 95% interval [+0.39, +1.83]), but the difference did not remain significant after Holm adjustment (p = 1.00).

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It failed to detect 27 statements. Thus, among all statements flagged by the module, 63% were genuine cases (positive predictive value). However, the module missed 42% of all true statements (false negative rate).

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

The RetractionWatch Check module compares the reference list against studies in the RetractionWatch database based on the DOI. If a study in the database is found, a reminder is provided that the study was retracted, has an expression of concern, or a correction.

The module requires that the reference has a DOI: the DOI in the paper’s reference list, or one looked up with add_bib_match() before the module runs (see ref_table()). References without a DOI are not checked.

It is possible the authors are already aware that a study was retracted, but the module can’t evaluate this.

The database can be manually updated with the rw_update function. For more information, see https://gitlab.com/crossref/retraction-watch-data.

How Replication Check works

This module checks references and warns for citations of original studies for which replication or reproduction studies exist in the FLoRA database.

The Replication Check module compares the reference list against studies in the FLoRA (FORRT Library of Replication Attempts) database based on the DOI. If a study in the database is found, a reminder is provided that a replication or reproduction of the original study exists, and should be cited. No reminder is given when the paper already cites that replication or reproduction (matched by its DOI).

The module requires that the reference has a DOI: the DOI in the paper’s reference list, or one looked up with add_bib_match() before the module runs (see ref_table()). References without a DOI are not checked.

It is possible the original study was cited for other reasons than the empirical claim tested, or that the replication/reproduction in the FLoRA database is for only one of the studies in the paper, and not the study the authors discuss.

The database can be manually updated with the FLoRA_update() function. For more information, see https://forrt.org/FLoRA/.

Full text

Abstract

Whether symptoms and sensor measurements improve menstrual phase estimation beyond bleeding and cycle position remains unclear. We compared input combinations for four-phase estimation in 42 mcPHASES participants using nested feature selection and leave-one-participant-out evaluation against reference phases derived from hormones and menstrual flow. Across two tree-based classifiers, additional symptoms and sensors produced little consistent improvement over bleeding and bleeding-derived cycle position. With a gradient-boosted tree classifier, adding sensors to bleeding, position and symptoms changed macro F1 by +0.17 percentage points (95% confidence interval [-0.61, +1.04]). With sensors alone, features representing recent changes, trends and deviations from participants' prior averages increased macro F1 from 30.08% to 43.55%. The sensor-only estimates used temporal filtering to incorporate expected phase order and duration using only current and past observations. Fertility had the lowest phase-specific F1, limiting interpretation for fertility-related decisions. Sensor measurements thus provided information about menstrual phase but offered little consistent additional benefit when bleeding and cycle position were already known. Studies evaluating additional symptoms or sensor measurements should include bleeding and bleeding-derived cycle position as baselines to establish their incremental predictive value.

Introduction

Menstrual phase tracking can draw on bleeding reports, symptom questionnaires and physiological measurements from wearable or continuous monitoring devices. These inputs differ in what they measure and in the effort required to collect them [1]. Understanding which measurements improve phase estimation can guide the design of tracking tools, help reduce unnecessary reporting or sensing, and identify priorities for future data collection. A useful assessment must therefore consider both the information a modality provides on its own and what it adds when simpler observations are already available.

Previous research has linked self-reported symptoms to cycle-length variability [2] and examined glucose and wrist-temperature variation across the cycle or in relation to hormones [3, 4]. Modelling studies have used self-tracked records to label reproductive states [5], wearable measurements to identify menstrual phases [6], and physiological signals to detect the fertile window [7]. A systematic review has also assessed wearable approaches to fertility-window detection [8]. Together, these studies establish a basis for investigating symptoms and sensors in menstrual tracking. However, associations with cycle physiology and successful prediction from combined inputs do not establish how much each measurement improves prediction beyond bleeding and cycle position.

Recent analyses of mcPHASES address related aspects of this question. Nsugbe and Lin compared combinations of hormonal, metabolic and wearable inputs, with normalized cycle day included in the hormone group [9]. Xu et al. reported that wearable-based ovulation inference improved subsequent next-menses timing over static calendar and history references, with gains varying across cycle-irregularity profiles [10]. FemWear investigates wearable representation learning across menstrual and other health tasks [11], while an unreviewed public analysis reports strong phase-classification performance using bleeding-derived cycle timing alone [12]. These findings motivate a more specific measurement-selection question: which non-hormonal input families and sensor categories improve daily four-phase estimation when bleeding and cycle position are already known? An-swering this question requires distinguishing cycle timing from non-flow symptoms and comparing inputs within the same cohort and prediction task.

The mcPHASES dataset provides an opportunity for these comparisons by combining self-reports and nonhormonal measurements with reference phases derived from hormone estimates and menstrual-flow boundaries [13, 14]. The source study also examined participants' experiences of multimodal tracking [1]. Its paired measurements allow input combinations to be evaluated on the same participants and days. Because menstrual flow contributes to the reference definitions, the predictive value of bleeding must be interpreted in that context.

Here, we evaluate the standalone and incremental predictive value of non-hormonal inputs for daily menstrual phase estimation. We ask which input families and sensor categories add value beyond bleeding and position, how the representation of changes over time affects prediction when bleeding and position are unavailable, and how findings depend on report availability and temporal filtering. By identifying where additional measurements help and where their benefit remains uncertain, this analysis aims to inform measurement selection and priorities for further investigation.

Results

Incremental value of symptoms and sensors

Adding symptoms and sensors to bleeding and cycle position produced small mean changes in macro F1, with confidence intervals spanning zero for both classifieronly pipelines. With history features, CatBoost classifier macro F1 was 62.86% for bleeding and position and 63.28% for all four input families; the corresponding Extra Trees scores were 64.23% and 64.20% (Table 1). Accuracy and performance across all input-family combinations are reported in Table S1 and Figure S2.

Adding symptoms to bleeding and position changed CatBoost classifier macro F1 by +0.24 percentage points (95% interval [-0.44, +0.96]). Adding sensors to bleeding, position and symptoms changed it by +0.17 points ([-0.61, +1.04]). The corresponding Extra Trees changes were -0.07 ([-1.05, +0.90]) and +0.04 points ([-0.92, +0.95]). With hidden semi-Markov model (HSMM) filtering, adding sensors to bleeding, position and symptoms improved CatBoost macro F1 by +1.09 points (nominal 95% interval [+0.39, +1.83]), but the difference did not remain significant after Holm adjustment (p = 1.00). Extra Trees did not show the same improvement (Figure 1).

The matched comparison with the pipeline without feature selection showed a similar overall pattern for classifier-only predictions. With nested feature selection, adding symptoms and sensors together to bleed-ing and position changed macro F1 by +0.42 percentage points (95% interval [-0.55, +1.51]) for CatBoost and -0.03 points ([-1.15, +1.03]) for Extra Trees (Figure S8).

Individual sensor categories produced positive and negative mean changes depending on the baseline. Adding resting heart rate to bleeding and position increased classifier-only macro F1, with an unadjusted 95% confidence interval excluding zero. Temperature and heart rate added to bleeding, position and symptoms showed nominal improvements with HSMM filtering. None of these additions remained significant after Holm adjustment (Figure 2). Phase-specific additions showed further variation across the four phases (Figures S4 and S5).

Matched-seed comparisons showed higher macro F1 for both primary learners than for the prior-only baseline, with and without HSMM filtering. Differences from logistic regression, random forest and the multilayer perceptron were uncertain, with all nominal intervals spanning zero (Table S4).

Effects of temporal representation

Dynamics features improved sensor-only prediction. CatBoost classifier macro F1 increased from 24.11% with history features to 35.17% with dynamics, while forward HSMM performance increased from 30.08% to 43.55% (Figure 3). The largest mean improvements in that comparison occurred in the sensor-only configuration; changes were smaller when bleeding and position were available.

Sensitivity to delayed and missing reports

In the scored data, bleeding reports were missing on 15.3% of days and each symptom item on 11.4%; all reports were missing simultaneously on 4.7% of days (Table S9).

Weekly reporting gaps caused larger mean performance losses than one-day delays. For the CatBoost classifier using bleeding and position, delaying reports by one day changed macro F1 by -3.78 percentage points (95% interval [-5.25, -2.39]), compared with -10.28 points ([-13.72, -7.12]) when three consecutive reporting days were removed in each seven-day block. Performance also decreased in configurations including symptoms and sensors; sensor-only predictions were unchanged (Figure 4).

In the post-hoc comparison under matched reporting conditions, adding sensors to bleeding, position and symptoms changed classifier macro F1 by +0.00 percentage points (95% interval [-1.54, +1.59]) under one-day delays and +0.81 points ([-0.89, +2.41]) under weekly gaps. Neither comparison established recovery of performance through the added sensor inputs.

Temporal filtering, phase errors and calibration

Temporal filtering changed transition detection more than average phase-classification performance. For fullinput CatBoost with history features, HSMM filtering changed macro F1 from 63.28% to 64.50%. With threeday matching, aggregate transition precision increased from 67.7% to 76.9%, while recall decreased from 73.3% to 72.1% (Table S6). The precision-recall trade-off persisted with one-day matching, while exact-day precision and recall were low across all methods (Figure S9 and Table S6). Participant-level changes varied, with both improvements and decreases following HSMM filtering (Table S5). Timelines illustrate this variation for participants nearest the lower quartile, median and upper quartile of full-input HSMM performance (Figure 5).

Exploratory participant-level analyses showed weak and imprecise associations between reporting completeness and full-input classification performance. Correlations with missing bleeding reports were -0.21 for Cat-Boost (95% interval [-0.49, 0.11]) and -0.25 for Extra Trees ([-0.55, 0.09]). Reporting completeness showed no clear association with gains from adding sensors. For Extra Trees, participants with more missing bleeding reports showed greater gains from HSMM filtering, although this exploratory association was not consistent across both classifiers (Table S7). Only 3 participants had at least three complete reference cycles, providing insufficient support for analysing performance against cycle-length variability. Although 13 participants reported irregular menstruation, the released data did not identify these participants, preventing a comparison by self-reported regularity.

Fertility had the lowest phase-specific F1 and Luteal the highest across the four CatBoost input/output configurations in Table S2. For the full-input classifier, reference Fertility days were classified as Luteal on 36.3% of days and as Follicular on 18.6%, after equal participant averaging (Figure S1). Adding inputs or temporal filtering did not change the ordering of these highest-and lowest-performing phases.

High-confidence HSMM predictions were overconfident. In the highest confidence bin for full-input Cat-Boost, mean confidence was 98.2%, whereas observed accuracy was 72.6% (95% interval [68.4, 76.3]%; Figure S3). Daily probability trajectories show how filtering altered confidence and phase transitions for the same participants used in the timelines (Figure S10).

Feature attribution and removal

Bleeding had the largest mean absolute grouped SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) attribution, followed by cycle position and symptoms. Among sensor categories, heart rate and activity had the largest grouped attributions (Figure S6). Removing individual sensor categories produced small macro-F1 changes. Several nominal intervals favoured retaining a category, particularly with HSMM filtering, but none remained significant after Holm adjustment (Figure S7). Sensor attribution in the fitted classifier therefore did not establish a robust incremental benefit from retaining a particular category.

Discussion

Bleeding and bleeding-derived cycle position accounted for much of the phase-classification performance in this cohort. Adding symptoms and wearable measurements produced small, model-dependent changes, with no consistent improvement beyond bleeding and position across the two tree learners. Sensor-only prediction nevertheless improved with features describing changes over time, and models using bleeding and symptoms were sensitive to delayed or missing reports. Together, these findings show that the added value of symptoms and sensors depends on the inputs already available and how changes over time are represented.

Bleeding and cycle position provide a strong starting point for predicting the supplied phase annotations. This result must be interpreted in relation to their construction: menstrual-flow boundaries contribute to the reference phases alongside hormone-based fertile-window estimates [14]. A target established independently of menstrual flow could yield a different comparison.

Previous wearable studies provide evidence of cyclerelated physiological variation. Goodale et al. reported phase-associated changes in wrist skin temperature, heart rate and respiratory rate, and developed a fertilewindow classifier [7]. Kilungeja et al. demonstrated menstrual-phase classification from wearable measurements [6]. Such findings are compatible with our sensoronly results. Physiological variation can support prediction without substantially improving a model that already has informative bleeding and position features. Our comparisons extend this work by measuring the additional predictive value of symptoms and sensors against those explicit baselines.

The prediction target also helps explain differences between mcPHASES studies. Xu et al. reported improved next-menses timing using wearable evidence relative to calendar and historical references, with the effects of personal history differing across irregularity profiles [10]. Their post-ovulation timing task asks when the next event will occur, whereas our task assigns a phase to each scored day. Wearable measurements may add more information for predicting the next menstrual onset than for classifying phases partly defined by bleeding, although we did not directly compare these tasks.

Nested feature selection did not reveal a consistent average benefit from adding symptoms and sensors beyond bleeding and position. CatBoost with HSMM filtering showed a nominal sensor benefit when symptoms were already included, but this was not reproduced by Extra Trees and did not remain significant after multiplicity adjustment. The result suggests that input value can depend on the learner and temporal filter, while leaving uncertainty about whether the improvement would generalise. Similarly, prominent SHAP attribution indicates that a fitted classifier uses a feature, while small changes after retraining without it indicate that comparable performance can be obtained from the remaining inputs. Substitutable information is one possible explanation, but these comparisons do not establish redundancy. The confidence intervals leave room for positive effects, but do not identify a reliably beneficial sensor category.

The improvement in sensor-only prediction with dynamics features suggests that changes over time can be more informative than simpler summaries of measurement levels. Kilungeja et al. likewise found that classification performance depended on temporal windowing and phase definitions [6]. Our comparison motivates further study of representations that capture within-person change, particularly when bleeding histories are unavailable. Predictive patterns may reflect measurement availability as well as physiology.

Temporal filtering addressed a separate question: how to combine daily classifier outputs with expected cycle progression. For full-input CatBoost with history encoding, filtering increased transition precision while reducing transition recall, with little change in macro F1 (Figure S9). Thus, fewer false transitions can come at the cost of missed transitions. Whether that trade-off is useful depends on the intended task and the importance of correctly timing phase boundaries.

The reliability curves show overconfidence in parts of the HSMM probability range (Figure S3), indicating that temporal filtering does not ensure well-calibrated probabilities.

For the evaluated target, the results support using bleeding and cycle position as a reference against which additional data collection is assessed. They provide little evidence that collecting every available symptom or sensor stream improves average prediction. This can help focus future studies on the benefit of additional measurements relative to their collection demands, rather than assuming that a larger input set will be more informative.

That comparison must also account for reporting availability. Delays and weekly gaps reduced performance in models using bleeding and symptoms, and wearable inputs did not reliably compensate under the tested schedules. A configuration requiring few input types may still depend on sustained engagement. Prospective studies should therefore evaluate prediction performance together with reporting adherence and participant burden, which were not measured by our simulations or symptom-budget analysis.

Although simulated reporting disruptions reduced performance, observed differences in reporting completeness were not clearly associated with full-input classifier performance across participants. These analyses address different questions: the simulations alter information availability within matched participants, whereas between-participant associations also reflect other individual differences. The wide intervals leave substantial uncertainty about these associations.

The next step is to test whether additional modalities improve tracking against independently established physiological outcomes, using longer records and more diverse cohorts. Longer observation could help distinguish stable individual differences from cycle-related changes and evaluate the role of personal history suggested by Xu et al. [10]. Personalised models could adapt phase-duration priors or classifier parameters to earlier cycles, particularly for people with irregular cycles. Testing this approach requires longer records with participant-linked cycle-regularity information and evaluation on subsequent cycles. The present results do not justify prioritising a particular sensor, but they identify useful evaluation criteria: improvement beyond existing information, robustness to missing reports and wellcalibrated prediction probabilities.

Several limitations qualify these findings. The reference phases combine Mira-derived fertile-window estimates with menstrual-flow boundaries. Although the dataset authors report physiological patterns consistent with the assigned phases, independent confirmation of ovulation was not reported for this cohort [14]. Agreement with these annotations therefore does not establish accuracy against an independent physiological reference, and their dependence on bleeding may favour bleedingbased predictors.

The cohort comprises 42 young adults from the Greater Toronto Area, most identifying as East or Southeast Asian, and excludes hormonal contraceptive users [14]. Evaluation on held-out participants assesses generalisation within this cohort. Applicability to other populations and variation in performance by cycle regularity or reproductive conditions remain uncertain.

The analyses are exploratory, and modelling choices were informed by prior investigation of this cohort. Nested validation separates participants during fitting and tuning, but does not remove the possibility that prior exploration influenced the research design. The small sample limits precision, particularly for phasespecific comparisons. Uncertain differences between input combinations should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalent performance.

Predictors used only information available by the prediction cutoff, but daily aggregation, questionnaire timing and the initial warmup period may differ from prospective use. Simulated reporting gaps may not reflect natural missingness, and evaluation on unseen par-ticipants does not assess personalised models trained on an individual's earlier cycles. Device-derived scores may reflect proprietary algorithms as well as physiology.

Finally, the evaluated models, feature-selection strategy and temporal representations cover a limited range of approaches. Other modelling choices may change the estimated benefit of symptoms and sensors. Longer records and independent cohorts are needed to assess whether the findings extend beyond the present study.

In this mcPHASES analysis, symptoms and wearable measurements showed little consistent improvement in daily menstrual phase classification beyond bleeding and bleeding-derived cycle position. Sensor-only prediction improved when features captured changes and trends over time, whereas wearable inputs did not reliably compensate for the simulated reporting disruptions. These findings support evaluating additional measurements against explicit bleeding and cycle-position baselines to establish their incremental predictive value. Because menstrual flow also contributes to the reference phase definitions, the findings describe prediction of these annotations rather than independently established physiological phases. Fertility remained the least accurately classified phase by F1, and these results do not establish reliability for fertility or contraceptive decisions.

Methods

Dataset and reference phases

We performed a secondary analysis of de-identified data from mcPHASES version 1.0.0 [13, 14]. The source study recruited 50 menstruating adults in the Greater Toronto Area, of whom 42 consented to release their data for future research. Diabetes and hormonal therapy or contraception within three months before enrolment were exclusion criteria. The released cohort had a mean age of 20.9 years (standard deviation 2.8); 13 participants reported irregular menstruation (Table 2). The original study was approved by the University of Toronto Research Ethics Board (Protocol 41568), with written informed consent for participation and separate consent for data release and future use. Secondary use is governed by the mcPHASES data use agreement [14].

We used the 2022 collection interval because it included all input families needed for the modality comparisons. The 2024 follow-up omitted glucose monitoring and made symptom diaries optional. All 42 participants were retained, including those with prolonged reporting gaps; no cycles were excluded on the basis of reporting completeness. The daily grid contains 3,698 participantdays. We used the first 14 calendar days of each participant's record as a warmup for constructing historical features, including 14-day summaries, and excluded those days from scoring. The same warmup was applied to all input configurations to keep the evaluation period consistent. Days with missing reference labels were not scored. This leaves 3,110 scored days (Figure 6); missing measurements within the historical windows were handled as described in section "Input families and feature construction".

The four targets are the dataset's Menstrual, Follicular, Fertility and Luteal reference labels. Menstrual days are defined by menstrual flow, Follicular days span the interval between flow and the fertile window, Fertility corresponds to the estimated fertile window, and Luteal days span the interval between that window and the next menstrual flow. These definitions combine Mira's hormone-based fertile-window estimates with menstrualflow boundaries [14]. Fertility denotes an estimated fertile window rather than a confirmed ovulation day. Hormone measurements and phase labels were excluded from predictors. We included self-reported bleeding as a readily obtainable input that does not require hormone testing, and as a practical baseline for evaluating the added value of symptoms and sensors. However, menstrualflow reports also contributed to the reference phase definitions, which should be considered when interpreting bleeding-based performance.

Observation coverage and reference durations are summarised in Table S8. 40 participants contributed 90 observation days, with the remaining 2 contributing 38 and 60 days. We identified 76 complete reference cycles across 41 participants, with a median length of 29 days (interquartile range 27-33). Complete cycles required two observed menstrual onsets and an uninterrupted sequence of all four reference phases. An observed onset required a non-Menstrual label on the preceding day; complete phase segments required observed neighbouring labels at both boundaries. Reporting gaps were consecutive missing-report runs on the full grid, including observation-boundary runs whose full lengths may be censored. Zero or Not at all responses counted as observed, and reporting gaps alone did not exclude a reference cycle. Observation-boundary fragments and phase segments truncated by boundaries or missing labels were counted separately. Descriptive durations use the full observation period, including warmup.

Prediction task and input availability

We estimate the current day's menstrual phase after the daily questionnaire is available. Report inputs are available at this prediction cutoff, while sensor inputs use the preceding day's summaries. This one-day delay ensures that sensor summaries cover a completed day. Historical features and temporal filtering use observations available through the prediction cutoff. Missing reports are retained as missing values.

Input families and feature construction

We group non-hormonal inputs into four families: bleeding, bleeding-derived cycle position, non-flow symptoms and sensors (Table 3). Symptoms and sensors are defined separately from bleeding and position so that each family's contribution can be evaluated. Position features are derived from bleeding reports. Hormonal assays, reference phases and demographic identifiers are excluded from predictive inputs.

Sensor inputs include physiological summaries, devicegenerated scores and recording coverage. Temperature represents nightly skin temperature; heart-rate variability uses log-transformed daily median root mean square of successive differences between interbeat intervals (RMSSD). Glucose inputs comprised relative variability and change measures rather than absolute concentrations. Estimated oxygen variation is analysed on its exported signal scale, not as oxygen saturation. Fitness is represented by the device-provided VO 2 -max estimate, aggregated by the median within each participantday. Sleep combines duration and efficiency measures with a device sleep score; device stress is a separate composite score from self-reported stress. Table S10 provides a compact dictionary of the source tables, measurements and units.

Processing was performed separately for each participant and study interval. Exact duplicate records were removed. Repeated timestamps were combined by the median of valid values for glucose, heart rate, oxygen variation and activity streams. Sleep, temperature and five-minute HRV intervals were assigned to their end day before applying the sensor delay. Invalid records were excluded; daily value summaries remained missing when coverage was insufficient, while recording-coverage features were retained.

Nightly skin-temperature records required positive sample counts, finite values and sleep duration in (0, 24] hours; daily temperatures were weighted by sample counts. Resting heart rate and estimated VO 2 max used daily medians of finite positive values. HRV required finite positive RMSSD and coverage of at least 0.8; its daily median required at least 12 intervals and was transformed using ln(1 + RMSSD). Glucose summaries required finite positive values, at least 144 distinct timestamps and at least 12 hours between the first and last timestamp, including the final five-minute sampling interval. The five glucose features were SD/mean, interquartile range/median, 90th/10th percentile ratio, daily mean divided by the preceding seven-day mean (at least three observed days), and observed fraction (sample count/288, capped at one).

Activity used four intensity-minute and four heartrate-zone values, each set finite and nonnegative with a total of 1-1440 minutes, aggregated by daily median. Steps, distance, calories and altitude gain used sums over valid nonnegative records plus observed-minute counts.

Sleep used the longest valid main episode ending that day, requiring elapsed time in (0, 24] hours, recorded duration of 1-86,400,000 ms, asleep/awake minutes of 0-1440, time in bed of 1-1440 minutes and efficiency of 0-100; the daily sleep score was the median of values from 1 to 100. Nightly respiration used medians of positive full-night rates and nonnegative SDs, requiring positive signal-to-noise. Oxygen variation retained signed values; its minute medians required 120 observed minutes for daily mean, SD, median and interquartile range. Heart-rate summaries used the same four statistics on positive minute-level values, requiring 360 observed minutes. Both streams also contributed observed-minute counts. Device stress used median scores from 1 to 100 with READY status and a false calculation-failed flag.

RMSSD and estimated VO 2 max units follow the https://developers.google.com/health/reference/rpc/google.devicesandservices.health.v4. Although the dataset README specifies mmol/L for glucose, exported values span 2.2-15.5 in 40 participants and 40-253 in 2, without per-record units. Dimensionless glucose ratios avoid assigning absolute concentrations, but ratios to prior days assume consistent scaling within participant.

Flow volume is encoded on an ordinal scale from 0 (Not at all), 1 (Spotting/Very Light), 2 (Light), 3 (Somewhat Light), 4 (Moderate), 5 (Somewhat Heavy), 6 (Heavy), to 7 (Very Heavy). Flow colour is represented by nine one-hot categories, including separate categories for Other and Not at all.

A bleeding onset occurs when volume is at least 2 after two low-flow days. Let d be days since the detected onset (days since bleed (DSB)). Position consists of d, sin(2πd/28), cos(2πd/28) and min(d/28, 2). The sine and cosine terms provide a repeating representation using a fixed 28-day reference period. The clipped term provides a bounded measure of elapsed time, reaching its maximum at 56 days, while d retains the unbounded elapsed-day value. The reference period and cap are fixed modelling choices rather than participant-specific estimates of cycle duration. Report-delay and gap scenarios reconstruct position from the available reports.

Wearable measurements vary across the menstrual cycle [7], and previous wearable-based phase classifiers have incorporated slopes and changes within temporal windows [6]. We compare three temporal representations to examine whether predictive performance depends on explicitly representing changes over time. The latest encoding contains available base values and their availability flags. History adds 7-and 14-day means, sample standard deviations and observed fractions for non-position inputs. These fixed windows summarize measurements at weekly and fortnightly timescales, providing shorter and longer temporal context. Window lengths were not tuned and do not represent individual phase boundaries. Dynamics further adds differences from 1, 3 and 7 days earlier, a 7-day regression slope, and deviation from the participant's expanding prior mean. Rolling summaries include the current available value, which for sensors represents the preceding day. Means, standard deviations and slopes require at least three observed values; observed fractions use the available window from its first row. The expanding baseline uses only prior values and requires seven observations. Missing derived values are retained until fold-specific preprocessing.

These transformations produce 138, 528 and 853 candidate columns for the full-input latest, history and dynamics representations, respectively. History offers 88 columns for bleeding and position and 184 when symptoms are added. Within each training fold, preprocessing removes constant columns and redundant columns with absolute Pearson correlation greater than 0.95. Cat-Boost handles missing numerical values natively [15]; Extra Trees uses median imputation fitted on the training participants. These steps are repeated within inner training folds and then on outer training participants. Retained columns therefore vary by fold; the generated feature inventory and saved fold manifests provide exact names and selections.

Models and temporal filtering

CatBoost [16] and Extra Trees [17] provide two nonlinear tabular learners with different tree-building procedures. Comparing them tests whether incremental modality findings depend on the learner. CatBoost candidates use 200 trees, depth 3 or 5, learning rate 0.05 and L2 regularisation 3. Extra Trees candidates use 250 trees, a minimum leaf size of 3 or 10, and consider all available features at each split.

Daily classifiers do not explicitly account for the ordered progression and duration of menstrual phases. We therefore evaluated whether incorporating these temporal patterns improves phase estimation beyond daily classifier predictions. We compared the classifier alone with a hidden Markov model (HMM), which represents phase order and persistence through transition probabilities, and a HSMM, which additionally models phaseduration distributions explicitly [18]. This comparison tests whether temporal structure improves prediction and whether explicit duration modelling provides further benefit. Both models used forward filtering, so each day's prediction depended only on observations available up to that day. Figure 7 summarises the prediction pipeline. Both use the cyclic order Menstrual → Follicular → Fertility → Luteal. For each phase, duration probabilities were estimated from complete training segments lasting 1-60 days. We applied weak smoothing to avoid assigning zero probability to durations not observed in training. Complete segments required observed labels at both bounding transitions and eligible days throughout; segments truncated by recording boundaries or missing labels were excluded. For phase s and duration d, prob-abilities were (n s,d + q d )/(N s + 1), where n s,d is the training count and N s the total eligible segment count. The smoothing distribution q d was geometric with success probability 0.1, with mass for durations of 60 days or longer pooled at day 60. Its total weight equalled one segment per phase. The common smoothing distribution did not impose a common fitted mean duration. HMM transition probabilities counted same-phase and next-phase transitions between consecutive eligible labelled days, adding one count to each allowed transition before normalisation.

For classifier probability p t (s), the emission weight is max(p t (s), 10 -12 ) α , with α ∈ {0.25, 1, 2}. A reset probability of 0 or 0.05 permits recovery from an incorrect latent state. Forward filtering updates the state distribution using observations available through day t. The selected reset probability remains fixed during held-out evaluation; bleeding events and reference labels do not trigger resets. Pointwise maximum-probability outputs can skip phases despite the cyclic latent-state prior; we quantify these noncyclic transitions in the evaluation.

Participant-level validation and model selection

Given the small cohort, we use 42 outer leave-onesubject-out (LOSO) folds to retain 41 participants for training while evaluating each participant in turn. All days from a participant remain together because repeated observations share individual characteristics and temporal dependencies. This separation evaluates generalisation to unseen participants.

Within each outer training set, 3-fold participant-level cross-validation jointly selects classifier settings and a feature budget of 16, 32 or all available columns. Reduced subsets are ranked by an Extra Trees model fitted only to the corresponding training participants; the fullfeature option retains the learner's usual preprocessing. We calculate macro F1 separately for each validation participant and select the combination with the highest mean score. This procedure is applied throughout the experiment matrix, except for the prior-only baseline, which has no input features. Ranking uses trainingmedian imputation and an Extra Trees classifier with 200 trees, a minimum of five samples per leaf, square-root feature sampling and a fixed seed. Ties favour the full feature set, followed by the earlier classifier candidate. In the symptom-budget analysis, questionnaire items are selected before engineered columns.

We then keep the selected classifier configuration fixed and select the HMM and HSMM settings separately using the same inner validation predictions. For each inner fold, transition and duration patterns are estimated only from its training participants. Candidate filter settings are compared by mean participant-level macro F1.

Finally, we recompute any selected feature ranking, fit the classifier and estimate the temporal priors using all 41 outer training participants, then evaluate the classifier alone and with temporal filtering on the held-out participant. Preprocessing is fitted separately within each training fold. We repeat each primary configuration with seeds 17, 42 and 73.

Input comparisons and sensitivity analyses

We evaluate bleeding, position, symptoms and sensors individually and in every combination (15 input configurations), using CatBoost and Extra Trees on the same scored participant-days. Comparisons between models retrained with and without each input family quantify its added predictive value. The main comparisons add symptoms to bleeding and position, then sensors to bleeding, position and symptoms. Individual sensor categories are added to three baselines: position alone; bleeding and position; and bleeding, position and symptoms. We also remove individual sensor categories from the full-input model and retrain to assess their contribution when the remaining inputs are available. We selected comparison models with different levels of complexity: a prior-only baseline without input features, logistic regression as a linear comparator, and random forest and a multilayer perceptron as alternative nonlinear approaches. These comparisons assess whether predictive performance depends on the choice of learning algorithm (Table S3).

The main comparisons use history features, which add 7-and 14-day summaries to the available measurements. Additional analyses use latest values or add changes and trends to test whether the findings depend on feature representation. Models using only measurement-availability indicators and observed fractions test whether recording patterns themselves predict phase.

We simulate two reporting disruptions: a one-day delay in the availability of bleeding and symptom reports, and missing reports on the last three days of each sevenday block in a participant's record. Historical features and bleeding-derived position are recalculated from the reports available under each scenario, while sensor inputs remain unchanged. Models are trained and evaluated under the same reporting scenario; each comparison uses a single, matched random seed for the modified and observed reporting conditions. An additional post-hoc comparison tests whether adding sensors to bleeding, position and symptoms improves prediction under the same reporting disruption.

We examine the effect of reducing the number of symptom questions by comparing models using 0, 2, 4, 6, 8 or all 12 symptom items alongside bleeding and position. Symptoms are ranked by their association with the reference phases using training data only, separately within each training fold. All features derived from unselected symptom items are excluded.

To examine sensitivity to feature selection, we compare the nested-selection pipeline with the otherwise matched pipeline using all available features. The comparison evaluates the selected ranking strategy using the same participant splits, scored days and random seeds (Figure S8). This exploratory comparison was developed after earlier analyses of the cohort and a pilot. Classifier-only modality increments are compared using paired participant-bootstrap intervals without additional hypothesis tests.

Performance measures and statistical analysis

The primary outcome was macro F1, averaged across 3 random seeds within each participant and then equally across participants. Secondary outcomes included accuracy, phase-specific precision, recall and F1, calibration, and phase-transition performance.

Nominal 95% intervals use 5,000 participant bootstrap resamples, paired for differences. Two-sided paired signflip tests use 49,999 randomisations. Holm adjustment is applied separately across the specified macro-F1 contrasts and the secondary-endpoint contrasts. Confidence intervals are not multiplicity-adjusted.

In a post-hoc analysis, we compared each full-input primary learner with the prior-only, logistic-regression, random-forest and multilayer-perceptron baselines, separately for classifier and HSMM outputs. These comparisons use seed 42 on both sides, matching the comparator runs, with the same participants and scored days. Paired macro-F1 differences use participant-bootstrap intervals and sign-flip tests as above, with Holm adjustment across the 16 baseline-model comparisons (Table S4).

Transitions are matched one-to-one by destination phase. We evaluate exact-day matching and tolerances of one and three days, using one-day matching in Figure S9; the three-day tolerance assesses sensitivity to a wider matching window. The same saved predictions are used at every tolerance. Counts are averaged across seeds within participant, then summed across participants before calculating precision and recall. Table S6 reports all tolerances and timing errors. We report missed and spurious transitions as well as timing error among matched transitions. Transitions across missing-label gaps are excluded. Aggregate transition counts are descriptive. Reliability curves assess the classifier and temporal-filter probabilities directly, without temperature scaling (Figure S3).

We explored participant-level associations between reporting completeness and held-out performance using Spearman correlations with 5,000 paired participantbootstrap resamples and nominal 95% percentile intervals. Outcomes were full-input classifier macro F1, the change after adding sensors to bleeding, position and symptoms, and the change after HSMM filtering of the full-input classifier. Each participant's scores were averaged across seeds before calculating differences. Missingness was measured on scored days; symptom missingness was averaged across the 12 items. These posthoc analyses used history encoding and observed reports; secondary descriptors were the longest missingbleeding run, scored-day count, complete reference-cycle count and cycle-length SD. Unscored or nonconsecutive days broke a missing-report run. Cycle-length variability required at least three complete cycles. Before inspecting associations, we required at least ten supported participants and at least five participants differing from the modal descriptor value; cycle-length variability and scored-day count did not meet these criteria. Spearman correlations were recomputed within each resample, without hypothesis tests or multiplicity adjustment. Inference for these associations and the baseline-model contrasts is conditional on fitted predictions and excludes uncertainty from model refitting and overlapping training sets; the matched single-seed baseline contrasts also exclude variation across training seeds.

Model interpretation and reproducibility

Tree SHAP [19] quantifies feature contributions to the raw class scores of full-input CatBoost models with history features; it does not explain the subsequent temporal filtering. The analysis covers 126 fitted models, one for each of 42 held-out participants and 3 random seeds. Reconstructed models are verified to reproduce the saved held-out probabilities exactly before calculating SHAP values. For each model, absolute SHAP values are averaged across the held-out participant's scored days and the four class scores, then across seeds within participant and equally across participants. Features excluded during training receive zero attribution for that fold. Category attribution sums signed feature contributions within category before taking magnitude and applying the same averaging procedure. These attributions describe how the fitted classifier uses its inputs; the retrained comparisons in section "Input comparisons and sensitivity analyses" assess their incremental predictive value.
