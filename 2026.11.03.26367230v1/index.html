Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 6 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Contemporary data on long-term, cause-specific mortality among stroke survivors are sparse, and much of the existing evidence derives from studies performed before the modern reperfusion era. We sought to characterize long-term cause-specific mortality among stroke survivors to discharge (differentiated by stroke subtype) when compared to in-hospital stroke decedents treated under contemporary standards of care.

Methods: Using a hybrid billing-code/imaging and large language model-assisted chart-review pipeline, we identified adults hospitalized for ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke in the Observational Study of Cardiac Arrest Risk (OSCAR) cohort (Cedars-Sinai Health System, Los Angeles) between 2017 and 2020, followed through June 30, 2025. Cause of death was determined and classified into 10 categories.

Results: Among 1,344 stroke patients (mean age 74.0 ± 14.9 years; ischemic n=1,036 [77.1%], hemorrhagic n=308 [22.9%]), 203 (15.1%) did not survive the index stroke. Of the 1,141 discharged survivors, 625 (54.8%) died over a median follow-up of 5.2 years. Cerebrovascular causes predominated during index stroke hospitalization (36.0%) but accounted for only 15.5% of post-stroke discharge deaths. Among ischemic stroke survivors, cardiac disease was the leading cause of death (31.6%), with sudden cardiac arrest alone accounting for 10.9% of deaths. Among hemorrhagic stroke survivors, cancer (38.9%) was the leading cause of death. Despite these divergent cause-of-death profiles, overall survival did not differ significantly by subtype (median 5.5 vs 5.8 years; P=0.90).

INTRODUCTION

Stroke is among the leading causes of death and disability worldwide. In 2019, this condition was the second-leading cause of death globally and accounted for more than 100 million prevalent cases, reflecting a large and expanding population of survivors 1 . For these survivors, a critical knowledge gap remains, particularly in the current reperfusion era: what causes longerterm mortality after stroke? Accurate characterization of these causes is an essential requirement for development of approaches for post-stroke surveillance in the reperfusion era.

Much of the prevailing understanding of mortality in stroke survivors comes from a small number of population-based cohorts assembled between the 1980s and the early 2000s: the Danish contribution to the WHO MONICA Project 2 , the Perth Community Stroke Study 3, 4 , the Rochester, Minnesota, population 5 , and the Northern Manhattan Stroke Study 6 . Together these studies suggest that patterns of mortality after stroke are highly temporal. In the acute and subacute period, mortality is dominated by the index neurological injury and complications related to malignant brain edema and herniation. Thereafter, the cause-of-death profile shifts, with cardiovascular disease and recurrent stroke predominating among medium-term survivors and an increasing contribution from non-vascular causes over longer follow-up.

Three considerations limit how well this evidence applies to stroke patients treated today.

First, these patterns have largely been described in ischemic stroke and may not extend to hemorrhagic stroke, which carries a far higher acute case fatality 7 and a distinct long-term causeof-death profile 8 . Pooling of these stroke subtypes may therefore obscure two clinically distinct mortality trajectories. Second, cardiac death, and specifically sudden or dysrhythmic death, after stroke is a plausible consequence of the bidirectional brain-heart relationship, whereby autonomic dysregulation, catecholamine surge, and neurogenic myocardial injury may precipitate cardiac events [9] [10] [11] ; this constellation has been called the stroke-heart syndrome 12 . However, sudden and dysrhythmic death after stroke remains under-recognized and under-adjudicated in long-term cause-of-death series 11, [13] [14] [15] . Third, nearly all these cohorts were assembled before, or at the dawn of, the modern reperfusion era. Even the more recent Oxford Vascular Study, which characterized the time course of cause-specific mortality in 1,606 ischemic stroke patients recruited between 2002 and 2014, predates routine mechanical thrombectomy as standard of care 16 . Intravenous thrombolysis was established after the pivotal 1995 trial of tissue plasminogen activator 17 , and endovascular thrombectomy did not become standard care for large-vessel occlusion until the five randomized trials of 2015 and the pooled HERMES analysis that followed [18] [19] [20] [21] [22] [23] . Across this same interval, population-based registers have documented substantial declines in stroke case fatality 24.

Contemporary cause-of-death data, by contrast, remain sparse and heavily weighted toward the short term, with most reports describing mortality only through the first days to weeks after the index event 25 . Far less is known about how stroke survivors die over the subsequent years, now that more patients survive the index event with preserved function. We therefore sought to characterize the causes of mortality among stroke survivors in the modern reperfusion era, with particular attention to differences by stroke subtype and discharge location, and compare these patterns to causes of death among patients who died during initial hospitalization for stroke.

METHODS

Study design

Detailed methods for the Observational Study of Cardiac Arrest Risk (OSCAR) study have been published previously 26 . Briefly, the OSCAR cohort is an electronic health records (EHR) study of Los Angeles County residents with at least one patient encounter per year in the Cedars-Sinai Health System during two consecutive years in the enrollment period from 2017 to 2020. Follow-up began on the date of the second qualifying visit (as early as Jan. 1, 2017 to as late as Dec. 31, 2020). EHR data were obtained from all outpatient and inpatient encounters across the health care system from year 2012 onwards, in order to establish the baseline EHR profile for all included patients.

For the current study, we included patients aged ≥35 years with a hospitalization for stroke after the start of follow-up until June 30, 2020, in OSCAR. Stroke patients were identified with a combined use of International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes and the ChartScope platform (described below).

Survival time was defined as the interval from the index stroke discharge date to death from any cause, or until the end of follow-up (June 30, 2025) for patients still alive. Baseline comorbidities were defined by ICD-10 diagnosis codes, supplemented by procedure data as appropriate as detailed in our group's OSCAR design paper 26.

This study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline 27.

Hybrid Billing Code/Imaging Code and LLM-based Approach for identification of stroke hospitalizations

Billing and imaging code-based algorithm for incident stroke: We identified patients admitted during the follow-up period (January 1, 2017 to June 30, 2020) with a diagnosis of stroke who had both 1) a final stroke-related diagnosis or billing ICD-10 code and 2) a procedure code indicating evidence of brain imaging during that admission, described below.

We classified stroke-related ICD-10 billing codes based on published studies [28] [29] [30] [31] , and used Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes and International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, Procedure Coding System (ICD-10 PCS) codes for stroke imaging modalities in consultation with an expert vascular neurologist (coauthor A.S.). These billing codes and imaging codes are detailed in Supplemental Table 1. We identified 1,748 patients (1,288 ischemic strokes and 460 hemorrhagic strokes based on ICD codes) who had both an ICD-10 billing code as well as ICD-10 PCS or CPT code for cranial imaging during hospitalization.

We evaluated the performance of the billing/diagnosis plus imaging codes by selecting a random sample of 301 patients from the 1,748 identified (Supplemental Figure 1); two physicians conducted a detailed manual chart review for each hospitalization to determine whether each event was a stroke event or not. We defined a true-positive stroke as a patient with an ICD-10 code and cranial imaging for stroke during index hospitalization, which was consistent with an adjudicated stroke event. We defined a false-positive stroke as a patient with a stroke code and imaging when no adjudicated stroke event was documented during hospitalization. The positive predictive value for the code-based approach was 80.4%.

To improve our ability to identify stroke, we augmented this using an artificial intelligence (AI) approach (Supplemental Figure 1). We used ChartScope, an AI-assisted chart review platform, to develop a scalable EHR machine learning/large language pipeline to identify acute incident stroke events and then extract stroke subtype (ischemic vs hemorrhagic).

ChartScope was developed by coauthors R.C. and N.T. at Cedars-Sinai. The platform uses large language model-enabled querying of unstructured clinical notes using prompts to identify relevant patients more accurately and efficiently. Using sequential prompt engineering in the 301 patients who had undergone gold-standard physician adjudication, we developed a ChartScope prompt (Supplemental Methods) that achieved 89% accuracy, 93% PPV, 67% NPV, 95% sensitivity, and 63% specificity. Ischemic stroke was defined as any patient with clear evidence of an acute or subacute infarct, a patient who received intravenous thrombolysis for stroke or mechanical thrombectomy, or who had neurologist documentation of ischemic stroke.

Hemorrhagic stroke was defined as any patient with either an intracerebral hemorrhage or subarachnoid hemorrhage, evidence of hemorrhagic conversion of an ischemic stroke, or neurologist documentation of hemorrhagic stroke. We found that ChartScope was 92% accurate in classification of type of stroke when compared to manual review, with most of the errors coming in patients who experienced hemorrhagic conversion of ischemic stroke. We then applied this prompt to all 1,748 code-based algorithmically defined patients (including the 301 adjudicated patients), yielding 1,344 LLM-defined stroke hospitalizations that formed the analytic cohort.

Determination of mortality status, sudden cardiac arrest and cause of death determination

The 1,344 patients identified by the approach described above were followed until June 30, 2025, or until death. Stroke decedents (patients who died in hospital or discharged to hospice) were determined by using the EHR's discharge status designation and confirming with death certificates. Stroke survivors were defined as patients who survived initial stroke hospitalization and were discharged successfully to home, to a post-acute care facility, or left against medical advice. Sudden cardiac deaths were determined from death certificate records from the California Department of Public Health.

We identified cases of presumed out-of-hospital cardiac arrest from the regional emergency medical services (EMS) database. Thereafter, trained physicians conducted a careful case adjudication using a standardized method as well as all available data, including medical records, EMS reports, and death certificates 26 . For this analysis, sudden cardiac arrest (SCA) was defined as a sudden and unexpected loss of pulse due to a likely cardiac cause 32 ; patients whose cardiac arrest occurred due to trauma, substance use, or natural non-cardiac causes were excluded. Survivors of SCA (n=1, 1.5% of 67 total SCA cases) were included in the SCA outcome definition.

Cause of death was determined using the primary ICD-10 mortality code on the death certificate. These causes of death were then sorted into 10 categories: stroke/cerebrovascular, cardiac, cancer, other neurological causes (primarily Alzheimer's Disease and other dementias), pulmonary, renal, infection/sepsis, diabetes/metabolic, GI/hepatic and other/unknown cases based on the leading causes of death as defined by the National Center for Health Statistics and other prior epidemiological papers 33, 34 . Adjudicated SCA cases were treated as a subset of cardiac cause of death; all SCA cases except 1 were fatal.

Statistical Analysis

Chi-square, Fisher's exact tests (for cells with expected counts of ≤5), and Welch's t-tests with two-sided P values <0.05 were used to evaluate statistically significant differences. For Age was analyzed as a continuous variable, reported as mean ± SD and median (IQR), and was also grouped into prespecified categories (<50, 50-74, 75-84, and ≥85 years) to characterize the age distribution of the cohort. Body-mass index was categorized according to World Health Organization criteria as underweight (<18.5 kg/m²), normal weight (18.5-24.9 kg/m²), overweight (25.0-29.9 kg/m²), obese class I (30.0-34.9 kg/m²), and obese class II or higher (≥35.0 kg/m²).

BMI category was missing for 40 patients (3.0%) and was the only imputed variable; patients with an ICD-10 diagnosis code for obesity were assigned to obese class I, and those without were assigned to the normal-weight category for analysis.

Survival curves were generated for the overall cohort and stratified by stroke subtype (ischemic vs. hemorrhagic) and discharge disposition.

RESULTS

Study Population

The study population consisted of a total of 1,344 patients from the overall OSCAR cohort hospitalized with acute ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke (between January 1, 2017 and June 30, 2020) and followed through June 30, 2025. The mean age of this cohort was 74.0 ± 14.9 years, 629 patients (46.8%) were male, and 1,036 (77.1%) had ischemic and 308 (22.9%) hemorrhagic stroke. On the basis of stroke index-admission disposition, 1,141 patients (84.9%) survived to discharge (to home, a post-acute care facility, or against medical advice [AMA]), whom we designated as stroke survivors. The remaining 203 (15.1%) patients died during the index admission or were discharged to hospice (designated as stroke decedents). Over follow-up until June 30, 2025 (median follow-up 5.2 years, range 4 days to 8.5 years), 625 (54.8%) of the 1,141 stroke survivors died during the follow-up period.

Baseline Characteristics: Stroke Decedents Versus Survivors (Table 1)

Stroke decedents and stroke survivors did not differ in age (75.2 ± 15.6 vs 73.8 ± 14.8 years; P=0.252) or male sex (42.4% vs 47.6%; P=0.169). However, stroke subtype differed significantly between groups: hemorrhagic stroke accounted for 46.3% of decedents but only 18.8% of survivors (P<0.001), and history of prior hemorrhagic stroke was more frequent among decedents (25.1% vs 14.3%; P<0.001). Decedents also carried a higher burden of prior cancer (45.8% vs 37.0%; P=0.017), whereas survivors more often had a prior transient ischemic attack (TIA; 42.6% vs 27.6%; P<0.001), hyperlipidemia (69.1% vs 59.1%; P=0.005), and a history of smoking (35.6% vs 25.6%; P=0.006). Body-mass index distribution also differed (P=0.030): survivors were more frequently overweight (36.4% vs 28.1%), whereas decedents were more frequently underweight (6.4% vs 3.2%).

Conventional vascular risk factors and comorbidities (such as hypertension, type 2 diabetes, coronary artery disease, heart failure, prior myocardial infarction, and chronic kidney disease) did not differ significantly between groups.

Baseline Characteristics among post-discharge stroke Survivors, by Subtype (Table 2)

Among the 1,141 post-discharge survivors, 927 (81.2%) had ischemic and 214 (18.8%) had hemorrhagic stroke. Survivors of ischemic stroke were older than survivors of hemorrhagic stroke (74.9 ± 14.2 vs 68.9 ± 16.0 years; P<0.001) and had a higher prevalence of atherosclerotic and cardiac comorbidities, including hypertension (81.7% vs 68.7%; P<0.001), hyperlipidemia (70.9% vs 61.7%; P=0.009), coronary artery disease (50.1% vs 40.7%; P=0.013), prior myocardial infarction (23.6% vs 17.3%; P=0.045), and heart failure (37.6% vs 29.4%; P=0.024).

Ischemic stroke survivors were more likely to have a history of a prior ischemic stroke (50.3% vs 36.4%; P<0.001) and prior TIA (45.4% vs 30.4%; P<0.001), whereas hemorrhagic-stroke survivors more frequently had a prior hemorrhagic stroke (40.7% vs 8.2%; P<0.001) and seizure history (22.4% vs 12.1%; P<0.001). Sex, race/ethnicity, body-mass index category, and discharge disposition did not differ by subtype.

Long term survival after discharge by stroke subtype and discharge location (Figures 1 and 2)

Median survival among patients who were discharged from the hospital after index stroke was 5.5 years (2,026 days) after ischemic stroke and 5.8 years (2,107 days) after hemorrhagic stroke, and all-cause mortality during follow-up did not differ significantly between subtypes (P=0.90, Figure 1).

Median survival among patients discharged home was >8.5 years (more than half of these patients were alive at the end of follow-up, so exact median survival cannot be calculated), whereas median survival among patients discharged to a post-acute care facility was 3.4 years (1,226 days) (P<0.001, Figure 2).

Causes of Death: Acute Decedents Versus Post-Discharge Deaths (Figure 3)

The distribution of physician-adjudicated causes of death differed significantly between the 203 acute stroke decedents and the 625 deaths occurring after discharge among survivors of the initial hospitalization for stroke (P<0.001). Cerebrovascular causes predominated among acute decedents, accounting for 36.0% of deaths versus 15.5% of post-discharge deaths (P<0.001). In contrast, cardiac causes were the leading mode of death after discharge, comprising 28.8% of post-discharge deaths versus 16.3% of acute deaths (P<0.001). Cancer accounted for a nearly identical proportion of deaths in both groups (approximately 22%). Other neurological (8.5% vs 3.4%; P<0.05) and infectious/septic (8.8% vs 3.4%; P<0.05) causes were also more frequent among post-discharge deaths than among stroke decedents.

Causes of Death Among Survivors, by Stroke Subtype (Figure 4)

Among the 625 post-discharge deaths (512 after ischemic and 113 after hemorrhagic stroke), the cause-of-death distribution differed significantly by index stroke subtype (P<0.001).

For ischemic stroke survivors, cardiac disease was the leading cause of death (31.6%), followed by cancer (18.4%) and stroke/cerebrovascular disease (16.4%). For hemorrhagic stroke survivors, the pattern was reversed: cancer was the leading cause (38.9%), followed by cardiac disease (15.9%) and stroke/cerebrovascular disease (11.5%). Among patients with no known history of cancer before stroke, cancer accounted for twice the proportion of post-discharge deaths after hemorrhagic stroke as after ischemic stroke (19.3% vs 9.6%; 11.3% among all stroke survivors).

Sudden cardiac arrest (SCA) was a prominent mode of death in both stroke subtypes, but ischemic-stroke survivors were disproportionally affected. As a clinical event during follow-up, SCA occurred in 56 ischemic-stroke survivors (6.0%) versus 9 hemorrhagic-stroke survivors (4.2%; P=0.296). The disparity was more pronounced among the subset of patients who died during the follow-up period. Among the 625 survivors who died during follow-up, SCA accounted for 10.9% of all deaths after ischemic stroke compared with 7.1% after hemorrhagic stroke. Overall, cardiac disease was responsible for 31.6% of deaths after ischemic stroke versus 15.9% after hemorrhagic stroke; SCA thus represented roughly one-third of all cardiac deaths in the ischemic group and nearly half in the hemorrhagic group.

DISCUSSION

In this contemporary cohort of 1,344 patients hospitalized with stroke in which survivors were followed for a median of 5.2 years, causes of death shifted from cerebrovascular disease during the index hospitalization to cardiac disease after discharge and diverged sharply by stroke subtype. Overall mortality in our cohort was consistent with contemporary US estimates. Early mortality (in-hospital death or discharge to hospice) in our cohort was 10.5% after ischemic stroke and 30.5% after hemorrhagic stroke, compared with national population-based 30-day case fatality of 8.1% for ischemic stroke, 39.5% for intracerebral hemorrhage, and 17.5% for subarachnoid hemorrhage 35 . In our study, cerebrovascular causes accounted for 36.0% of deaths during the index hospitalization but only 15.5% of deaths after discharge, when cardiac disease became the leading cause (28.8%). This cardiac predominance was most evident after ischemic stroke, in which cardiac disease caused 31.6% of post-discharge deaths and SCA alone accounted for 10.9%, approximately one-third of cardiac deaths. After hemorrhagic stroke, cancer was the leading cause of post-discharge death (38.9%). Despite these divergent profiles, overall survival after discharge did not differ by subtype, indicating that similar all-cause mortality can conceal fundamentally different causes of death.

Much of the current understanding of mortality in stroke survivors is derived from population-based cohorts assembled before endovascular thrombectomy became widespread. In those cohorts, cerebrovascular and cardiac causes were often combined, with little data on the specific causes of cardiac mortality. In addition, those studies combined hemorrhagic and ischemic stroke and thus did not investigate whether there was a difference in mortality patterns based on stroke subtype [2] [3] [4] [5] 16 . Contemporary cause-of-death data, by contrast, are sparse and largely limited to the first weeks after the index event, when deaths are driven mainly by the index stroke and its complications 25 . Our data show that this temporal difference in cause of death persists in a contemporary cohort. The dominant late cause differs by subtype, with cardiac and oncologic contributions after ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke, respectively, that exceed what pooled historical series would predict. In prior studies, cancers were the cause of death in 7%-11.8% of patients, and cardiac causes of death occurred in 20%-23% of patients, but these studies largely pooled ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke [2] [3] [4] [5] . Another strength of our study is the systematic, physician-adjudicated ascertainment of SCA assisted by EMS records.

The cardiac predominance after ischemic stroke is consistent with prior work and likely reflects overlapping processes 6, 10 . First, ischemic stroke and coronary disease share an atherosclerotic substrate, and ischemic stroke survivors in our cohort carried a high burden of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, coronary artery disease, prior myocardial infarction, and heart failure. Second, stroke can itself injure the heart through autonomic dysregulation, catecholamine surge, and neurogenic myocardial damage, a constellation termed the stroke-heart syndrome 12.

Cardiac complications develop in 10% to 20% of patients within days of stroke and are independently associated with 90-day mortality 12, 13 . Atrial fibrillation detected after stroke may partly reflect this neurogenic substrate 11, 14 . Sudden cardiac death (SCD) is a recognized but frequently under-attributed consequence of these mechanisms 15, 36 . However, secondary prevention after ischemic stroke may not include dedicated imaging to evaluate coronary perfusion; our study raises the notion that some segment of these stroke survivors may benefit from evaluation and treatment of undiagnosed ischemic heart disease and extended rhythm monitoring for SCA [37] [38] [39] [40].

The prominence of SCA is consistent with growing evidence that stroke is itself a risk factor for SCD, not merely a marker of shared vascular risk 15, 41 . During continuous monitoring in the first 72 hours after acute ischemic stroke, serious ventricular arrhythmias are detected in approximately 5% of patients and atrial arrhythmias in approximately 20% 42 . Because atrial fibrillation is a recognized cause of ischemic stroke, its detection after the event does not establish whether it preceded or followed the stroke. In contrast, more research is needed to understand whether detection of ventricular arrhythmia post-stroke is secondary to pre-established cardiac disease and a pre-stroke risk factor, versus whether it develops post-stroke secondary to cardiac autonomic dysregulation triggered by the stroke 14 . Insular cortex injury has been linked to disturbed cardiac autonomic control and myocardial injury [43] [44] [45] . Among patients with a first ischemic stroke in the Northern Manhattan Study, sudden death accounted for the largest share of cardiac deaths, and left parietal infarction independently predicted cardiac death 6 . Post-stroke repolarization abnormalities, including QT prolongation, have likewise been linked to malignant dysrhythmias but remain under-recognized 36 . Together with our observation that SCA constituted one-third of cardiac deaths after ischemic stroke, these data support viewing ischemic stroke survivors as a population at risk for SCD, consistent with the stroke-heart syndrome framework 10, 12 . Beyond monitoring for atrial fibrillation, structured longitudinal assessment of cardiac and sudden-death risk, including electrocardiographic abnormalities, ischemic evaluation, left ventricular function, and heart failure phenotype, may be warranted in the months and years after discharge, when the competing neurological hazard has receded. Identifying the stroke survivors at highest risk of SCD is an actionable target for a prospective study.

After hemorrhagic stroke, cancer accounted for more than twice the proportion of postdischarge deaths observed after ischemic stroke (38.9% vs 18.4%). Prior cancer was more common among acute decedents than survivors (45.8% vs 37.0%) and, among survivors, tended to be more frequent after hemorrhagic than ischemic stroke (42.1% vs 35.8%), raising the possibility that, in some patients, malignancy did not merely coexist with the hemorrhagic stroke but may have also contributed to it. Indeed, even among patients with no known history of cancer before stroke, cancer accounted for twice the proportion of post-discharge deaths after hemorrhagic stroke as after ischemic stroke (19.3% vs 9.6%; 11.3% among all stroke survivors). This is biologically plausible: in a large series of patients with cancer, intratumoral hemorrhage and coagulopathy accounted for most intracranial hemorrhages, whereas hypertension was implicated in approximately 5% 46 . Thrombocytopenia and treatment-related vascular injury provide additional pathways 46, 47 . Historical descriptions of survivorship after ICH, which carries a high early case fatality, identify cerebrovascular disease as the leading long-term cause of death, although these deaths occur largely soon after the index event 7, 8 . Among patients in our cohort who survived to discharge after hemorrhagic stroke, malignancy instead predominated. Thus, occult or active malignancy merits consideration in the evaluation of ICH without a clear hypertensive or amyloid explanation, consistent with guideline recommendations to investigate secondary causes 48.

Prognosis after hemorrhagic stroke may also depend more on cancer prognosis than on conventional vascular risk factors.

Collectively, these findings indicate that pooling of stroke subtypes obscures two clinically distinct mortality trajectories and that subtype-specific follow-up, with cardiac and dysrhythmic surveillance after ischemic stroke and oncologic evaluation after hemorrhagic stroke, may serve survivors better than a single generic post-stroke pathway. Discharge disposition was also strongly associated with long-term survival. Patients discharged home lived substantially longer than those discharged to a post-acute care facility: more than half of those discharged home were still alive at the end of follow-up, so their median survival could not be estimated, whereas median survival after discharge to a post-acute care facility was 3.4 years. The survival curves separated early, consistent with frailty and functional dependence after stroke as important determinants of longterm outcome.

Limitations

This study has limitations. First, it was conducted in a single health system in Los Angeles; although the cohort was demographically diverse, the case mix of a quaternary academic center may limit generalizability. Second, stroke identification relied on a hybrid billing-code, imaging, and large language model-assisted pipeline; despite a high positive predictive value (92.9%) and sensitivity (95%) against physician adjudication, its lower specificity (63.3%) and negative predictive value (67.4%) imply some misclassification of stroke status. Third, individual-level data on reperfusion therapy, stroke severity, medications, lesion characteristics, and functional status were not available, so we could not examine whether these factors modified cause-specific mortality. Fourth, except for adjudicated SCA, cause of death was based on the primary ICD-10 code on the death certificate, which is subject to misclassification 49.

Conclusions

In this cohort, the leading cause of death after discharge differed by stroke subtype: cardiac disease, including sudden cardiac arrest, after ischemic stroke and cancer after hemorrhagic stroke.

These findings support subtype-specific post-stroke follow-up and warrant further prospective evaluation of structured cardiac and sudden-death risk assessment in ischemic stroke survivors.
