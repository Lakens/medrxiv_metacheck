Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Introduction

Infectious diseases remain a major global health challenge, causing millions of deaths and substantial disability each year [1] [2] [3]. Symptom-based surveillance systems, which test patients who seek care rather than the population at large, are essential for monitoring pathogen circulation, co-infection patterns and pathogen interactions. Multiplex molecular panels now allow many pathogens to be tested in a single clinical encounter, and the resulting data on pathogens detected in symptomatic patients are growing rapidly [4, 5]. Inference from these data rests on an assumption that is seldom stated: that a pathogen detected in a patient contributed to the clinical event that brought the patient into surveillance.

Aetiology research has long shown that this assumption cannot be taken for granted, because pathogens are routinely detected in people without disease. The Global Enteric Multicenter Study (GEMS) used matched asymptomatic controls to estimate the fraction of pediatric diarrhea attributable to each pathogen [6]. In the CDC Etiology of Pneumonia in the Community (EPIC) study, multiple pathogens were detected in about a quarter of hospitalized children with pneumonia [7], and a comparison with asymptomatic controls showed that the significance of a viral detection depends on the virus and on the age of the patient [8]. These findings motivated statistical methods that integrate case and control data while accounting for imperfect measurements, including partially latent class models and Bayesian integrated analyses [9]. Their conclusions can depend on the diagnostic approach: quantitative PCR reanalysis of the diarrhea study raised the pathogen-attributable fraction of cases from 51.5% to 89.3% [10]. In the multicountry Pneumonia Etiology Research for Child Health (PERCH) study, almost all cases (98.9%) and community controls (98.0%) had at least one pathogen detected by PCR in nasopharyngeal-oropharyngeal specimens, so aetiology had to be estimated by integrating case-control data from multiple specimens [11].

The same problem arises when a single pathogen is detected among admitted patients. In a chart review of SARS-CoV-2-positive admissions in four US health systemshttps://paperpile.com/c/nRnw8e/3f5fx about a quarter were incidental, that is, patients admitted with rather than for COVID-19 [12] , and national hospital analyses accordingly distinguished admissions primarily for COVID-19 from the rest [13].

As multiplex panels spread, co-detections have become routine observations, but they remain difficult to interpret. Meta-analytic evidence on whether viral co-infection worsens clinical disease is inconclusive [14] https://paperpile.com/c/nRnw8e/UjokT and apparent changes in pathogen-specific burden can follow changes in testing practice [15] https://paperpile.com/c/nRnw8e/mpXcw as when the introduction of multiplex panels changed the ascertainment of pertussis-attributable deaths [16]. Surveillance samples are also subject to selection: restricting analyses to patients who are ill enough to seek care or be admitted (collider bias) [17] [18] [19] can induce spurious associations between pathogens that are independent in the community, a concern raised specifically for co-detection studies [20, 21]. Healthcare-seeking behavior and imperfect test performance [22] [23] [24] [25] [26] and shared seasonality [27] [28] [29] further complicate interpretation, and co-detection prevalence is a poor guide to interaction even in the absence of selection [30].

For mechanistic transmission models, the observation process that links infection to surveillance data matters as much as the transmission process: biases in surveillance data can propagate to estimates of the reproduction number [31]. Eales et al. [32] developed a mathematical description of case-based surveillance and showed that common indicators, such as case counts and the test-positive proportion, can be heavily biased by co-circulating pathogens with similar symptom profiles, in part because people infected with the target pathogen may be tested because of symptoms generated by another process. Their analysis centered on surveillance indicators. How such misattribution propagates into the parameters of mechanistic models fitted to the data, notably severity and pathogen interaction, has not been characterized.

Mechanistic models are the main tool for inferring pathogen interactions from surveillance data. Simulation studies show thathttps://paperpile.com/c/nRnw8e/dr0P8 when the epidemiological and observation processes are well specified, time series can carry enough information to identify interactions [33] , but identifiability can fail toward the extremes of plausible parameter ranges [33, 34]. Interactions [35] can alter incidence trends and the interpretation of surveillance [36] https://paperpile.com/c/nRnw8e/lXixp and co-infections by non-interacting pathogens need not be statistically independent [37]. Models of this kind have been fitted to multiplex clinical data [38] and to influenza and RSV surveillance data [39] https://paperpile.com/c/nRnw8e/iYkg6 whereas simpler non-mechanistic methods remain unreliable [39, 40]. Some have been fitted directly to hospital-based multiplex counts [35]. These models treat each pathogen-specific detection as a hospitalization due to that pathogen. An incidental-detection pathway could produce a similar signature, because the second pathogen is detected in patients admitted because of the first, yet whether and how such a pathway is absorbed into estimated parameters has not been examined.

We call this mechanism hitchhiker bias. It occurs when a mildly pathogenic or asymptomatic pathogen is detected in a co-infected patient because another, more pathogenic infection caused the hospitalization that triggered testing, and the detection is then attributed to the detected pathogen. Selection on admission (a collider) is followed by misattribution of the admission to the detected pathogen (a misclassification); hitchhiker bias refers to the second step. When infections are independent, the probability that the milder virus is detected among patients admitted because of the severe virus equals its community prevalence, so it is the attribution of that detection, not the detection itself, that is biased. Collider bias therefore distorts associations between pathogens through sample selection, whereas hitchhiker bias distorts the mapping from pathogen detection to pathogen-attributable disease burden.

Here we ask how hitchhiker bias distorts inference from hospital-based multiplex surveillance and whether an explicit observation model can correct it. We couple a two-pathogen SEIR model with explicit co-infection to a multiplex observation process that separates pathogen-attributable hospitalizations from pathogen detections among hospitalized patients. We first derive how the relative inflation of observed hospitalizations depends on the relative severity of the two viruses and on the co-infected fraction of the milder virus's prevalence. We then simulate epidemics from minimal to complete temporal overlap to characterize the amplitude and timing of the bias. Next, we fit transmission models to synthetic hospitalization data, with and without true biological interaction, to determine whether the unmodeled pathway is absorbed into reproduction numbers, pathogenicity, or interaction strength. Finally, we test whether an adjusted observation model recovers the underlying parameters. Our contribution is threefold: (i) we couple pathogen attribution to a two-pathogen transmission model with explicit co-infection; (ii) we show which mechanistic parameters absorb the misspecification and how this depends on epidemic overlap and relative pathogenicity; and (iii) we evaluate, in simulation, an adjusted observation model that represents hitchhiking detections.

Results

To characterize the hitchhiker mechanism in its simplest form, we first considered two co-circulating viruses with no biological interaction (θ = 1, i.e., infection with one virus does not change susceptibility to the other) that differed only in their probability of causing hospitalization, denoted by . Virus 1 was specified as the more pathogenic virus (ρ₁ = 0.05) ρ and virus 2 as the less pathogenic virus (ρ₂ = 0.0005), corresponding to a severity ratio κ = ρ₁/ρ₂ = 100. All other epidemiological parameters were identical for the two viruses (Table 1). We varied the relative timing of the two epidemics by changing the initial number of individuals exposed to virus 1 while keeping that of virus 2 fixed, which generated scenarios ranging from temporally separated to fully synchronized epidemics (Fig. 3a). For each scenario, we compared two quantities for each virus: the true hospitalizations, defined as hospitalizations attributable to that virus (Hᵢ), which are the epidemiological target of inference; and the observed hospitalizations, defined as virus-positive hospitalizations recorded by multiplex surveillance (Hĩ), which are the quantities actually observed (Fig 3b). Because the simulations were deterministic and free of observation error, any discrepancy between Hᵢ and Hĩ arose solely from the incidental detection of one virus in patients hospitalized because of the other. We first describe this discrepancy for three representative scenarios (minimal, partial, and complete overlap) and then quantify how it depends on epidemic overlap and relative severity.

Co-infection with a more pathogenic virus inflates the observed burden of a mild virus

Minimal overlap: When the two epidemics were temporally separated, the epidemic of virus 1 had largely ended by the time virus 2 began to spread (Fig. 3a, left). Virus 1 peaked at day 40 and virus 2 at day 220, and their prevalence curves crossed only at very low levels, so co-infections were negligible. Almost every hospitalized patient who tested positive for virus 2 had therefore been hospitalized because of virus 2 itself, and the observed hospitalization curves were indistinguishable from the true curves for both viruses (Fig. 3b, left). In this setting, symptom-based surveillance gave an accurate picture of the low burden of virus 2, and hitchhiker bias was absent.

Partial overlap:

When the virus-2 epidemic was initiated before the virus-1 epidemic had subsided, the simulations showed a clear signature of hitchhiker bias (Fig. 3b, middle). During co-circulation, a fraction of individuals infected with virus 2 were also infected with virus 1. In the model, a subset of these co-infected individuals was hospitalized because of virus 1, and multiplex testing then detected virus 2 incidentally. Because the standard observation model attributes every detection to the detected virus, these individuals were also recorded as virus-2 hospitalizations, even though virus 2 did not contribute to their hospitalization. This hitchhiker component (shaded area in Fig. 3b) inflated the observed virus-2 hospitalization curve. We quantified this amplitude distortion as the relative bias in peak hospitalizations, defined as [max(H2) -max(H₂)]/max(H₂), irrespective of when each maximum occurred. A relative bias of 0 indicates no distortion, and a value of 1 indicates that the observed peak is twice the true peak. In this scenario, the observed peak reached ≈3.5 hospitalizations per 10⁵ population per day, compared with a true peak of ≈1.3, giving a relative bias of ≈1.7. Because virus 1 peaked earlier, the hitchhiker component was concentrated in the ascending phase of the virus-2 epidemic and advanced the observed peak. We quantified this temporal distortion as the difference between the times at which H2 and H₂ reached their maxima. The observed peak occurred ≈11 days before the true peak. Partial overlap therefore distorted both the magnitude and the timing of the observed virus-2 epidemic. We characterize both distortions across the full range of epidemic overlap in the next section.

Complete overlap: When the two epidemics were fully synchronized (Fig. 3a, right), the prevalence of co-infection was highest and the hitchhiker component made up most of the observed virus-2 hospitalizations (Fig. 3b, right). Because both viruses circulated at high prevalence over the same period, a large fraction of individuals infected with virus 2 were also infected with virus 1. Many of them were hospitalized because of virus 1 and then recorded as virus-2 hospitalizations following multiplex testing. The observed peak reached ≈17 hospitalizations per 10⁵ population per day, compared with a true peak of ≈1.3, giving a relative bias of ≈12.6. This was the largest amplitude distortion among the three scenarios, even though the intrinsic pathogenicity of virus 2 was the same in all of them. Unlike under partial overlap, the observed and true peaks occurred on the same day (peak shift ≈0 days). Because the hitchhiker component scales with the prevalence of co-infection, it rose and fell in step with the virus-2 epidemic. It therefore increased the amplitude of the observed curve and made it more sharply peaked, but did not shift its timing. Complete overlap thus produced the largest distortion in magnitude but none in timing, whereas partial overlap distorted both.

In all three scenarios, the observed and true hospitalizations of virus 1 were nearly identical (Fig. 3b). In principle, virus 1 can also hitchhike on virus 2: a patient hospitalized because of virus 2 may test positive for virus 1, and that detection would be recorded as a virus-1 hospitalization. This pathway is included in the model as the term (1 -ρ₁)ρ₂γ₂NP₁₂ in H1 (Fig. 1). However, because virus 2 rarely causes hospitalization (ρ₂ was 100-fold lower than ρ₁), few such patients exist, and the contribution of this pathway was negligible. Under the conditions simulated here, hitchhiker bias was therefore strongly asymmetric. It distorted the observed dynamics of the less pathogenic virus and left those of the more pathogenic virus essentially unaffected. These three scenarios were chosen to illustrate the mechanism rather than to represent specific pathogen pairs or realistic epidemic timings. They show that, in this theoretical setting, the extent of hitchhiker bias depends on both the degree of epidemic overlap and the relative pathogenicity of the two viruses. We quantify these dependencies systematically in the next section.

Hitchhiker bias depends on epidemic overlap and relative pathogenicity:

To quantify hitchhiker bias systematically, we examined how the relationship between true (H₂) and observed (H2) virus-2 hospitalizations changed as the two epidemics overlapped to different degrees. To describe this overlap with a single number, we calculated the Pearson correlation coefficient (r) between the prevalence of infection with virus 1, I₁(t), and with virus 2, I₂(t), over the simulation period (see Model and Methods). We refer to this quantity as epidemic overlap. A value of r = 1 means that the two prevalence curves rise and fall together; that is, the epidemics are fully synchronized. Lower values mean that the epidemics are increasingly separated in time. Across our simulations, epidemic overlap ranged from r = -0.11 (95% confidence interval [CI]: -0.20 to -0.02) for the most temporally separated epidemics considered (minimal overlap) to r = 1 for fully synchronized epidemics (complete overlap). The lowest value did not approach -1, as might be expected for non-overlapping epidemics. A correlation of -1 would require one curve to rise exactly as the other falls. Two separated epidemics, by contrast, are both close to zero for most of the simulation period, before and after their respective peaks. These shared periods of low prevalence keep their correlation near zero, even when their peaks are far apart. The value r = -0.11 therefore represents the lowest overlap achieved in our simulations rather than a theoretical minimum. Values between -0.11 and 1 correspond to progressively greater temporal alignment of the two epidemics.

Amplitude Distortion:

We first asked how much hitchhiker detections inflate the observed number of virus-2 hospitalizations. As described above, we measured this inflation as the relative bias in peak hospitalizations, [max(H2) -max(H₂)]/max(H₂), which expresses the excess of the observed peak over the true peak as a proportion of the true peak. For a severity ratio of κ = 100, the relative bias increased steadily as the two epidemics became more synchronized (Fig. 4a). Under minimal overlap (point A), it was close to zero, meaning that the observed and true peaks were almost identical. Under partial overlap (point B), it reached ≈1.7, so the observed peak was nearly three times the true peak. Under complete overlap (point C), it reached ≈12.6, so the observed peak was more than an order of magnitude higher than the true peak.

This pattern follows directly from the structure of the observation model (Fig. 1). At any time point, the relative inflation of observed over true virus-2 hospitalizations is given by (H2 -H₂)/H₂ = [ρ₁(1 -ρ₂)/ρ₂] • (γ₁/γ₂) • P₁₂/(P₂ + P₁₂).

Because the two viruses have equal recovery rates in our simulations (γ₁ = γ₂), this expression simplifies to (H2 -H₂)/H₂ = κ(1 -ρ₂) • P₁₂/(P₂ + P₁₂).

The bias is therefore the product of two terms, each with a simple interpretation. The first, κ(1 -ρ₂), is a severity term. It describes how much more likely virus 1 is than virus 2 to cause hospitalization, and is approximately equal to κ when virus 2 is mild. The second, P₁₂/(P₂ + P₁₂), is an overlap term. It is the proportion of individuals infected with virus 2 who are also infected with virus 1 at that time. This proportion is close to zero when the epidemics are separated in time, and it increases as they become synchronized. This decomposition also explains why the bias was largest for a mild virus. Because virus 2 rarely causes hospitalization on its own, its true hospitalizations (H₂) are small. Even a modest number of incidental detections in patients hospitalized because of virus 1 is therefore large relative to H₂, and dominates its observed signal.

Temporal Bias in Peak Detection: Hitchhiker detections also changed the apparent timing of the virus-2 epidemic. We measured this effect as the peak shift, defined as the difference between the day on which observed virus-2 hospitalizations (H2) reached their maximum and the day on which true virus-2 hospitalizations (H₂) reached theirs. Negative values indicate that the observed peak occurs earlier than the true peak. For κ = 100, the peak shift did not change monotonically with epidemic overlap (Fig. 4b). Under minimal overlap, the observed and true peaks coincided. As overlap increased, the observed peak moved progressively earlier, reaching a maximum advance of ≈11 days at partial overlap (epidemic overlap, r ≈ 0.15-0.25). With further increases in overlap, the shift became smaller again and disappeared when the epidemics were fully synchronized (r = 1). This non-monotonic pattern can be understood by viewing the observed curve as the sum of two components: the true virus-2 hospitalizations and the hitchhiker component arising from co-infected patients hospitalized because of virus 1. The hitchhiker component depends on co-infection prevalence, which is highest when both viruses circulate at high levels. When virus 1 peaks before virus 2, the prevalence of co-infection, and therefore the hitchhiker component, peaks earlier than the true virus-2 curve. Adding an earlier-peaking component to the true curve shifts the maximum of the observed curve to an earlier time. The size of this shift depends on two opposing factors. The first is how large the hitchhiker component is relative to the true curve. The second is how far apart the peaks of the two components are. Under minimal overlap, the peaks are far apart, but the hitchhiker component is negligible, so the observed peak is not displaced. Under complete overlap, the hitchhiker component is large but peaks at the same time as the true curve, so it raises the observed peak without moving it. Only at intermediate overlap is the hitchhiker component both substantial and time-shifted relative to the true curve, and this is where the largest shifts occurred. The stepwise appearance of the curve in Fig. 4b reflects the daily resolution at which peak times were recorded.

Hitchhiker bias can therefore distort not only how large an epidemic of a mild virus appears in surveillance data, but also when it appears to peak. Amplitude and timing distortions were maximal under different conditions: amplitude bias was greatest when the epidemics were fully synchronized, whereas timing bias was greatest when they overlapped partially. An earlier apparent peak may be interpreted as faster epidemic growth. As shown below, this has consequences for the estimation of transmission parameters when models are fitted to observed data.

Dependence on relative pathogenicity:

The analyses above assumed a strong contrast in pathogenicity between the two viruses (κ = 100). In practice, co-circulating pathogens can differ in severity to varying degrees. We therefore examined how hitchhiker bias changes as this contrast is reduced. Keeping the hospitalization probability of virus 1 fixed (ρ₁ = 0.05), we varied that of virus 2 from ρ₂ = 0.0005 to ρ₂ = 0.05, corresponding to severity ratios from κ = 100, where virus 2 is much milder than virus 1, to κ = 1, where both viruses are equally likely to cause hospitalization. For each combination of severity ratio and epidemic overlap, we calculated the relative bias in peak hospitalizations (Fig. 4c) and the peak shift (Fig. 4d) for virus 2. In both panels, the horizontal axis shows epidemic overlap, the vertical axis shows the severity ratio, and the color scale and contour lines show the size of the distortion. Each contour line connects combinations of overlap and severity ratio that produce the same level of distortion.

The relative bias increased with both epidemic overlap and severity ratio (Fig. 4c). It was smallest in the lower-left part of the panel, where the epidemics were separated in time, and the two viruses had similar pathogenicity. It was largest in the upper-right corner, where the epidemics were synchronized, and virus 2 was much milder than virus 1 (relative bias ≈12.6). As κ approached 1, the bias became small at every level of overlap. When the two viruses are equally pathogenic, virus 2 causes a substantial share of hospitalizations itself, so incidental detections make up only a small fraction of its observed hospitalizations. The shape of the contour lines follows from the decomposition derived above. Because the relative bias is approximately the product of a severity term (≈κ) and an overlap term (the co-infected fraction of virus-2 infections), lines of equal bias are approximately hyperbolic. A reduction in one factor can therefore be offset by an increase in the other. For example, a moderate difference in pathogenicity combined with fully synchronized epidemics can produce the same bias as a large difference in pathogenicity combined with only partial overlap. Neither epidemic timing nor relative severity alone is therefore sufficient to judge whether hitchhiker bias is likely to be important; both must be considered together.

The peak shift showed a different pattern (Fig. 4d). For every severity ratio, the shift was close to zero at both extremes of overlap and largest at low-to-intermediate overlap (r ≈ 0.1-0.4), reproducing the non-monotonic pattern described above for κ = 100. The magnitude of this shift depended on the severity ratio. When κ was close to 1, peak shifts were negligible, because the hitchhiker component was too small relative to the true curve to displace its maximum. As virus 2 became milder, the hitchhiker component grew relative to the true curve and pulled the observed peak increasingly earlier. The largest advances, of ≈10-12 days, occurred at high severity ratios (κ ≳ 70) and low-to-intermediate overlap. At high severity ratios, the shift increased only slowly with κ. Once the hitchhiker component dominates the observed curve, the observed peak approaches the co-infection prevalence peak. The maximum possible shift is therefore limited by the time between the peak of co-infection and the true virus-2 peak, which is set by epidemic overlap rather than by severity. As in Fig. 4b, the stepwise contours reflect the daily resolution of peak times.

Together, these results show that, in this theoretical setting, relative pathogenicity and epidemic overlap play distinct roles in hitchhiker bias. The low pathogenicity of virus 2 determines how strongly its observed dynamics are distorted. Epidemic overlap determines how the distortion appears: as an inflated peak when the epidemics are synchronized, as an earlier peak when they partially overlap, or as both. Because these two forms of distortion affect different features of the observed epidemic curve, they may also bias different parameters when transmission models are fitted to surveillance data. We examine this question next.

Ignoring hitchhiker detections biases estimates of pathogenicity, transmissibility and interaction

We next asked whether the distortions described above carry over into the parameters estimated when transmission models are fitted to surveillance data. For each of the three overlap scenarios (minimal, partial and complete), we generated 100 synthetic datasets of observed daily hospitalizations for both viruses. Observation noise was added by drawing daily counts from a negative binomial distribution whose mean equaled the simulated observed hospitalizations (H1, H2), with dispersion parameter 0.04. We then fitted two models to each dataset by MCMC. In the first model, the hitchhiker effect was ignored (red in Fig. 5): every detection of a virus in a hospitalized patient was treated as a hospitalization caused by that virus. In the second model, the hitchhiker effect was modeled (teal in Fig. 5): the observation process explicitly included incidental detections in patients hospitalized because of the other virus. Both models estimated the same six parameters: the basic reproduction numbers (R₀₁, R₀₂), the hospitalization probabilities (ρ₁, ρ₂), the interaction parameter (θ) and the negative binomial dispersion. In every case, the data were generated without biological interaction (θ = 1). Fig. 5 shows, for each replicate, the posterior mean and 95% credible interval. Density curves summarize the distribution of posterior means across replicates.

Reproduction number of virus 1.

Estimates of the basic reproduction number of the more pathogenic virus were largely unaffected by the hitchhiker effect (Fig. 5, first row). Under minimal and partial overlap, posterior means from both models were centered on the true value (R₀₁ = 2.50), and the credible intervals of almost all replicates included it. The precision of these estimates increased with overlap: credible intervals spanned roughly 2.45-2.55 under minimal overlap and 2.49-2.51 under partial overlap. Under complete overlap, the model accounting for the hitchhiker effect still recovered the true value. The model ignoring it, by contrast, slightly but consistently underestimated R₀₁ (posterior means ≈2.48), and most credible intervals excluded the true value. This deviation was small, less than 1% of the true value. However, the observed virus-1 data were themselves essentially unbiased (Fig. 3b), so it cannot reflect incidental detections of virus 1. It instead points to a compensatory adjustment linked to the bias in other parameters, which we discuss below.

Pathogenicity of virus 1.

A similar pattern was observed for the hospitalization probability of virus 1 (ρ₁; Fig. 5, second row). Under minimal and partial overlap, both models recovered the true value (ρ₁ = 0.05). Under complete overlap, the model ignoring the hitchhiker effect overestimated ρ₁ (posterior means ≈0.058, roughly 16% above the true value), whereas the model accounting for it remained centered on the true value.

Reproduction number of virus 2.

The basic reproduction number of virus 2 was affected mainly under partial overlap (Fig. 5, third row). In this scenario, the model ignoring the hitchhiker effect overestimated R₀₂ (≈2.62 vs. 2.50), whereas the model accounting for it recovered the true value. This bias is consistent with the earlier apparent peak of the observed virus-2 curve under partial overlap (Fig. 4b). A model that ignores the hitchhiker effect can reproduce an earlier, steeper rise in observed hospitalizations by increasing transmissibility. Under complete overlap, where the observed peak was inflated but not shifted, R₀₂ was only marginally underestimated (≈2.49). Under minimal overlap, both models gave slightly low but uncertain estimates (≈2.43), with credible intervals that generally included the true value.

Pathogenicity of virus 2.

The clearest consequence of ignoring the hitchhiker effect was on the estimated hospitalization probability of virus 2 (ρ₂; Fig. 5, fourth row). Under minimal overlap, both models recovered the true value (ρ₂ = 0.0005). As overlap increased, the model ignoring the hitchhiker effect increasingly overestimated ρ₂. Posterior means clustered around ≈0.0015 under partial overlap and ≈0.0034 under complete overlap, approximately 3-fold and 7-fold the true value, respectively. These estimates were precise: their credible intervals were narrow and excluded the true value. When the hitchhiker effect was ignored, incidental detections were therefore attributed to the intrinsic pathogenicity of virus 2, and the model inferred, with high apparent confidence, that virus 2 was considerably more severe than it was. When the hitchhiker effect was modeled, estimates of ρ₂ were close to the true value in all three scenarios.

Interaction.

Ignoring the hitchhiker effect also produced spurious evidence of interaction between the two viruses, even though none was present in the simulations (Fig. 5, fifth row). The direction of this bias depended on epidemic overlap. Under complete overlap, the model ignoring the hitchhiker effect estimated θ ≈ 2.1, implying that infection with one virus roughly doubles susceptibility to the other. Under partial overlap, it estimated θ ≈ 0.7, implying a moderate inhibitory interaction. In both scenarios, the model accounting for the hitchhiker effect produced estimates close to the true value (θ = 1). Under minimal overlap, both models gave similar, highly uncertain estimates (posterior means ≈2.3; credible intervals spanning roughly 0.3-8). With almost no co-infections in this scenario, the data carry little information about interaction, and the estimates largely reflect the prior. Spurious interactions are particularly problematic because they could lead to incorrect conclusions about pathogen ecology. Depending on epidemic timing, an unmodeled hitchhiker effect can make two non-interacting viruses appear either to facilitate or to inhibit each other.

Propagation of bias across parameters. The biases in different parameters appeared to be linked. Under partial overlap, the overestimation of was accompanied by an underestimation of θ. This may reflect the model reproducing the early rise of the observed virus-2 curve through higher transmissibility, while reducing the interaction to avoid overpredicting later co-infections. Under complete overlap, the excess virus-2 detections were absorbed mainly by ρ₂ and θ. The spurious facilitation inferred in this scenario increases co-infection and therefore alters the simulated dynamics of virus 1, which the model offsets by slightly lowering and raising ρ₁. Bias arising from ignoring the hitchhiker effect for one pathogen can therefore propagate to estimates for the other, even when the data for that other pathogen are unbiased.

Together, these analyses show that, in our simulations, ignoring the hitchhiker effect biased several mechanistic parameters, and that which parameters were affected depended on epidemic overlap. Under partial overlap, where the main distortion was an earlier observed peak, the bias appeared in the transmissibility and pathogenicity of virus 2 and as spurious inhibition. Under complete overlap, where the main distortion was an inflated peak, the bias appeared mainly as overestimated pathogenicity of virus 2 and spurious facilitation, with smaller compensatory changes in the parameters of virus 1. In all scenarios, modeling the hitchhiker effect recovered parameter values close to the truth. Similar patterns were observed for other values of (0.005, 0.0125 and 0.025; S1-S3 Figs).

Ignoring hitchhiker detections biases estimates of pathogenicity, transmissibility, and interaction in the presence of virus-virus interactions

The analyses above assumed that the two viruses did not interact. Respiratory viruses may, however, interact biologically. Innate immune responses triggered by one infection can reduce susceptibility to another, whereas tissue damage can increase it. We therefore examined whether a genuine interaction modifies hitchhiker bias, and whether it can still be estimated correctly from surveillance data. In our model, interaction is represented by the parameter θ, which scales susceptibility to one virus in individuals currently infected with the other (Fig. 2).

Values of θ > 1 represent a positive interaction (facilitation), in which infection with one virus increases the risk of acquiring the other. Values of θ < 1 represent a negative interaction (inhibition), in which infection with one virus reduces that risk. θ = 1 represents no interaction. We considered a negative interaction (θ = 0.5) and a positive interaction (θ = 2) and compared them with the non-interacting case (θ = 1) under minimal, partial, and complete epidemic overlap. All other parameters, including the hospitalization probabilities (ρ₁ = 0.05, ρ₂ = 0.0005), were the same as in the previous sections.

Effect of interaction on observed hospitalizations.

We first compared the true (H₂) and observed (H2) hospitalizations of virus 2 for each value of θ (Fig. 6a). Under minimal overlap, the observed and true curves coincided for all three values of θ. Because the two epidemics were separated in time, co-infections were rare regardless of interaction, leaving little opportunity for virus 2 to enter the surveillance data through hospitalizations caused by virus 1. As epidemic overlap increased, observed hospitalizations of virus 2 increasingly exceeded true hospitalizations, and the size of this excess depended on the interaction. Under partial overlap, the observed peak reached ≈2.2, ≈3.5 and ≈7 hospitalizations per 10⁵ population per day for θ = 0.5, 1 and 2, respectively, compared with a true peak of ≈1.3 in all cases. Under complete overlap, the corresponding observed peaks were ≈15, ≈17 and ≈25. These differences follow from the effect of interaction on the frequency of co-infection. A positive interaction increases the proportion of virus-2 infections that are co-infections with virus 1, and therefore the number of patients in whom virus 2 is detected incidentally after hospitalization because of virus 1. A negative interaction reduces this proportion and attenuates the bias. It did not, however, eliminate the bias, because co-infections still occurred whenever the epidemics overlapped. Interaction therefore modified the magnitude of hitchhiker bias but not its basic structure.

Estimation under interaction.

We next asked whether the model parameters, including a true interaction, can be recovered when models are fitted to surveillance data. As in the non-interacting case, we generated 100 synthetic datasets with negative binomial observation noise for each combination of interaction (θ = 0.5 or 2) and epidemic overlap. To each dataset, we fitted two models, one in which the hitchhiker effect was ignored (red) and one in which it was modeled (teal). Replicates whose chains did not converge (R ̂ > 1.05) were excluded.

Pathogenicity of virus 2.

As in the absence of interaction, ignoring the hitchhiker effect led to overestimation of ρ₂ whenever the epidemics overlapped (Fig. 6b and 6c, upper rows). The magnitude of this overestimation depended on the interaction. Under a positive interaction, posterior means were ≈0.0023 under partial overlap and ≈0.0045 under complete overlap, approximately 5-fold and 9-fold the true value. Under a negative interaction, the corresponding values were ≈0.0012 and ≈0.0028, approximately 2.4-fold and 5.6-fold the true value. These values bracket those obtained without interaction (approximately 3-fold and 7-fold; Fig. 5). The bias in pathogenicity therefore increased with the strength of facilitation and decreased with the strength of inhibition, consistent with the corresponding changes in the number of incidental detections (Fig. 6a). Under minimal overlap, both models recovered ρ₂ accurately. When the hitchhiker effect was modeled, ρ₂ was recovered close to its true value in all scenarios.

Interaction.

Ignoring the hitchhiker effect also biased the estimated interaction, and the direction of this bias depended on epidemic overlap (Fig. 6b and 6c, lower rows). Under complete overlap, θ was overestimated for both types of interaction. For a true positive interaction, the model ignoring the hitchhiker effect estimated θ ≈ 4.5, more than twice the true value. For a true negative interaction, it estimated θ ≈ 1.2, so that a truly inhibitory interaction appeared weakly facilitative. Under partial overlap, the pattern differed. For a true negative interaction, θ was underestimated (≈0.2), which exaggerated the inhibitory effect. For a true positive interaction, it was only slightly overestimated (≈2.3). When the hitchhiker effect was modeled, posterior estimates of θ were centered close to the true values under partial and complete overlap. Under partial overlap with a negative interaction, however, credible intervals were wide, indicating that the interaction was only weakly identifiable in that setting.

Other parameters. The remaining parameters showed the same pattern as in the absence of interaction (S4 and S5 Figs). Under minimal and partial overlap, both models recovered R₀₁ and ρ₁ close to their true values. Under complete overlap, the model ignoring the hitchhiker effect slightly underestimated R₀₁ (≈2.48 for both θ = 0.5 and θ = 2) and overestimated ρ₁. The overestimation of ρ₁ increased with θ, from ≈0.056 for θ = 0.5 to ≈0.060 for θ = 2, compared with ≈0.058 without interaction. The reproduction number of virus 2 was overestimated under partial overlap (≈2.62 for θ = 0.5 and ≈2.60 for θ = 2), consistent with the earlier apparent peak of the observed virus-2 curve (Fig. 4b), and was close to the true value or slightly underestimated under complete overlap. The model accounting for the hitchhiker effect recovered all of these parameters close to their true values. The biases in transmissibility were therefore determined mainly by epidemic overlap and were nearly independent of the true interaction, and the bias in the pathogenicity of virus 1 varied only modestly with θ. By contrast, the biases in the pathogenicity of virus 2 and in the interaction parameter depended on both epidemic overlap and the true interaction, because the interaction changes the frequency of co-infection and hence the number of incidental detections of virus 2.

These results show that hitchhiker bias distorts inference even when the pathogens genuinely interact. Whenever the epidemics overlapped, ignoring the hitchhiker effect overestimated the pathogenicity of the mild virus, and more so under facilitation than under inhibition. It also biased the estimated interaction. Under complete overlap, θ was overestimated roughly two-fold for every true value considered (0.5 → ≈1.2, 1 → ≈2.1, 2 → ≈4.5). A true facilitation therefore appeared stronger, an absence of interaction appeared as facilitation, and a true inhibition appeared as weak facilitation. Under partial overlap, a true inhibition was instead exaggerated. In addition, smaller biases in transmissibility and in the pathogenicity of the more pathogenic virus were reproduced consistently across interaction scenarios. If the observation process does not account for incidental detections, excess co-detections can thus be absorbed into several mechanistic parameters at once, and even the inferred direction of an interaction may be wrong. Explicitly modeling the hitchhiker effect separated biological interaction from incidental detection and recovered the underlying parameters in all scenarios in which the data were informative about interaction.

Discussion

Multiplex testing of hospitalized patients is increasingly used to track the circulation, severity, and interactions of respiratory pathogens. Yet the data it generates record which pathogens are present, not which one caused the admission. By coupling a transmission model of two co-circulating viruses, in which individuals can carry both at once, with a multiplex-testing model, we showed that this gap can systematically misrepresent a mildly pathogenic virus that co-circulates with a more pathogenic one. Patients admitted because of the more pathogenic virus are tested, the milder virus is found incidentally, and the finding is counted as a hospitalization caused by the milder virus. In our simulations, this hitchhiker bias inflated the observed burden of the mild virus by more than an order of magnitude and advanced the apparent timing of its epidemic. When it was ignored during model fitting, it was absorbed into estimates of pathogenicity, transmissibility and interaction. A mild virus therefore appeared severe, and viruses that did not interact appeared to facilitate or inhibit one another. A model that represented incidental detection explicitly recovered the underlying parameters whenever the data contained information about them.

The size of the bias was governed by two quantities: the ratio of the hospitalization probabilities of the two viruses, and the proportion of infections with the milder virus that were co-infections with the more pathogenic one. Because the relative bias is approximately the product of these terms, a large difference in severity combined with modest epidemic overlap can be as distorting as a moderate difference combined with full synchrony (Fig. 4c). The bias should therefore be larger when the more pathogenic virus is more transmissible or more widespread. A less intuitive consequence is that low pathogenicity amplifies the bias rather than protecting against it. A virus that rarely causes admission on its own has a small true signal, which a modest number of incidental detections can overwhelm. Conversely, incidental detection of the more pathogenic virus in patients whose admission was caused by the milder one was negligible, so the bias was strongly asymmetric between the two pathogens.

Epidemic overlap affected the amplitude and the timing of the observed epidemic in different ways. Inflation of the observed peak increased steadily as the two epidemics became synchronized. The shift in peak timing, by contrast, was largest (up to about 11 days) when they overlapped only partly. This contrast reflects when incidental detections occur. When the more pathogenic virus peaks first, incidental detections accumulate in the rising phase of the mild-virus epidemic and pull its apparent peak earlier. When both viruses peak together, the same detections raise the observed curve without displacing it. The consequences of hitchhiker bias therefore depend on the timing of co-infections relative to each epidemic, not only on their number. Because epidemic timing is itself used to infer transmission and interaction, this temporal component matters for model-based inference as much as the inflation of burden.

When transmission models that ignored the hitchhiker effect were fitted to synthetic data, the hospitalization probability of the mild virus was the parameter most consistently affected. In the absence of interaction, it was overestimated roughly 3-fold under partial overlap and 7-fold under complete overlap. These estimates were precise, with credible intervals that excluded the true value. Other parameters absorbed the bias according to the way the observed curve was distorted. Under partial overlap, the earlier apparent peak was reproduced partly through a higher reproduction number of the mild virus. Under complete overlap, the inflated peak was reproduced mainly through higher pathogenicity and stronger interaction. Small compensatory shifts also appeared in the parameters of the more pathogenic virus, even though its own observations were essentially unbiased. In coupled transmission models, an error in observing one pathogen can therefore propagate to inference about the other.

Hitchhiker bias is related to collider bias but concerns a different inferential target. Collider bias arises when analyses are conditioned on healthcare seeking, testing, or hospitalization, because this conditioning can create associations between pathogens that are absent, or different, in the source population [17] [18] [19] [20] [21]. Hitchhiker bias concerns the interpretation of detections after this selection has taken place. A pathogen found in a hospitalized patient is treated as a cause of the admission, when the admission was in fact caused by another pathogen. The first bias therefore arises from who is included in the data, whereas the second arises from how their test results are attributed.

The two biases operate at different stages of the surveillance process and require different remedies. Collider bias is a property of analyses restricted to admitted patients. When two pathogens independently increase the risk of admission, they become associated among admitted patients even if they circulate independently in the community, and in the simplest case this association suggests inhibition rather than facilitation. Because collider bias requires both pathogens to influence admission, it is weakest when one rarely causes hospitalization, which is precisely when hitchhiker bias is strongest. Hitchhiker bias does not depend on any association being analyzed. It arises whenever pathogen-specific detections are used as a measure of admissions caused by that pathogen. In our simulations, it inflated estimated severity and, in most scenarios, generated apparent facilitation or inhibition depending on overlap. When both biases are present, they may therefore reinforce or partly offset each other, and correcting one does not correct the other. Collider bias is addressed by sampling from the source population or, where this is not possible, by analytical approaches such as weighting for the probability of selection [41]. Hitchhiker bias additionally requires information on the baseline probability of detecting each pathogen among patients admitted for other reasonshttps://paperpile.com/c/nRnw8e/zvWiR+UYtk4 as provided by control groups in aetiological studies [6, 11] , or from an explicit observation model such as the one developed here.

From a methodological perspective, our model provides a proof of concept for correcting hitchhiker bias. When the observation process was correctly specified, representing incidental detection explicitly improved the recovery of pathogenicity, transmissibility, and interaction parameters in simulations. Applying this framework to surveillance data will require empirical validation and an assessment of its robustness when these conditions are not met. More broadly, three strategies could improve the reliability of inference from symptom-based surveillance. First, existing hospital-based data can continue to be used, with models such as ours applied to adjust explicitly for incidental detection. This adjustment is strengthened when routinely collected information helps to separate detection from attribution, such as the primary reason for admission [12] or quantitative measures of viral load [10]. Second, clinical surveillance can be complemented with random population-based sampling, which captures both symptomatic and asymptomatic infections and provides direct estimates of community prevalence. Thirdhttps://paperpile.com/c/nRnw8e/zvWiR+UYtk4 hybrid designs can combine model-based correction with targeted community sampling or appropriate control groups [6, 11] , pairing the accuracy of population data with the feasibility of hospital-based surveillance.

Ignoring hitchhiker bias could also have practical consequences for public health. Mildly pathogenic or largely asymptomatic pathogens may be regarded as important causes of hospitalization, which could lead to misplaced concern, misallocation of clinical and diagnostic resources, and interventions targeted at the wrong pathogen. Spurious or distorted interaction estimates could, in turn, mislead expectations about the indirect effects of interventions, such as vaccination against one of two interacting viruses [39]. Accounting for incidental detection through model-based correction, expanded sampling, or a combination of both would give surveillance systems a more reliable basis for assessing pathogen burden and guiding policy.

Several limitations of our study should be noted. First, our model includes only two co-circulating pathogens, which simplifies the more complex setting in which multiple respiratory viruses and bacteria circulate simultaneously. We also represented pathogen interaction using a single parameter that modifies susceptibility during infection. In reality, pathogen interactions may be asymmetric, depend on the order and timing of infection, and affect susceptibility, transmissibility, infection duration, or disease severity through different mechanisms. Second, our analysis was based entirely on simulated data, and we have not yet demonstrated the presence or magnitude of hitchhiker bias using empirical surveillance data. The simulations establish that this bias can arise under the specified transmission and observation processes, but they do not show how large it is for any particular pathogen pair or surveillance system. Applying the framework to real-world multiplex testing data will require additional information on community infection prevalence, symptom profiles, healthcare-seeking behavior, testing practices, viral load or infection timing, and the clinical reasons for hospitalization. Third, although we examined a wide range of severity ratios (κ = 1-100), we assumed that each hospitalization was caused by a single pathogen. When two pathogens differ less in severity, both may contribute to the same clinical event, and attributing a hospitalization to one of them may need to be probabilistic rather than absolute.

Finally, although we used hospitalizations to illustrate hitchhiker bias, the same mechanism may apply to other forms of symptom-triggered surveillance, including outpatient consultations, emergency-department attendance, and mortality surveillance. Future studies should therefore validate the framework using empirical data and assess its robustness across different pathogens, surveillance systems, and observation processes.

In conclusion, a pathogen detected in a hospitalized patient is not necessarily the cause of the hospitalization. When this distinction is ignored, a mildly pathogenic virus that co-circulates with a more pathogenic one can appear severe, its epidemic can appear to peak earlier, and its interaction with the other virus can appear stronger, absent or even reversed. Mechanistic modeling alone does not remove these distortions; it requires an observation model that separates pathogen-attributable disease from incidental detection. As multiplex testing becomes routine, recognizing and correcting for hitchhiker bias will be important both for reliable inference about pathogen severity and interaction and for public health decisions that depend on such estimates.

Transmission Model:

To investigate hitchhiker bias in hospital-based multiplex surveillance, we used a deterministic compartmental model of two respiratory viruses spreading in a closed population. The model extends the classic SEIR (susceptible-exposed-infectious-recovered) framework to include co-infection: individuals can be infected with either virus alone or with both at the same time [20, 42, 43]. Both viruses were given identical transmission parameters (basic reproduction number, latent period and infectious period; Table 1), loosely based on SARS-CoV-2. They differed only in their hospitalization probabilities, so any difference between their observed dynamics reflects hitchhiker bias rather than differences in transmissibility.

The state space comprises 16 compartments, each defined by an individual's joint SEIR status for virus 1 (first letter; rows in Fig. 2) and virus 2 (second letter; columns in Fig. 2). For example, SS denotes individuals susceptible to both viruses, IS individuals infectious with virus 1 and susceptible to virus 2, SI individuals susceptible to virus 1 and infectious with virus 2, and II individuals infectious with both viruses. The full system of ordinary differential equations is given in S1 Text.

Each virus progresses through the SEIR states at its own rates. Susceptible individuals acquire virus i at the force of infection λᵢ, exposed individuals become infectious at rate σᵢ (mean latent period 1/σᵢ), and infectious individuals recover at rate γᵢ (mean infectious period 1/γᵢ). The two viruses are coupled through a single interaction parameter, θ, which scales susceptibility to one virus in individuals currently infectious with the other. These individuals acquire virus 2 at rate θλ₂ and virus 1 at rate θλ₁ (dotted arrows in Fig. 2). Values of θ > 1 represent facilitation, θ < 1 represents inhibition, and θ = 1 means no interaction. This formulation captures biologically plausible within-host effects during an ongoing infection: innate immune responses may reduce susceptibility to a second virus, whereas damage to the respiratory epithelium may increase it. We first simulated epidemics without interaction (θ = 1) to test whether, in the presence of hitchhiker bias, standard model-fitting approaches infer a spurious interaction (θ ≠ 1). We then simulated an inhibitory (θ = 0.5) and a facilitative (θ = 2) interaction to test whether a true interaction can still be recovered.

Symptom-based Surveillance:

To simulate surveillance, we assume that only infectious individuals who require hospitalization are eligible for testing. Each virus has an associated probability of hospitalization ( for virus 1 and for virus 2), representing the probability that an infected individual will be hospitalized due to infection and thus captured in surveillance data. Testing is applied only to compartments containing infectious individuals (i.e., SI, IS, EI, IE, II, IR and RI). All hospitalized patients are tested for both viruses with a multiplex panel of perfect sensitivity and specificity, and individuals who are exposed but not yet infectious test negative. Infections that do not result in hospitalization remain undetected unless accompanied by co-infection with a virus that does lead to hospitalization. MCMC

Model fitting was accomplished using a Bayesian MCMC approach implemented in the BayesianTools package in R. More specifically, we employed differential evolution with snooker updates (DEzs) as the sampling algorithm, which has been shown to perform well for correlated posterior distributions with few chains [45, 46]. Prior distributions and fitting settings are listed in S1 Table. Starting values for all chains were drawn at random from the prior distributions, so that chains started from dispersed points in parameter space, as required for multi-chain convergence diagnostics [47]. Five independent chains were then run for 20,000 iterations each, with the first half of samples discarded as burn-in to reduce the influence of initialization. Posterior samples from the remaining iterations were pooled to summarize marginal distributions and estimate posterior means and 95% credible intervals for each parameter. We computed the Gelman-Rubin potential scale reduction factor (R ̂) for each parameter and excluded replicates with R ̂ > 1.05 for any parameter. We visually inspected trace plots for evidence of mixing across chains and examined pairwise posterior correlation plots to evaluate identifiability and dependencies between parameters.
