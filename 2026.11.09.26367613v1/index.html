Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

statistical_tests Step 1: Testing group by age and group by sex interactions for cortical structural measures
We plan to run linear mixed-effects models including group, sex, age, group by sex, group by age, age by sex, site, and Euler number, with random intercepts for subject nested in families in order to account for sibling pairs. There are four structural brain measures of interest, as in the VIA 11 cortical paper, which are brain volume, cortical volume, surface area, and cortical thickness. As we have four brain measures of interest, we will use FDR correction for these four measures. Using the lme4 package in R, the models will look like this:

lmer(brain_measure ~ groupage + groupsex + agesex + site + euler_number + (1 | family_id / sub_id))

We are primarily interested in interpreting the interaction terms for group by age and group by sex. We are including the group by sex interaction already in step 1 because we expect there to be sex-specific group differences given the findings from VIA 11. For group by age, we expect to see a reduction across time for each cortical structural brain measure in each group, which will be more pronounced in FHR-SZ. To ease interpretation, we will mean center age such that the main effect of group can be interpreted as the effect at the mean age, across the investigated age range. For group by sex, we expect to replicate results from VIA 11, namely to see reduced brain volume, cortical volume, and surface area in FHR-SZ males, and increased brain volume and cortical volume in FHR-BP females. We additionally expect to see reduced cortical thickness in FHR-SZ males, which was not previously seen in VIA 11 but is predicted now that data also extends to VIA 15, given the accelerated cortical thinning observed in adolescence. Age by sex is included as a control variable since we expect slopes to differ by sex (i.e., earlier maturation in females) although we are not primarily interested in this variability. We further control for MRI site and euler number.

Step 2: Testing group by age interactions, stratifying by sex
Whether or not the group by age or group by sex interactions are significant in step 1, we will proceed to step 2, since there is a strong justification to test for sex-specific group differences in slope between VIA 11 and VIA 15 given the sex-specific effects already identified in VIA 11. To test for sex-specific group differences, we will stratify the sample by sex and run linear mixed-effects models similar to those in step 1, including group, age, group by age, site, and Euler number, with random intercepts for subject nested in families. The models will look like this:

For females only: lmer(brain_measure ~ groupage + site + euler_number + (1 | family_id / sub_id))

For males only: lmer(brain_measure ~ groupage + site + euler_number + (1 | family_id / sub_id))

We expect group effects similar to those observed in VIA 11. For group by age, similar to step 1, we expect to see a reduction across time for each cortical structural brain measure in each group, which will be more pronounced in FHR-SZ, for both females and males. Since accelerated cortical thinning starts earlier for females, we predict that reduced cortical thickness will extend to FHR-SZ females as well as males. One exception to the pattern of reduced cortical structural brain measures is FHR-BP females, who we predict will not show more pronounced significant age-related reductions in brain volume or cortical volume compared to PBC females at the whole group level. This is because they had larger brain volume and cortical volume in VIA 11, and studies of adult relatives of individuals with BP have larger volumes, so FHR-BP females may be on a different maturational trajectory.

Step 3: Testing group by age by sex interactions
Since sex differences cannot be formally tested in stratified models, we will test them in a three-way interaction (group by age by sex) in models that look like this:

lmer(brain_measure ~ groupagesex + site + euler_number + (1 | family_id / sub_id))

In this model, we can directly test the effect of sex-specific group differences on slope between VIA 11 and VIA 15. As noted above, we predict that FHR-SZ females will have a steeper decline than FHR-SZ males, which will also be true though to a lesser extent for females in general. This is because FHR-SZ females do not show reductions in brain volume, cortical volume, surface area, or cortical thickness already in VIA 11, while FHR-SZ males do, and because we know that females in general show an earlier inflection point of cortical thinning during adolescence, or the age at which the rate of thinning is fastest. Conversely, we predict that FHR-BP females will decline less than FHR-BP males, particularly in brain volume and cortical volume, as these measures were increased in FHR-BP females in VIA 11. Generally, however, we do not expect major sex-specific group differences in slope.

Step 4: Planned follow-up analyses
Additionally, planned follow-up up analyses will be performed based on the findings from the previous steps. For example, if we see a significant group effect in step 1, then we would take the step 1 model and adjust it according to the planned follow-up analysis (e.g., adding an additional covariate). If we see a significant group effect in step 2, then we would also add to the model from step 2. We think that models from step 2 are the most likely to yield significant results and advance to the planned follow-up analyses.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Objective: Schizophrenia (SZ) and bipolar disorder (BP) emerge in late adolescence or adulthood.

Offspring at familial high risk for SZ (FHR-SZ) or BP (FHR-BP) are more likely to develop psychiatric disorders. Prior work from the Danish High Risk and Resilience (VIA) Study found sex-specific volumetric brain differences in offspring at FHR-SZ and FHR-BP aged 11-12 years.

This study builds upon these findings by testing whether they reflect group differences in rate of change or stable group differences from early to mid-adolescence.

Methods: This study investigated MRI data from offspring at FHR-SZ, FHR-BP, and populationbased controls (PBC) at baseline (11-12 years; n=288) and follow-up (15-16 years; n=249).

FreeSurfer longitudinal processing derived total brain volume, cortical measures, and intracranial volume (ICV). Linear mixed-effects models tested group-by-age interactions and sex-specific group effects, as well as associations with psychotic experiences (PE) and lifetime Axis-I mental disorders.

Results: The analysis did not detect group-by-age interactions, indicating similar developmental slopes across groups. However, at both time points, males at FHR-SZ demonstrated reduced brain and cortical volumes compared to PBC and FHR-BP males. These effects were attenuated after adjusting for ICV, which was reduced in males at FHR-SZ. The analysis additionally detected volumetric reductions in males with PE, after controlling for risk status.

Conclusions: These results extend the previous findings to ages 15-16 years, indicating group differences are stable and persist into mid-adolescence. Associations with ICV suggest early neurodevelopmental effects, and volumetric reductions in males with PE suggest convergent effects with FHR-SZ.

INTRODUCTION

Schizophrenia (SZ) and bipolar disorder (BP) are severe mental disorders that typically emerge during late adolescence or early adulthood (1). Identifying their neurodevelopmental risk markers may enable earlier detection and preventative intervention (2). Following adolescents at familial high risk for SZ (FHR-SZ) or BP (FHR-BP) provides a unique opportunity to examine markers preceding illness onset, given the relatively high proportion of individuals at family risk who develop SZ or BP. Among those at FHR-SZ or FHR-BP, an estimated ~8-9% develop the same disorder as their diagnosed parent by adulthood, and more than 50% develop any mental disorder (3).

Neurodevelopmental markers of FHR-SZ and FHR-BP include structural brain alterations in childhood and adolescence. Cross-sectionally, FHR-SZ offspring aged 8-23 years have reduced intracranial volume (ICV), total brain volume, cortical surface area, and cortical thickness compared to controls (4, 5). FHR-BP offspring aged 8-19 years show less pronounced alterations (4), but exhibit larger lateral ventricles, cortical volume, and cortical surface area, and thinner cortex, compared to controls (4, 6, 7). Reduced surface area and gray matter volume in FHR-SZ and FHR-BP offspring aged 6-23 years have been linked to later psychotic symptoms (8). SZ itself is associated with decreased gray matter volume, cortical thickness, and surface area (9,10), with reduced thickness relating to symptom severity and illness duration (9). BP is more weakly associated with reductions in gray matter volume and cortical thickness (10,11). Structural alterations related to FHR-SZ and FHR-BP thus largely resemble those observed in SZ and BP, albeit with weaker effects (9).

Childhood and adolescence are periods of great neuroanatomical change, yet little is known about how FHR-SZ and FHR-BP relate to structural brain maturation, which may be more . sensitive to risk status than snapshots at any particular time (12). Typically, cortical thickness peaks early in childhood and declines continuously with age, while surface area and total brain volume peak later, around age 12 years, before declining (12,13). Previous work suggests that FHR-SZ is related to a steeper decline in cortical thickness between ages 8 and 23 years compared to controls, and that both FHR-SZ and FHR-BP are related to a less steep decline in surface area between ages 6 and 17 years (5, 8). A steeper decline in cortical thickness among children and adolescents at FHR-SZ and FHR-BP has also been linked to later psychotic symptoms (8). There is therefore preliminary evidence that risk status is related to deviating trajectories of cortical development. However, research in this area is relatively scant, and previous studies have included broad age ranges, limiting interpretability as developmental effects may differ across maturational periods, and have not considered sex differences. Sex-specific patterns in FHR-related neurodevelopmental alterations are crucial to consider given known sex differences in brain structure, brain development, and pubertal hormonal changes, which likely contribute to sexspecific patterns of symptom progression in SZ and BP (14) (15) (16). Males with SZ typically have more severe premorbid dysfunction, earlier onset, and greater cognitive impairment compared to females, while females with SZ or BP typically have greater affective symptoms, including depression and suicidality, compared to males (17, 18).

Previous work from our group investigated a large cohort of offspring born to parents with SZ, BP, or neither, enrolled in the prospective Danish High Risk and Resilience Study (VIA Study) (19) (20) (21). The VIA cohort comprises same-aged offspring, thus tightly controlling for age, and follows children and adolescents through a key developmental period (22), making it well positioned to address questions of brain maturation associated with family risk. At the neuroimaging baseline (20), when offspring were 11-12 years old, we identified sex-specific . patterns of structural brain alterations in the risk groups: smaller brain volume, cortical volume, and surface area in males at FHR-SZ compared to male controls, as well as larger brain and cortical volume in females at FHR-BP compared to female controls (23). The present study (preregistered on Open Science Framework; https://osf.io/nax97/overview) follows up on the neuroimaging baseline, investigating FHR-and sex-related longitudinal changes in brain structure between early and mid-adolescence, using data from the VIA Study's neuroimaging baseline and when participants were 15-16 years old (21). We hypothesized that we would replicate the sex-specific group differences observed at ages 11-12 at ages 15-16. While we did not previously observe FHRrelated differences in cortical thickness compared to population-based controls (PBC), we hypothesized they would emerge at 15-16, since the cortex undergoes the most rapid thinning in mid-adolescence (22). We additionally hypothesized that FHR groups would exhibit deviating rates of change relative to PBC, particularly for offspring at FHR-SZ, and that these effects would be more pronounced in offspring with an Axis-I disorder or psychotic experiences (PE).

METHODS

Participants

The present study included offspring enrolled in the VIA Study, a prospective, longitudinal, register-based cohort study following 522 offspring born to at least one parent with SZ (FHR-SZ, n=202), BP (FHR-BP, n=120), or neither of these disorders (PBC, n=200) since they were 7 years old (19) (20) (21). Written and informed consent from the legal guardian(s) and oral assent from In the present study, we analyzed structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data for 288 participants aged 11-12 years (VIA 11; FHR-SZ: n=104, FHR-BP: n=68, PBC: n=116) and for 249 participants aged 15-16 years (VIA 15; FHR-SZ: n=93, FHR-BP: n=61, PBC: n=95). A flowchart of all exclusion procedures is presented as supplemental material (SM; Figure S1). A dropout analysis comparing participants included with those not included in the present analyses is presented in the SM (Table S1).

Clinical variables

We used the caregiver-reported Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL) school-age version to measure behavioral and emotional problems (24). We used the Children's Global Assessment Scale (CGAS), based on caregiver and child interviews, to measure general functioning in the past month (25). We used the semi-structured interview Kiddie-Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia-Present and Lifetime Version (K-SADS-PL; (26)) with the child and their primary caregiver, followed by a clinical conference with a specialist in child and adolescent psychiatry blinded to risk status (author AAET), to assess lifetime Axis-I disorders, excluding elimination disorders, transient/unspecific tics, and specific phobias. We used the psychosis supplement of the K-SADS-PL to assess whether any PE had occurred in the past six months and four years. We used Tanner Staging to determine self-assessed pubertal stage based on the highest stage across pubic hair and breast/genital ratings. We used the Personal and Social Performance (PSP) Scale, based on an interview with the primary caregiver, to assess the caregivers' level of functioning in the past month (27). All measures were assessed at 11-12 and 15-16.

.

MRI protocol

At 11-12 and 15-16, participants underwent structural whole-brain MRI at either the Danish Research Centre for Magnetic Resonance (DRCMR) or the Center of Functionally Integrative Neuroscience (CFIN). Participants were scanned on a 3T Siemens Magnetom Prisma Fit MR scanner (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) using a 64-channel head coil at DRCMR, and on a Siemens 3T Magnetom Skyra with a 32-channel head coil at CFIN, using harmonized MRI protocols. T1weighted images were derived from 3D magnetization prepared 2 rapid acquisition gradient echoes (MP2RAGE) sequences, with fat image navigators for retrospective motion correction (28), using similar scan parameters across sites (see SM).

FreeSurfer preprocessing, extraction of brain measures, and quality assessment T1-weighted images were processed using the longitudinal processing stream in FreeSurfer (version 7.4.1. (29)), including surface-based nonlinear registration to the 68 parcel Desikan-Killiany atlas (30). We extracted cortical gray matter volume, surface area, and cortical thickness from each parcel and from each hemisphere, averaging hemispheres to create whole cerebral cortex measures. We also extracted total brain volume and through the FreeSurfer samseg processing stream (31) intracranial volume (ICV). See SM for our quality control procedure.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics

All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.2). We computed descriptive statistics separately at ages 11-12 and 15-16 for the full sample and sex-stratified subsamples. To test FHR group differences on descriptive variables, we conducted one-way analyses of variance (ANOVAs) for continuous variables (age, CBCL Total Problems, and CGAS) and chi-square tests for categorical variables (site, Axis-I, PE past six months, PE past four years). For categorical variables, we used Fisher's exact tests if any cell had N<5 (PE past six months, PE past four years).

Hypothesis testing: group-by-age interactions and group main effects

We tested four whole-brain measures: brain volume, cortical volume, surface area, and cortical thickness. We used the lme4 package (version 1.1-37) in R to fit linear mixed-effects models, with random intercepts for participant nested within family (9 sibling pairs; FHR-SZ: n=5, FHR-BP: n=2, PBC: n=2) to account for repeated measures over time. All subsequent linear mixed-effects models described included random intercepts for family and participant ID, and controlled for site and Euler number.

First, we tested models with group-by-age, group-by-sex, and age-by-sex interactions to examine whether age effects on any brain measure differed between FHR and PBC groups and whether group effects varied by sex. Second, given our previous sex-specific findings at ages 11-12 (23) and known sex differences in brain maturation (14-16), we stratified by sex to test for group-by-age interactions in females and males separately. If the group-by-age interaction was not significant, we refit models without the interaction to evaluate the main effect of group. We ran these models with and without ICV as an additional covariate to test group differences in relative and absolute brain measures. In exploratory analyses, we tested models with ICV as the dependent variable, stratified by sex. We lastly tested models with group-by-age-by-sex three-way interactions. For each set of models, we controlled for multiple comparisons using false discovery rate (FDR) correction across the four brain measures of interest. For FHR group main effects significant after FDR correction, we extracted pairwise FHR group differences using the emmeans package (version 2.0.0) in R to identify group differences driving the main effect.

Follow-up analysis: Tanner Stage, PE, and Axis-I

In follow-up analyses, for significant FDR-corrected group main effects, we added PE past four years, lifetime Axis-I, or puberty stage, in separate models. All measures were assessed at ages 11-12 and 15-16, and puberty stage encompassed Tanner Stage and the time delay between Tanner Stage assessment and MRI.

We included PE -past four years as an additional covariate to test whether the group main effect persisted, and whether there was a main effect of PE. If there was a significant main effect of PE, we further explored this effect by splitting PE into four groups: no PE ever ("No PE"), PE at 11-12 only ("Remitting PE"), PE at 15-16 only ("Emerging PE"), and PE at 11-12 and 15-16 ("Persistent PE"). Second, we fit separate models with either a PE-by-age or a group-by-PE interaction to test whether age or FHR group effects, respectively, differed by PE group. We used the same approach to test effects of lifetime Axis-I. As these were follow-up analyses, we did not employ FDR correction.

Parcelwise analysis

In an exploratory analysis, for significant group main effects, we tested pairwise FHR group differences across 68 parcels in the Desikan-Killiany atlas to identify cortical regions driving the effects.

RESULTS

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Demographic and clinical characteristics are presented in Table 1 for the present sample (i.e., a subsample of the VIA cohort) and stratified by females and males at ages 11-12 and 15-16. FHR groups did not differ by sex, age, or site at either time point. At ages 11-12 and 15-16, FHR-SZ and FHR-BP groups had higher CBCL Total Problems scores and lower CGAS scores than PBC, indicating elevated behavioral problems and lower global functioning. Both FHR groups had a higher prevalence of any lifetime Axis-I diagnosis than PBC at ages 11-12, as previously reported (32), while only the FHR-SZ group had a higher prevalence of Axis-I diagnosis than PBC at 15-16 in this subsample. The FHR-SZ group also had a higher prevalence of PE past six months than PBC at ages 15-16, but not 11-12. Groups did not significantly differ in changes over time for CBCL Total Problems, CGAS, Axis-I diagnosis, PE past four years, or PE past six months. Table S1 compares participants included in the present analysis with those not included, by FHR group.

Hypothesis testing: group-by-age interactions and group main effects

None of the group-by-age interactions were significant (Eta 2 s<0.025, Fs<2.600, ps>0.075), indicating that slopes did not differ across groups. Two group-by-sex interactions for brain (Eta2 =0.024, F=4.103, p=0.017) and cortical volume (Eta2 =0.02, F=3.486, p=0.032) suggested sex-dependent group main effects but were not significant after FDR correction. Changes in brain and cortical volumes between ages 11-12 and 15-16 are shown in Figure 1 for each FHR group, stratified by sex.

In sex-stratified models, we also did not observe significant group-by-age interactions for females or males (ps>0.059; Table 2), but we did observe FDR-corrected significant group effects for brain (Eta2 =0.065, F=6.056, p=0.003) and cortical volume (Eta2 =0.047, F=4.318, p=0.015) in males only (Table 2). These effects were driven by smaller brain and cortical volumes in FHR-SZ males compared to both PBC and FHR-BP males (ds<-0.400, ts<-2.100, ps<0.035). See Table S2 for pairwise group differences.

Adding ICV diminished the group effects in males for both brain and cortical volume (ps>0.154) (Table 2). We also observed significant group effects on ICV only in males (Eta2=0.061, F=5.822, p=0.004) (Table 2), with smaller ICV in FHR-SZ males relative to PBC and FHR-BP males (ds<-0.570, ts<-2.700, ps<0.010) (Table S2).

In females, group effects for brain and cortical volume (Eta 2 s>0.035, Fs>3.245, ps<0.050) became more pronounced when adding ICV, but did not survive FDR correction (Table 2). These results were driven by FHR-BP females having larger brain and cortical volumes than PBC females (ds>0.290, ts>2.350, ps<0.025) (Table S2).

Models testing group-by-age-by-sex interactions revealed no significant three-way interactions (Eta 2 s<0.010, Fs<1.140, ps>0.320) and are reported in the SM. S3). PE was independently significantly associated with lower brain (Eta2 =0.073, F=6.832, p=0.011) and cortical volume (Eta2 =0.039, F=4.093, p=0.046). See Figure 2 and Table S3. We found no evidence for PE-by-age or group-by-PE interactions for brain or cortical volume (Eta 2 s<0.025, Fs<1.160, ps>0.315). When exploratorily testing "No PE" (n=172), "Remitting PE" (n=45), "Emerging PE" (n=24), and "Persistent PE" (n=19), there was a significant effect of PE group on cortical (Eta2 =0.050, F=2.680, p=0.049) but not brain volume (Eta2 =0.035, F=1.824, p=0.145) (Figure 2), with "Persistent PE" having descriptively the lowest mean cortical volume.

Any Axis-I disorder:

In males, group effects on brain and cortical volume persisted when adding Axis-I diagnosis (ps<0.020; Table S3). There was no main effect of Axis-I diagnosis, nor Axis-I-by-age or group-by-Axis-I interactions (Eta 2 s<0.015, Fs<1.025, ps>0.310), for either brain or cortical volume in males.

Parcelwise group main effects

Sex-specific pairwise group differences for each parcel revealed widespread smaller cortical volumes in males at FHR-SZ relative to FHR-BP and PBC. For FHR-SZ versus FHR-BP, eight parcels survived FDR correction in males: right lateral orbitofrontal cortex, superior temporal gyrus, pars orbitalis, paracentral cortex, and inferior temporal gyrus, as well as left pars orbitalis and medial and lateral orbitofrontal cortex. Sex-specific pairwise group differences in cortical volume are visualized in Figures 3 and S2.

DISCUSSION

In this, to date, largest longitudinal study of same-aged offspring at FHR-SZ or FHR-BP, we identified global volumetric reductions in FHR-SZ males compared to FHR-BP and PBC males at ages 11-12 and 15-16 years, extending our previous findings from this cohort (23). We therefore conclude that stable group differences persist from early to mid-adolescence. Parcel-wise analyses suggest widespread cortical effects. The observed volumetric reductions in males at FHR-SZ were driven by smaller ICV, suggesting an early neurodevelopmental effect. Observed group effects on volume remained when accounting for pubertal stage, lifetime Axis-I diagnosis, or PE, and volumetric reductions in males with PE suggest convergent effects of PE and FHR-SZ.

The observed volumetric reductions in males at FHR-SZ, driven by smaller ICV, are consistent with evidence implicating early neurodevelopmental processes in genetic vulnerability to SZ (33). ICV largely reflects early brain growth, with 80-90% of maximum brain volume attained by age 3 years (13). In an analysis combining both sexes, reduced fetal head circumference during the third trimester has been reported in offspring of women with psychotic disorders (34).

Genetic evidence also implicates placenta processes in SZ vulnerability (33). Higher placentarelated polygenic risk scores for SZ have been associated with infant ICV, with significant associations in males but not in females (35). These findings suggest that prenatal developmental and genetic factors may contribute to the volumetric reductions observed in males at FHR-SZ in the present study. Future studies integrating longitudinal neuroimaging and genomic data are needed to examine sex differences in the origins of volumetric alterations in children at FHR-SZ, including the contributions of placenta-related genetic risk to early alterations and genetic liability to SZ to subsequent developmental changes.

Contrary to our hypotheses, we did not observe FHR-related deviations in age-related changes of any brain measures. We had expected offspring at FHR-SZ and FHR-BP to exhibit steeper slopes since the present study covered a developmental period characterized by reductions in cortical thickness and volume (22), and both SZ and BP are characterized by thinner cortex and volumetric reductions (9,10). However, brain volume, cortical volume, and cortical thickness decreased over time to a similar extent across groups. Further, we did not observe group differences in cortical thickness at either time point, suggesting that differences in cortical thickness may emerge later in adolescence or closer to disorder onset.

Despite substantial clinical burden among FHR groups (e.g., (32)), neither Axis-I diagnosis nor PE moderated volumetric reductions in males at FHR-SZ. There were also no group-by-Axis-I or group-by-PE interactions. These findings indicate that the observed volumetric reductions do not reflect clinical symptomatology. However, the presence of PE was independently associated with reduced brain and cortical volume in males, above and beyond FHR group effects. These effects were primarily driven by individuals with persistent PE. This pattern was consistent with volumetric reductions in FHR-SZ males, suggesting overlapping volumetric alterations associated with FHR-SZ and PE in males. Previous work has found that among FHR-SZ and FHR-BP offspring aged 6-17, those who later develop psychotic symptoms have smaller baseline gray matter volume (8). Together, these findings suggest that reduced brain volume may reflect an underlying neurodevelopmental vulnerability that precedes the emergence of PE. Lastly, we observed increased brain and cortical volumes in FHR-BP females at ages 11-12 and 15-16. Although these effects did not survive FDR correction and must be interpreted cautiously, they are consistent with baseline findings (23) and thus appear to persist over time from early to mid-adolescence. These effects remained after controlling for ICV, suggesting that they may reflect structural deviations occurring during late childhood or adolescence. However, previous findings of increased ICV in first-degree relatives of BP patients (36, 37) and in individuals at genetic risk for BP (38) suggest early neurodevelopmental vulnerabilities.

Strengths and limitations

The VIA Study's large sample size provides sufficient statistical power to detect FHR-related and sex-specific alterations. Including only same-aged offspring limits age-related heterogeneity, increasing statistical power to detect morphometric brain changes between precise age bands.

Another major strength of the present study is that it covers the important developmental period from early to mid-adolescence. However, having only two time points limits the modeling of morphometric changes to slopes. The FHR-BP group was also relatively small compared to FHR-SZ and PBC groups, likely underestimating effects of FHR-BP. FHR-SZ participants included in the analyses were less impaired (e.g., lower internalizing problems, higher general functioning, and higher caregiver level of functioning) than those not included, likely underestimating FHR-SZ effects. The PE subgroups were relatively small (e.g., "Persistent PE", n=19) and these findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously and require replication in future work.

Conclusions

Our results reveal global and cortical volumetric reductions in males at FHR-SZ relative to males at FHR-BP and PBC, as well as in males reporting PE, persisting from early to mid-adolescence. These volumetric reductions were widespread across the cortex and were largely accounted for by reduced ICV, supporting the hypothesis that volumetric differences associated with FHR-SZ risk may originate early in brain development, and highlighting sex-specific vulnerabilities. Together, our findings highlight sex-specific, early neurodevelopmental pathways associated with FHR-SZ and PE, underscoring the importance of early developmental windows for identifying neurobiological markers of FHR-SZ and FHR-BP prior to potential SZ and BP onset.
