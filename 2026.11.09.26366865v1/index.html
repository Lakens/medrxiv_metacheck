Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Summary

Two otherwise healthy patients from Colombia, now aged 30 and 38 years, developed pulmonary tuberculosis due to inherited, complete TNF deficiency at the ages of 19 and 18 years. We report here a previously healthy Iranian patient with inherited complete TNF deficiency who was diagnosed with both pulmonary and extrapulmonary TB between 10 and 15 years of age. The homozygous TNF variant (c.673C>T) introduces a premature stop codon (TNF p.Q225*) resulting in losses of expression and function. Despite normal myeloid and lymphoid subset development, the patient's primary leukocytes do not secrete detectable amounts of TNF. As in the TNFdeficient Colombian patients, the respiratory burst of GM-CSF-derived macrophages is impaired.

Our findings expand the spectrum of TB phenotypes in patients with TNF deficiency and confirm that TNF is essential for immunity to M. tuberculosis in humans but apparently redundant against many other pathogens, including weakly virulent mycobacteria.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is most frequently triggered by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M. tb) 1 . A quarter of the world's population is latently infected with M. tb 1 . In 5-10% of cases, infection progresses to active TB, which remains the leading infectious cause of death worldwide 1 . Discoveries of the genetic etiologies underlying Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease (MSMD), characterized by susceptibility to weakly virulent mycobacteria and the Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) vaccine (an attenuated live strain of Mycobacterium bovis) 2 , were instrumental in demonstrating that TB can also be a monogenic disorder in some patients 3, 4 . Indeed, genetic defects disrupting interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) immunity have been reported in patients with TB.

Biallelic genotypes involving rare variants of TNF 5 , LY9 6 , ITK 7 , IL12RB1 8 , IL23R 9 , SPPL2A 10 and 11 and the common variants IL23R R381Q 9 and TYK2 P1104A 12 have all been reported to underlie TB. Human genetics studies have clearly shown that the protection against TB provided by host immunity is dependent on an intact IFN-γ circuit, including the molecules inducing IFN- and those induced by IFN-, to eliminate the mycobacteria within macrophages. Tumor necrosis factor (TNF) is a pro-inflammatory cytokine produced by macrophages in response to IFN-γ; it drives inflammation, promotes cell death and activates the respiratory burst mediated by the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) during infection 5, 13 . IFN-γ can therefore drive ROS production both directly and indirectly, by upregulating the transcription of the NADPH oxidase components involved in the respiratory burst 14 and inducing the production of TNF 15.

In this context, we previously described two patients with TB from the same Colombian family with inherited, complete TNF deficiency 5 . TNF has been implicated in host defense against multiple pathogens in mice 16 , but these two patients presented with recurrent pulmonary TB, despite broad pathogen exposure, including BCG vaccination. They were homozygous for a private TNF variant underlying complete TNF loss-of-function (LOF) 5 . They developed TB at the ages of 19 and 18 years. They are now 30 and 38 years of age, respectively, in complete remission, and healthy. TNF deficiency weakened antimycobacterial defense at the cellular level, by impairing the respiratory burst in GM-CSF-matured and alveolar macrophages 5 , but not in neutrophils. This observation is consistent with rare CYBB variants selectively impairing ROS production in macrophages and leading to MSMD, a phenotype largely restricted to mycobacterial disease [17] [18] [19] . By contrast, patients with chronic granulomatous disease (CGD) have a broad clinical phenotype due to the impairment of ROS production in all phagocytes 20 . We searched for new patients with autosomal recessive (AR) TNF deficiency, to expand our allelic, clinical and cellular understanding of TNF deficiency.

Results

Case presentation

We studied a female patient (P) born to consanguineous parents from Iran (Figure 1A). She was diagnosed with miliary pulmonary TB (between 10-15 years of age) with dissemination to the left pelvic bone, presenting with fever, fatigue, muscle pain, and an unexplained loss of about 3 kg of body weight. Acid-fast microscopy on a sputum sample revealed the presence of M. tb., and a tuberculin purified protein derivative (PPD) skin test with 5 tuberculin units resulted in an induration of 25 mm. These tests were also performed on the patient's parents and sibling, yielding negative results. CT scans of the patient's lungs were indicative of miliary TB (Figure 1B). At the time of diagnosis, immunological profiling showed the patient to have essentially normal proportions of leukocytes. The patient had a leukocyte count of 8800/mm 3 , with the proportions of neutrophils (36%), eosinophils (5.3%), and lymphocytes (45%) all within the normal range. The 102 proportion of monocytes was slightly high, at 12%. Acute-phase reactant levels were high, with a 103 C-reactive protein (CRP) concentration of 63 mg/liter. The patient received a three-month course 104 of anti-TB treatment with a five-drug regimen consisting of isoniazid, rifampin, ethambutol, 105 levofloxacin, and clarithromycin, followed by nine additional months of treatment with isoniazid 106 and rifampin, leading to complete recovery.

TNF p.Q225* confers a complete functional deficiency in an overexpression system

We assessed the impact of the variant on the production of the corresponding mRNA and protein in vitro by generating cDNA constructs for the expression of TNF p.Q225* in HEK293T cells. As controls, we included the previously described TNF p.Y163H and TNF p.A221R variants 22 (encoding proteins that do not bind TNFR1 and TNFR2), the TNF p.V77* variant (which cannot be cleaved to generate a soluble fragment 5 ), and the previously reported TNF p.P64Lfs*13 variant 5 . In a transient overexpression system in HEK293T cells, the mRNA encoding TNF p.Q225* was present in similar amounts to the mRNAs encoding WT TNF and p.P64Lfs*13 (Figure 2A). However, on immunoblot analysis, the TNF p.Q225* protein was undetectable in both the cell lysate and supernatants harvested from cells transfected with TNF p.Q225* (Figure 2B). As expected, p.V77* and p.P64Lfs*13 were also undetectable, and p.Y163H and p.A221R were detected but present in smaller amounts than WT TNF. Importantly, the epitope of the anti-TNF antibody used in these analyses occurs upstream from the p.Q225* variant. These results suggest that the patient's variant results in a loss of TNF. We then investigated the functional impact of TNF p.Q225* in an NF-κB luciferase assay on HEK293T cells. Soluble TNF was generated by transiently transfecting HEK293T cells with the WT and mutant TNF alleles, or with empty vector (EV) used as a negative control. The supernatant was harvested 24 h later. HEK293T cells transiently transfected with NF-κB-Firefly and Renilla luciferases (as a transfection control) were then stimulated with these supernatants, or with commercially available recombinant human TNF, overnight.

TNF-induced NF-κB activity was detected in cells simulated with WT TNF but was absent from cells stimulated with TNF p.Q225* (Figure 2C), confirming that TNF p.Q225* was, indeed, a loss-of-function (LOF) allele.

The patient's leukocytes lack functional TNF

We assessed the production and function of TNF p.Q225* in primary patient leukocytes directly, by isolating peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from the whole blood of the patient and two healthy donors as controls. Flow cytometry staining for TNF on phorbol 12-myristate 13acetate (PMA)/ionomycin-stimulated PBMCs detected intracellular TNF in the control cells but not in the cells of the patient (Figure 2D). Determinations of soluble TNF in the supernatant of PMA/ionomycin-stimulated cells showed an absence of TNF secretion by the patient's cells, whereas normal amounts of IL-6 were detected (Figure 2E). We then assessed ROS production in GM-CSF-matured monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs) in a luminol assay. In this assay, CYBB -/Y MDMs were used as a control for defective ROS production. ROS production in response to stimulation with PMA was impaired in patient-derived GM-CSF MDMs relative to those derived from a healthy control (Figure 2F). Importantly, a dihydrorhodamine (DHR) test performed on whole blood from the patient revealed no change in ROS production in neutrophils (data not shown). Thus, the cells of the patient did not produce or secrete functional TNF and ROS production was impaired in her GM-CSF-derived macrophages, accounting for her susceptibility to TB.

Normal leukocyte development

We then investigated the effect of TNF deficiency on leukocyte development in this patient.

Complete blood-cell counts were normal (data not shown). Five years post-TB recovery, we obtained whole blood from the patient and used mass cytometry (CyTOF®: Cytometry by Time-Of-Flight) to investigate the various PBMC subsets of the patient, comparing them with those of healthy controls matched with the patient for age and sex. The patient had slightly lower proportions and absolute counts of total and naïve CD4 + T cells, but higher proportions (but not counts) of effector memory and effector memory re-expressing CD45RA (TEMRA) CD4 + T cells (Figure 3A). She had similar proportions of central memory CD4 + T cells to the controls (Figure 3A). The frequencies of γδ T cells were unaffected by TNF deficiency (Figure 3B), whereas the frequencies and counts of mucosal-associated invariant T (MAIT) cells were higher than in controls (Figure 3C). The patient had similar proportions of CD8 + T cells, naive, central memory and effector memory subsets, and higher proportions, but not absolute counts, of TEMRA CD8 + T cells (Figure 3D). The proportions of the T helper, natural killer (NK) cell, B-cell, monocyte and dendritic-cell (DC) subsets were all within the normal range (Figure 3E-I). Overall, these data suggest that complete TNF deficiency did not alter the development of the major myeloid and lymphoid subsets in the blood.

Discussion

We report a new patient with inherited TNF deficiency as a genetic etiology of TB, confirming the importance of TNF as a non-redundant component of human immunity to M. tb. The defects observed in this patient are consistent with our findings for other TNF-deficient patients 5 , in whom impaired TNF signaling leads to defective ROS production in GM-CSF-matured MDMs ex vivo, with broader myeloid and lymphoid differentiation remaining intact. This dissociation between the preservation of hematopoietic development and selective effector dysfunction highlights a specialized role for TNF in macrophage antimicrobial programming rather than in immune lineage specification. Collectively, these data support a mechanistic model in which TNF is crucial for the optimization of macrophage bactericidal activity via ROS-dependent pathways to provide essential protection against M. tb infection. Our findings for this patient extend the genetic and clinical field of TNF deficiency-associated TB to new geographic and ethnic backgrounds, adding an Iranian individual to the previously reported Colombian cases, thereby increasing the generalizability of TNF deficiency as a monogenic cause of TB. A highly consistent immunological phenotype was observed in all the reported patients: preserved resistance to the agent used for BCG vaccination and to a range of other common intracellular pathogens, including environmental and atypical mycobacteria, but with selective susceptibility to M. tb. This pattern suggests that TNF is not broadly required for host defense against most pathogens, instead playing a more specific role in optimizing antimycobacterial effector mechanisms, particularly those linked to ROS generation in GM-CSF-matured macrophages. Unlike the previously reported Colombian patients, who had primarily pulmonary disease, this patient had disseminated TB, with lesions in her left pelvic bone.

TB is the most lethal human infectious disease worldwide 1 , reflecting the ability of M. tb to exploit vulnerabilities in host immunity. It is, therefore, unsurprising that any impairment of ROS production by TNF deficiency, which is crucial for macrophage function, renders the host susceptible to severe TB. Conversely, it is surprising that TNF deficiency results in a selective susceptibility to TB disease and not to other pathogens, including weakly virulent mycobacteria.

The data presented here and elsewhere show that the impairment of ROS production in TNFdeficient macrophages is not as severe as that observed in the cells of CGD patients (those with gp91 phox CYBB deficiency, for example) 17, 20 . It is possible that the 'milder' impairment in ROS production allows the host to maintain immunity to bacteria other than M. tb. Materials and methods

Human participants

Both the patient and her living relatives studied here are alive and followed up in Iran. Written informed consent was obtained from all family members tested. Travel controls from Iran were provided. Experiments were performed in France, in accordance with local regulations. Wholeblood samples from the patient and her healthy relatives were collected into heparin-containing tubes.

Sanger and whole-exome sequencing (WES)

Genomic DNA extracted from heparin-treated whole-blood samples from the patient and her healthy relatives was used for WES and verification by Sanger sequencing. WES was performed with the SureSelect Human All Exon V6 kit from Agilent on an Illumina sequencing platform.

Homozygosity rates were estimated from genomic DNA, as previously described 30 . TNF exon 4 was amplified from genomic DNA with the DreamTaq DNA polymerase (#K1082; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Amplicons were then sequenced by the Sanger method with the Big Dye Terminator kit v3.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and capillary electrophoresis (#A30469; Applied Biosystems 3500xL system, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Expression vectors

The constructs encoding TNF in a pCMV expression vector have been described elsewhere 5 . TNF c.673C>T was generated by PCR mutagenesis. The pGL4-NF-κB firefly luciferase and pRL-TK Renilla luciferase constructs have been described elsewhere 5.

Cell lines

HEK293T cells (human kidney epithelial cells; RRID:CVCL_0063) were maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM; Life Technologies). The culture medium was supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS; Sigma), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (GIBCO). HEK293T cells were cultured at 37°C under an atmosphere containing 10% CO2 in a humidified incubator. Cells were routinely screened for mycoplasma.

Transient transfection

HEK293T were transfected with Xtreme Gene9 (Sigma), in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. The supernatant was harvested 24 h after transfection and used to stimulate cells in subsequent experiments and the cells were lysed for analysis.

Cell stimulation

Where appropriate, cells were treated with the following inhibitors or ligands. Supernatants containing soluble TNF were generated by the transient transfection of HEK293T cells and were harvested 24 h post transfection. Recombinant human TNF was used at concentrations of 0.3, 3 and 30 ng/mL for the times indicated. For the detection of TNF, PMA and ionomycin were used at final concentrations of 40 ng/mL and 10 -2 M, respectively, for 6 h. For the determination of intracellular TNF, GolgiPlug (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 1:1000) was added 1 h after the addition of PMA/ionomycin, and the sample was incubated for a further 5 h.

Luciferase assay

HEK293T cells were transiently cotransfected, in technical triplicates, with the NFBfirefly luciferase reporter construct and a constitutive Renilla luciferase construct in the presence of Xtreme Gene9, in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. We added supernatants containing soluble TNF to the cells 24 h post-transfection and incubated the cells for a further 24 h. Reporter activity was then measured with the Stop and Glo Reporter Assay System (Promega), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Firefly luciferase activity was normalized against

Renilla luciferase activity and is expressed as a fold-change relative to unstimulated cells (relative luminescence units; RLU). Luminescence was captured on a microplate reader (VICTOR Nivo™).

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from cells with the Quick-RNA MicroPrep spin column kit (Zymogen, R1050), according to the manufacturer's protocol. For cDNA synthesis, 10 ng of total RNA was used for random oligomer-primed reverse transcription with Superscript II Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen, 18064022). Quantitative RT-PCR was performed with the TaqMan Universal Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, 4305719) and Taqman probes for GUSB (4326320E) and TNF (Hs00174128_m1; Thermo Fisher Scientific). and Image Lab software.

Immunoblotting

Differentiation of monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs)

CD14 + cells were isolated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) with CD14 MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-050-201). GM-CSF-matured MDMs were generated with M1-Macrophage Generation Medium XF (PromoCell, C-28055).

ROS production assay

Superoxide production by monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs) was evaluated with the Superoxide Anion Assay Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, CS1000) according to the manufacturer's instructions. In brief, 3 × 10 4 MDMs were plated in a 96-well plate in the presence or absence of superoxide dismutase and stimulated with PMA (400 ng/mL). Luminescence was captured on a microplate reader (VICTOR Nivo™).

Detection of intracellular TNF

PBMCs were isolated and dispensed into a 96-well round-bottomed plate at a density of 2 × 10 5 cells per well. Cells were stimulated with PMA, ionomycin and GolgiPlug, as described above.

Validated cutoffs existed for 56 of the 200 antigens (30 cosmopolitan, 26 travel-associated); the remaining 144, including all influenza, hepatitis, SARS-CoV-2 and vaccine antigens, were expressed only as robust z scores (median/MAD) against the 81 unrelated samples run on the same plates, and were never called positive or negative. Antibody breadth was defined as the number of seropositive antigens among the 30 cosmopolitan antigens. Groups were compared by two-sided Mann-Whitney tests, unadjusted. Because breadth is strongly age-dependent in this cohort (Spearman ρ = +0.58, P = 0.007, ages 4-86 years), comparisons were repeated on residuals of a linear fit of breadth on age. Per-antigen comparisons used Fisher's exact test with Benjamini-Hochberg correction across the antigens tested. Analyses were performed in Python 3 (numpy, scipy, matplotlib). Quantification and statistical analysis Results from technical triplicates are presented as the mean and standard deviation (S.D.). The statistical significance of differences between two groups was assessed in unpaired t-tests. All graphical data were prepared and analyzed in GraphPad Prism (Version 9).
