Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Abstract

Background

Hemoglobinopathies are among the most prevalent hereditary diseases in the world. They comprise a group of inherited blood disorders affecting hemoglobin, the oxygen-carrying protein of red blood cells, and are broadly categorized into two main groups: structural hemoglobin variants, which alter the structure or functional properties of globin proteins, and thalassemia syndromes, characterized by reduced or absent globin chain synthesis [1, 2].

Among hemoglobinopathies, thalassemia syndromes constitute a substantial global health burden. A recent analysis of Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 data estimated that 1.31 million individuals are living with thalassemia [3]. The underlying carrier burden is considerably greater, with an estimated 5-20% of the global population carrying one or more α-thalassemia variants [4] and approximately 1.5% carrying a β-thalassemia-associated variant [5]. Although α-thalassemia is considerably more common, β-thalassemia accounts for a disproportionate burden of severe disease, with more than 40,000 affected births annually, nearly two-thirds of which represent transfusion-dependent thalassemia [3, 6]. The global distribution of thalassemia is characterized by marked regional differences in allele frequencies, with substantial differences observed even across relatively small geographic regions. These patterns have been largely shaped by historical malaria-driven balancing selection, which favored heterozygous carriers and contributed to the high prevalence of specific pathogenic globin variants in affected populations [1].

The αand β-globin genes are organized into two highly conserved, developmentally regulated gene clusters located on chromosome 16 (α-globin locus; Accession: NG_000006) and 11 (β-globin locus; Accession: NG_000007), respectively. The α-globin locus contains the highly sequence-similar HBA1 and HBA2, while the β-globin locus comprises HBB, alongside embryonic and fetal globin genes, all tightly coordinated by upstream regulatory locus control regions [7]. Pathogenic variants affecting these loci variously disrupt globin chain synthesis, protein stability, oxygen affinity, tetramer assembly, and erythrocyte survival, giving rise to the diverse clinical spectrum of hemoglobinopathies [8].

The molecular basis of thalassemia is highly heterogeneous and includes both single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and copy number variants (CNVs) affecting globin genes and their regulatory elements, with more than 2000 mutant alleles reported to date [9, 10]. Individuals normally carry four α-globin genes, one copy each of HBA1 and HBA2 on both chromosomes 16 (αα/αα), but only two β-globin genes, one HBB copy on each chromosome 11 (β/β). This difference contributes to the distinct genotype-phenotype relationships of αand β-thalassemia. In α-thalassemia, loss of one α-globin gene results in a clinically silent carrier state (-α/αα), whereas loss of two genes causes α-thalassemia trait (--/αα or -α/-α).

Inactivation of three α-globin genes leads to moderate-to-severe HbH disease (--/-α), while absence of all four genes (--/--) results in α-thalassemia major (hydrops fetalis), a condition that is typically lethal in utero or shortly after birth [4]. Conversely, β-thalassemia results from pathogenic variants affecting one or both HBB alleles that reduce or abolish β-globin synthesis primarily through defects in transcription, RNA processing, translation, or globin chain stability. Depending on the extent of residual β-globin production, alleles are traditionally classified as mild (β ++ ), moderate (β + ), or complete loss of function (β 0 ). Clinical severity is determined by the combination of the two inherited alleles, ranging from clinically asymptomatic carriers to severe transfusion-dependent disease with multisystem complications [11]. The extensive allelic heterogeneity, the involvement of two globin gene loci in disease manifestation, and the influence of modifier genes contribute substantially to the complexity of molecular diagnosis and clinical variant interpretation [12].

To standardize variant interpretation and minimize laboratory discordance, the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG/AMP) published consensus recommendations in 2015 for sequence variants in Mendelian disorders [13]. This framework utilizes a five-tier classification system comprising benign (B), likely benign (LB), variants of uncertain significance (VUS), likely pathogenic (LP) and pathogenic (P), derived from a semiquantitative integration of population, computational, functional, segregation, and allelic data. Individual evidence codes are assigned specific strengths, ranging from supporting to very strong (pathogenic) or stand-alone (benign).

While these guidelines provide a robust foundation, their broad design necessitates geneand disease-specific adaptations to account for disease-specific molecular and clinical characteristics. This is particularly relevant for the globin gene loci, where the application of population-based, functional, and case-level evidence must account for unique molecular mechanisms, genotype-phenotype relationships, and population-specific allele distributions.

Accordingly, the Clinical Genome Resource (ClinGen) [14] established the Variant Curation Expert Panel (VCEP) framework to support the development of gene-and disease-specific ACMG/AMP specifications via expert consensus and systematic evaluation. The ClinGen Hemoglobinopathy VCEP was formed as an international collaborative effort through the ITHANET portal [9, 10] and the Global Globin Network of the Human Variome Project [15] to develop standardized specifications for the interpretation of globin gene variants associated with hemoglobinopathies [16]. The present study reports the finalized ClinGen-approved ACMG/AMP specifications for the interpretation of SNVs in HBB and HBA2 related thalassemia. These specifications represent the first ClinGen-approved ACMG/AMP recommendations developed by the Hemoglobinopathy VCEP and correspond to the initial HBB and HBA2 gene-disease pairs formally evaluated under the ClinGen Gene Curation framework [17]. Developed through international expert consensus and informed by extensive pilot variant curation, these specifications provide a standardized framework for the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-recognized globin variant interpretation aimed at improving consistency across laboratories, resolving conflicting classifications, and reducing the burden of variants of uncertain significance in hemoglobinopathy diagnostics.

Methods

Figure 1 provides an overview of the development, pilot validation, and ClinGen approval pathway for the Hemoglobinopathy VCEP specifications. The methodology underpinning each step is described below.

Panel composition and scope

The Hemoglobinopathy VCEP (ClinGen affiliation ID 50052, https://www.clinicalgenome.org/affiliation/50052/) was established in December 2019 to develop gene-and disease-specific adaptations of the ACMG/AMP sequence variant interpretation framework [13] for hemoglobinopathies (Step 1). At its inception, the VCEP comprised 26 experts in hemoglobinopathies from 13 countries across Europe, Asia, Africa, and North/Central America, with expertise spanning clinical and laboratory genetics, molecular diagnostics, hematology, bioinformatics, database curation, population genetics, and translational research. A center in China joined with two members during the pilot testing of the draft specifications. All members disclosed potential conflicts of interest in accordance with ClinGen requirements. Rule specification process A core subgroup representing diverse expertise within the panel convened regularly to review the applicability, strength modifications, and disease-specific considerations of the ACMG/AMP criteria for SNVs in HBB (HGNC: 4827), HBA2 (HGNC: 4824), and HBA1 (HGNC: 4823) associated with hemoglobinopathies. Initial specifications were developed based on published evidence and expert review, followed by iterative refinement and consensus approval by the full VCEP through teleconferences and e-mail correspondence. Published recommendations and feedback from the ClinGen Sequence Variant Interpretation (SVI) Working Group, now part of the ClinGen Variant Classification Working Group (VCWG), were incorporated through multiple rounds of review and revision. Criteria deemed not applicable were excluded, whereas others underwent gene-or disease-specific adaptation. Computational evidence specifications were informed by an independent benchmarking study of in silico prediction tools across globin gene variant types [18]. These efforts culminated in ClinGen approval of the ACMG/AMP specifications for pilot testing in April 2021 (Step 2). The framework comprised 34 specified evidence codes, including criteria applied at different strengths according to the quantity and quality of available evidence. Criteria were combined according to the original ACMG/AMP framework to derive variant classifications [13]. The complete ClinGen-approved specifications are publicly available through the ClinGen Criteria Specification Registry (CSpec, https://cspec.genome.network), which is updated periodically. Readers should refer to the online registry for the most current versions of the specifications, including HBB and betathalassemia HBB/LCRB (GN170; MONDO:0013517), and HBA2 and HBA2-related alpha thalassemia spectrum (GN173; MONDO:0100562).

Pilot variant selection

A final pilot set of 20 HBB and 13 HBA2 variants with thalassemia-associated annotations in ClinVar [19] and ITHANET [9] was selected to validate the specified ACMG/AMP criteria.

Variants represented diverse molecular consequences, including promoter and 5′UTR variants affecting transcription; consensus and cryptic splice-site, polyadenylation signal, and 3′UTR variants affecting mRNA processing; initiation codon, nonsense, and antitermination variants affecting translation; missense variants; and other intronic variants.

Loss-of-function variants were intentionally limited because PVS1 alone may provide Very

Strong pathogenic evidence, while variants with uncertain classifications were included to assess criterion applicability in the presence of insufficient and/or conflicting evidence. The pilot set was thus designed to maximize assessment of ACMG/AMP criteria across diverse variant contexts and classification outcomes. Final HBB and HBA2 specifications During pilot testing and review, evolving ClinGen VCEP procedures incorporated genedisease validity assessments [21], standardized disease entity definitions [22], and lumping and splitting recommendations [23]. The specifications were aligned with these requirements without altering their underlying evidence logic. Results

Summary of criterion specifications

The Hemoglobinopathy VCEP developed specifications for 22 ACMG/AMP criteria, while six criteria were excluded from both gene-disease frameworks, either due to limited applicability in globin gene variant interpretation (PP2, PP4, BP1, BP3) or following recommendations from the ClinGen SVI Working Group (PP5, BP6) [24]. The resulting framework comprises 34 individual evidence specifications. For HBB-β-thalassemia, 14 criteria received geneand/or disease-specific modifications (PVS1, PS2, PS3, PS4, PM1, PM2, PM5, PP1, PP3, BA1, BS1, BS2, BS3, BP4), 8 were adapted through strength-level modifications (PVS1, PS3, PS4, PM2, PM3, PP1, BS2, BS3), and 7 followed the original ACMG/AMP recommendations unchanged (PS1, PM4, PM6, BS4, BP2, BP5, BP7). A nearly identical framework was developed for HBA2-α-thalassemia, differing only in two aspects: BP5 was considered not applicable, and an additional specification, PS1_Moderate, was introduced to account for amino acid substitutions previously established as pathogenic in the paralogous HBA1. A complete summary of the criteria specifications and their respective designations across both gene-disease pairs is provided in Table 1. The modified ACMG/AMP evidencecombining rules adopted by the Hemoglobinopathy VCEP are summarized in Table 2, including recognition of ≥2 independent Very Strong criteria (PVS1, PM3 and PS4 at Very Strong strength) as sufficient for a pathogenic classification.

Population data (BA1, BS1, PM2_Supporting) BA1, BS1: population frequency thresholds BA1 (benign stand-alone) and BS1 indicate that a variant is too frequent in the general population to be causative of disease. Disease-specific thresholds were derived using the Whiffin/Ware allele frequency framework and calculator [25], incorporating conservative assumptions regarding disease prevalence, penetrance, and genetic and allelic heterogeneity. For BA1, calculations assumed the highest reported disease prevalence (1 in 1,000 individuals) [26], penetrance values of 90% for HBB and 80% for HBA2 (to account for silent variants and, for HBA2, potential underdiagnosis of HbH disease), genetic heterogeneity of 100% for HBB and 75% for HBA2 (reflecting the estimated contribution of HBA2 to pathogenic α-thalassemia variants curated in the IthaGenes globin variant database [9]), and a maximum allelic contribution of 15% (i.e., assuming no single pathogenic variant accounts for more than 15% of disease alleles). These assumptions resulted in a BA1 threshold of ≥0.005 (0.5%). For BS1, a more stringent disease prevalence of 1 in 3,000 individuals and a maximum allelic contribution of 5% were assumed, yielding a threshold of ≥0.001 (0.1%). Consistent with SVI recommendations, both criteria additionally required the variant to be observed in at least five alleles within a reference dataset comprising a minimum of 2,000 alleles [27]. Population frequencies were assessed using the GroupMax Filtering Allele Frequency (FAF; 95% confidence interval) from gnomAD v4, which represents the highest filtering allele frequency among non-bottlenecked ancestry groups.

To avoid inappropriate application of population frequency criteria because of malaria-driven selection or founder effects, the VCEP established a population-frequency exclusion list using allele frequency data curated in IthaMaps, which compiles country-and region-specific frequencies for globin gene variants worldwide [9, 10]. Variants included in the exclusion list were exempt from BA1 and BS1 application and from the PM2_Supporting rarity requirement when evaluating PM3 (Supplementary Table 1).

PM2_Supporting: rarity in population databases

Consistent with SVI recommendations [28], the VCEP adopted PM2 at the Supporting strength level (PM2_Supporting) for variants that are absent or rare in population reference datasets. The allele frequency threshold was set at <0.0001 (0.01%), one order of magnitude below the BS1 threshold of ≥0.001 (0.1%) [29]. In accordance with the approved specifications, PM2_Supporting is applied using gnomAD v4 when a variant has a total allele frequency <0.0001 (0.01%), read depth ≥20, and passes gnomAD quality control filters.

Variant type and location (PVS1, PS1, PM1, PM4, PM5)

PVS1: null variants

Loss of function (LoF) is an established disease mechanism for thalassemia, supported by the absence of benign or likely benign null variants in HBB and HBA2 in ClinVar and by likelihood ratio analyses demonstrating a consistent association between null variants and pathogenicity. Consequently, PVS1 is applicable to sequence variants (SNVs and short indels) predicted to abolish normal gene function, including nonsense, frameshift, canonical ±1 and ±2 splice sites, and initiation codon variants. Gene-specific specifications were developed using the MANE Select transcripts NM_000518.5 (HBB) and NM_000517.6 (HBA2).

Because the molecular consequences of predicted LoF variants depend on transcript context, the VCEP developed gene-specific PVS1 decision trees for HBB and HBA2 to calibrate PVS1 strength based on nonsense-mediated decay (NMD), alternative splicing, and translation re-initiation (Figure 2). Both specifications follow SVI recommendations for NMD prediction, whereby premature termination codons located within the 3′-most exon or the 3′-most 50 nucleotides of the penultimate exon are not expected to trigger NMD [30].

For HBB, published functional studies demonstrating translation re-initiation and NMD escape informed refinement of the decision tree [31] [32] [33]. Premature termination codons occurring at or before p.Glu23 (up to c.69) were considered within the 5′ NMD-resistant region, those occurring between p.Val24 (c.70) and p.Ala87 (c.261) were considered NMDsensitive, and those occurring at or beyond p.Thr88 (from c.262 onward) were evaluated as NMD-resistant with consideration of the predicted production of truncated β-globin chains. In contrast, for HBA2, where the NMD boundaries remain less well characterized because of the predominance of deletional disease mechanisms and the limited number of experimentally studied truncating variants [34, 35], the standard SVI framework was adopted.

Accordingly, premature termination codons occurring before p.Leu84 (c.251) were considered NMD-sensitive, whereas those occurring at or beyond p.Leu84 (c.251), corresponding to the terminal 50 nucleotides of exon 2 and the entirety of exon 3, were predicted to escape NMD.

For both genes, canonical ±1 ±2 splice site variants and initiation codon variants were interpreted based on their predicted transcript consequences, including their expected effects on NMD. These gene-specific considerations informed the assignment of PVS1_Very

Strong, PVS1_Strong, and PVS1_Moderate evidence strengths within the VCEP framework.

PM4: protein length changing variants

PM4 was retained without modification and applied to in-frame deletions/insertions and stoploss variants that disrupt protein function. A notable example is the α-globin antitermination variant NM_000517.6:c.427T>C (TAA>CAA, Hb Constant Spring) in HBA2 exon 3 that accounts for the majority of non-deletional forms of α-thalassemia worldwide [4]. For HBA2, PM1 was applied to the polyadenylation signal sequence and residues directly involved in binding α-hemoglobin stabilizing protein (AHSP; α32R, α104H, α118F, and α120P), whose interaction is essential for α-globin stability, preventing precipitation of nascent α-globin chains before hemoglobin assembly [8]. Variants affecting these regions disrupt well-established mechanisms regulating globin production or stability. The resulting globin chain imbalance promotes accumulation and precipitation of unmatched globin chains, leading to oxidative membrane damage and ineffective erythropoiesis that characterizes disease pathogenesis in thalassemia syndromes.

PM5: Novel variant at a residue or nucleotide position with a previously established pathogenic variant PM5 was applied when a novel variant occurred at an amino acid residue where a different missense variant had previously been established as pathogenic. In thalassemia, this criterion was additionally extended to non-coding nucleotide positions where a different pathogenic substitution had previously been reported, reflecting the important contribution of regulatory and splicing variants to globin gene disease mechanisms. A caveat to PM5 application is that nucleotide substitutions may alter RNA splicing in addition to changing the encoded amino acid.

Therefore, PM5 is not applied when the pathogenicity of the comparator variant is attributable primarily to altered splicing rather than the affected amino acid residue or nucleotide position itself. Such evidence can be assessed independently under PP3/BP4.

PS1: Same amino acid change as a previously established pathogenic variant

PS1 was retained according to the original ACMG/AMP recommendations and applied when a variant resulted in the same amino acid substitution as a previously established pathogenic variant, irrespective of the underlying nucleotide change. For HBA2, an additional Moderatestrength application (PS1_Moderate) was specified when the same amino acid substitution had previously been established as pathogenic in the paralogous HBA1 gene. This modification was supported by the high degree of sequence and functional conservation between the two α-globin genes [36].

Computational data (PP3, BP4, BP7)

Computational evidence thresholds were informed by a comprehensive benchmarking study using 1,627 curated HBB, HBA2, and HBA1 variants and 31 in silico prediction tools.

Predictor performance was evaluated and calibrated within the ACMG/AMP Bayesian framework [28] to derive evidence-based thresholds for pathogenic and benign computational evidence. The resulting recommendations identified REVEL as the preferred predictor for missense variants, SpliceAI for splice prediction, CADD PHRED score for nonmissense variants, and GERP++ for conservation assessment, and formed the basis for the VCEP specifications of PP3, BP4, and BP7 described below. Further methodological details are provided elsewhere [18].

PP3: computational evidence supporting pathogenicity

PP3 was specified using variant type-specific thresholds. For missense variants, PP3 was applied when REVEL >0.8 or SpliceAI Δ score (DS) >0.3; if REVEL was unavailable, CADD PHRED >23.5 was used instead. For non-coding variants, synonymous variants, in-frame insertions/deletions, and stop-loss variants, PP3 was applied when CADD PHRED >12 or SpliceAI DS >0.3. PP3 was not applied to canonical ±1 and ±2 splice site variants or other variants evaluated under PVS1, to avoid double counting of the same evidence.

BP4: computational evidence supporting a benign effect

For missense variants, BP4 required REVEL <0.7 together with SpliceAI DS ≤0.3; if REVEL was unavailable, CADD PHRED ≤20 was substituted. For non-coding variants, synonymous variants, in-frame insertions/deletions, and stop-loss variants, BP4 required CADD PHRED ≤11 and SpliceAI DS ≤0.3. Splice prediction was applied to non-canonical splice-altering variants, including those predicted to create cryptic splice sites or activate nearby alternative splice sites.

BP7: synonymous variants with no predicted functional impact.

The VCEP adopted BP7 for synonymous variants predicted to have no impact on splicing (SpliceAI DS ≤0.3) and lacking evolutionary conservation (GERP++ <0). BP7 provides additional evidence that a synonymous variant has a low likelihood of being disease-causing and may be applied together with BP4 when the respective requirements for both criteria are met.

Case level/segregation data (PS2, PS4, PM3, PM6, PP1, BP2, BP5, BS2, BS4)

Case-level observations constitute a major source of evidence for variant interpretation, contributing to multiple ACMG/AMP criteria including PS2/PM6 (de novo occurrence with or without parental testing), PM3/BP2 (allelic observations in trans or in cis), PP1/BS4 (cosegregation in affected family members, or lack thereof), PS4/BS2 (variant observation in cases or controls), and BP5 (alternate locus observations). A distinctive feature of thalassemia is that heterozygous carriers are typically clinically asymptomatic yet exhibit characteristic hematological abnormalities and hemoglobin profiles detectable by routine laboratory testing [37]. Coupled with the long-standing implementation of carrier and newborn screening programs in many regions [38], this has enabled diagnostic laboratories to accumulate extensive case-level evidence for both common and rare globin variants.

Importantly, many rare variants are observed only in heterozygous carriers or compound heterozygous individuals and may otherwise remain difficult to classify, making such observations a particularly valuable source of evidence for variant interpretation. The VCEP therefore developed disease-specific specifications to systematically utilize this unique body of evidence, incorporating published observations, curated case-level genotype-phenotype data from IthaPhen [39], and anonymized unpublished data contributed by participating expert laboratories under the ClinGen framework for protected case-level data sharing while safeguarding sensitive genetic and personal health information.

PS4: allelic observations in the heterozygous state

The original ACMG/AMP framework permits application of PS4 when the prevalence of a variant is significantly increased in affected individuals compared with controls. However, conventional case-control studies are unavailable for most rare globin variants. To address this limitation, the VCEP developed a phenotype-based point system that quantifies evidence from unrelated heterozygous individuals exhibiting laboratory findings characteristic of thalassemia (Table 3), consistent with recommendations implemented by other VCEPs [40, 41] For HBB-β-thalassemia, the combination of reduced mean corpuscular volume (MCV <79 fL), reduced mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH <27 pg), and elevated HbA₂ (>3.5%) was considered highly specific for the carrier state and therefore assigned the highest per-case point value. In contrast, isolated microcytosis and hypochromia are less specific and receive proportionally lower scores. For HBA2-α-thalassemia, hematological findings overlap substantially with iron deficiency and other conditions; therefore, lower point values were assigned unless additional evidence, such as exclusion of iron deficiency or corroborating laboratory parameters, increased diagnostic specificity. Only unrelated individuals were counted, multiple phenotype categories were not combined for the same observation, and potential complex genotype interactions were considered during evidence evaluation.

PM3: detected in trans with a pathogenic variant

The VCEP adopted the ClinGen SVI recommendations for PM3 (v1.0), implementing the established quantitative framework in which cumulative observations of a variant in trans with a pathogenic or likely pathogenic allele determine the final evidence strength (i.e., Supporting, Moderate, Strong, Very Strong). Disease-specific specifications were introduced to define qualifying phenotypes and acceptable comparator variants for thalassemia.

Specifically, PM3 evidence was considered only for homozygous or compound heterozygous individuals exhibiting a phenotype more severe than the expected heterozygous carrier state based on hematological and/or clinical findings; when detailed laboratory data were unavailable, a well-established clinical diagnosis (e.g., β-thalassemia major or HbH disease) was accepted as evidence of affected status. To avoid circularity, the pathogenicity of the variant on the opposite allele was required to be established independently of the variant under assessment using VCEP classifications (CSpec) or published expert consensus recommendations [18]. In accordance with the SVI recommendations, both alleles were required to satisfy rarity requirements, homozygous observations were capped at a maximum cumulative contribution of 1.0 point, and repeated observations involving the same recurrent founder pathogenic allele were interpreted cautiously, as they may reflect founder allele frequency rather than independent evidence.

PP1/BS4: Segregation with disease or lack thereof

The original ACMG/AMP framework recognizes co-segregation with disease as evidence supporting pathogenicity and allows increasing evidence strength with increasing numbers of informative segregations but does not define quantitative thresholds. The Hemoglobinopathy VCEP therefore adopted the ClinGen SVI likelihood-based approach for autosomal recessive disorders, consistent with recommendations implemented by other VCEPs [42] [43] [44], applying PP1_Supporting, PP1_Moderate, and PP1_Strong at LOD scores of >0.9, >1.5, and >2.1, respectively (Table 4). These thresholds correspond to increasing statistical support that the variant segregates with disease within a family, whereas BS4 provides strong evidence against pathogenicity when convincing lack of co-segregation is demonstrated. Disease-specific specifications focused on ensuring that only informative segregations, as defined under the ClinGen framework [17], contributed to evidence assessment. Accordingly, the same criteria used to define affected status for PM3 were applied when evaluating segregation evidence. In addition, compound heterozygous individuals contributed to segregation analyses only when the two variants were confirmed to be in trans, and caution was exercised in families harboring additional potentially pathogenic variants or established genetic modifiers that could confound interpretation.

BS2: observed in unaffected individuals

The original ACMG/AMP framework applies BS2 when a variant is observed in healthy individuals despite full penetrance being expected at an early age. Recognizing the recessive nature of thalassemia, the Hemoglobinopathy VCEP adapted this criterion to observations in homozygous or compound heterozygous individuals who remain asymptomatic or exhibit only the expected heterozygous trait phenotype. A single informative observation met BS2_Supporting, whereas two or more independent observations met BS2.

Unaffected individuals were required to be well characterized clinically and hematologically, including a full blood count and hemoglobin characterization, to exclude subtle disease manifestations and identify additional variants or abnormal hemoglobin quantities. In compound heterozygous individuals, the variant on the opposite allele was required to be independently established as pathogenic, and alternative genetic explanations, including pathogenic variants in the corresponding globin genes (i.e., HBB or HBA1/HBA2, as appropriate) and established disease modifiers, were excluded. Finally, only individuals older than 2 years of age were considered informative, as persistence of fetal hemoglobin during early childhood may attenuate the expected phenotype.

BP2: observed in cis with a pathogenic variant

The VCEP retained the original ACMG/AMP definition of BP2, applying the criterion when the variant under assessment was observed in cis with an independently established pathogenic variant. The VCEP did not apply BP2 if the phenotype was more severe than expected for either variant in isolation or if the variant under assessment had only ever been reported in cis with a pathogenic variant, as its significance in isolation could not be established. Pathogenicity of the accompanying variant was required to be established using VCEP classifications (CSpec) or published expert consensus recommendations.

PS2/PM6: de novo occurrence

The VCEP retained the original ACMG/AMP definitions of PS2 and PM6, distinguishing confirmed de novo occurrences (PS2) from assumed de novo occurrences in which biological parentage was not verified (PM6). For hemoglobinopathies, these criteria were considered applicable in individuals with disease as well as those exhibiting a characteristic heterozygous trait phenotype (Table 3), provided there was no family history consistent with inheritance. Furthermore, PS2 was used only when no other established pathogenic variant was present that could explain the observed phenotype.

BP5: alternate molecular explanation

The VCEP applied and specified BP5 only for HBB-β-thalassemia. The criterion was met when the observed phenotype could be fully explained by an alternative molecular basis independent of the variant under assessment. Representative examples include βthalassemia intermedia resulting from co-inheritance of an α-globin locus duplication and a correspondingly aggravated imbalance of the α-globin/β-globin ratio [45], or β-thalassemialike phenotypes caused by pathogenic variants in SUPT5H [46]. In such circumstances, the alternative molecular diagnosis provides a more plausible explanation for the phenotype, supporting a benign interpretation of the variant under evaluation.

Functional data (PS3, BS3)

PS3_Supporting, BS3_Supporting: functional evidence

Functional studies provide direct evidence of the biological consequences of genetic variants and can therefore contribute to pathogenic or benign classification when they assess mechanisms relevant to disease pathogenesis. For thalassemia, the VCEP reviewed available functional assays and specified those that directly interrogate established disease mechanisms, including impaired globin gene expression, abnormal RNA splicing, defective globin chain synthesis, and structural or stability defects of variant hemoglobins. Although many of the approved assays are well established and routinely used in the diagnosis and characterization of hemoglobinopathies, the published literature does not consistently report the standardized validation controls and performance characteristics required to support higher evidence strengths under the ClinGen SVI framework [47]. Moreover, interpretation of some assay readouts, particularly transcript-based assays, may require consideration of the downstream molecular consequences of the observed effect. Accordingly, the VCEP specified these assays at the Supporting evidence level (PS3_Supporting and BS3_Supporting). Assay-specific recommendations are summarized in Supplementary Table 2.

Globin biosynthetic assays. Thalassemias are characterized by an imbalance in the normal coordinated synthesis of the αand β-globin chains (1:1 ratio) that make up the hemoglobin tetramer. Accordingly, assays that quantify globin chain synthesis and evaluate the α:β synthesis ratio, directly assess the primary molecular defect underlying these disorders and provide functional evidence of impaired globin production [48, 49].

Hemoglobin stability assays: Disruption of the normal structure of the hemoglobin molecule may reduce globin stability, resulting in intracellular precipitation and accelerated degradation of the affected globin chain [8]. The contribution of globin instability to thalassemia pathogenesis varies according to the underlying molecular defect. For example, elongated α-globin variants such as Hb Constant Spring (c.427T>C) and Hb Paksé (c.429A>T) in HBA2 produce unstable proteins that contribute directly to the thalassemic phenotype, whereas variants such as Hb E (c.79G>A) and Hb Knossos (c.82G>T) in HBB primarily act through aberrant splicing and reduced β-globin synthesis but also exhibit altered structural properties, including reduced stability [11, 50]. In both settings, reduced globin stability may decrease the effective pool of functional globin available for hemoglobin assembly and exacerbate α:β chain imbalance. The VCEP distinguishes these variants from classical unstable hemoglobinopathies, in which globin instability is the primary pathogenic mechanism leading to hemolytic anemia, and from dominant β-thalassemia caused by NMDescaping HBB variants that produce highly unstable truncated β-globin chains [51].

Accordingly, hemoglobin stability assays (e.g., heat stability or isopropanol precipitation tests) were considered relevant functional studies for demonstrating a variant-induced reduction in globin stability, when such an effect is consistent with the molecular consequence of the specific variant.

Hemoglobin electrophoresis and chromatographic quantification: Hemoglobin electrophoresis, capillary electrophoresis, and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) are routinely used to detect and characterize variant hemoglobins. Within the VCEP specifications, these assays were considered informative only when they demonstrated an abnormal quantity of the variant hemoglobin (e.g., reduced peak or abundance), consistent with impaired globin synthesis, stability, or expression. Detection of a variant hemoglobin based solely on altered electrophoretic mobility or chromatographic retention time was not considered sufficient functional evidence. Furthermore, measurements of hemoglobin fractions such as HbA 2 , HbF or Hb H were not used as PS3_Supporting evidence, as these findings are incorporated into phenotype-based criteria (e.g., PS4, PM3, and PP1) and would otherwise constitute double counting of the same evidence.

In vitro splicing assay: Aberrant RNA splicing is an established disease mechanism underlying many forms of αand β-thalassemia, with pathogenic variants disrupting normal pre-mRNA processing through canonical splice-site alterations or activation of cryptic splice sites [52]. Accordingly, in vitro splicing assays were considered relevant functional studies for variants with potential effects on RNA processing. These assays evaluate the splicing pattern generated by the variant, for example using minigene plasmid-based constructs or transcript analyses, and determine whether the variant produces aberrant splice products, including cryptic splice-site activation, partial intronic inclusion, or exon skipping [53, 54].

Detection of aberrant splice products, including in the presence of residual wild-type transcript, was considered evidence that the variant disrupts normal RNA processing, whereas exclusive detection of the expected wild-type transcript supported normal splicing.

Importantly, although detection of an abnormal transcript provides direct evidence that a variant disrupts RNA splicing, its downstream consequence should not be inferred from the splicing assay alone. Rather, interpretation should consider whether the aberrant transcript is predicted to undergo NMD, preserve the reading frame, or produce an altered protein product, as these features may influence the application of other ACMG/AMP criteria, including PVS1 and PM4 [47].

In vitro-cell based assay: Reporter assays and transfection studies in erythroid cell models (e.g., K562, HEL, or HUDEP-2 cells) were considered relevant functional studies when they directly evaluated the effect of a variant on globin gene expression. These assays typically involve transfection with constructs carrying mutant promoter or enhancer sequences linked to a reporter cassette, or expression of cloned mutant globin genes, followed by quantification of reporter activity or globin mRNA/protein levels relative to the corresponding wild-type construct. Because reduced globin expression is an established disease mechanism in thalassemia, experimentally demonstrated decreases in transcription or expression provide direct evidence of variant impact on gene function. The VCEP considered such assays appropriate when they specifically assessed the molecular consequence of the variant under investigation using suitable wild-type controls, biological replicates, and statistics. This specification also encompasses RNA studies performed in cells derived from heterozygous or homozygous individuals, where assessment of wild-type and aberrantly transcripts can provide direct evidence of variant effects on gene expression or RNA processing.

Pilot application of the VCEP specifications

To evaluate the applicability of the gene-/disease-specific criteria, the final specifications were piloted independently on 20 HBB and 13 HBA2 variants. Variants with established ClinVar classifications, primarily supported by a two-star review status, were selected to assess concordance with classifications derived using the VCEP specifications. The pilot set also included two VUS, one for each gene. The ClinVar and final VCEP classifications, criteria applied, and supporting evidence summaries for each pilot variant are provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Following application of the respective specifications, 16/20 (80%) HBB variants (Figure 3A) and 11/13 (84.6%) HBA2 variants (Figure 3B) were concordant with their existing ClinVar classifications at the exact or broader P/LP or B/LB level.

Overall, 27 of 33 variants (81.8%) were concordant. The two VUS included in the pilot remained unresolved. Four additional variants-one HBA2 variant classified as B, two HBB variants classified as LB, and one HBB variant classified as P/LP in ClinVar-were classified as VUS due to conflicting evidence identified during curation. Across both pilot sets, the final VCEP classifications comprised 17 P, two LP, six VUS, one LB, and seven B variants, with no reclassification opposing pathogenic and benign classification categories. In an exploratory analysis, classification using the Bayesian framework described by Tavtigian et al [28] was concordant with the VCEP classification for 32/33 (97.0%) variants. The only discordant result involved the HBB variant classified as P/LP in ClinVar but resolved as VUS by the VCEP; it was classified as P under the Bayesian framework, illustrating how quantitative integration of conflicting evidence may resolve classifications that remain uncertain under the categorical ACMG/AMP framework. The pilot sets collectively enabled application of a broad range of evidence criteria, spanning population data (BA1, BS1, PM2_Supporting), variant type and location (PVS1 at Strong and Very Strong strengths; PS1_Moderate; PM1; PM4; PM5), computational data (PP3, BP4), case-level/segregation data (PS4 at Supporting to Very Strong strengths; PM3 at Moderate to Very Strong strengths; BS2_Supporting; PP1 at Supporting to Strong strengths), and functional data (PS3_Supporting, BS3_Supporting) (Figure 3C). Overall, 20/24 pathogenic and 5/10 benign criterion-strength specifications were represented in the pilot, with representation across all evidence strength levels, including Stand-Alone evidence. Criteria not encountered largely reflected the molecular basis and recessive inheritance of betathalassemia HBB/LCRB and HBA2-related alpha thalassemia spectrum, including the rarity of de novo occurrence (PS2, PM6) and the predominance of variants that reduce or abolish globin chain synthesis rather than impact the globin amino sequence, limiting opportunities to apply criteria relevant to amino acid substitution (PS1) or synonymous variants (BP7) [11].

Other criteria required observations not represented in the selected pilot set, including lack of segregation (BS4; captured under PP1), in cis co-occurrence (BP2), or an alternative molecular basis for disease (BP5).

Discussion

These ClinGen-approved specifications represent an important milestone for the hemoglobinopathy community by systematically translating decades of accumulated molecular, clinical, and laboratory knowledge into disease-specific ACMG/AMP criteria [55, 56]. Rather than redefining existing knowledge, they codify well-established principles of hemoglobinopathy diagnosis into a transparent framework that can be applied consistently across laboratories. The high concordance observed during pilot curation, particularly for community-annotated pathogenic and benign variants, demonstrates that the specifications faithfully capture current understanding of globin variant pathogenicity while providing a rigorous approach for interpreting variants with conflicting or limited evidence. Importantly, uncertainty was largely retained where supporting evidence remained insufficient, emphasizing that the framework standardizes interpretation without implying an unwarranted level of certainty.

The need for such harmonization is becoming increasingly evident as diagnostic testing expands from targeted assays to multigene panels, exome sequencing, and whole-genome sequencing, leading to the identification of an ever-growing number of rare globin variants requiring clinical interpretation [57]. Despite HBB being one of the best-characterized Mendelian disease genes, ClinVar (accessed 14 July 2026) continues to contain a considerable number of variants with uncertain (~300) or conflicting (~100) classifications.

These observations illustrate that extensive biological knowledge alone does not ensure consistent variant interpretation and underscore the need for disease-specific specifications supported by expert review. Since the recognition of ClinGen by the FDA in 2018, ClinGen Variant Curation Expert Panel assertions have been recognized as valid scientific evidence for clinical test development and validation. Collectively, these developments highlight the importance of reliable, transparent, and harmonized variant interpretation for carrier screening, genetic counselling, diagnosis, prognosis, and the clinical management of hemoglobinopathies.

The specification process highlighted several challenges unique to hemoglobinopathies.

Perhaps the most notable was the interpretation of population frequency data. Unlike many rare Mendelian disorders, pathogenic globin variants are not necessarily rare in the general population because of the evolutionary history of these disorders, including the phenomenon of heterozygote advantage associated with historical malaria-driven balancing selection [58].

Consequently, population frequency alone cannot be interpreted as evidence against pathogenicity, particularly for variants enriched in specific ancestral populations. Accordingly, BA1, BS1, and PM2_Supporting required disease-specific specification to avoid excluding pathogenic variants on the basis of frequency alone, including specific exemptions for established pathogenic variants with elevated population frequencies [27].

Defining the appropriate disease context for variant interpretation presented another important challenge. Clinical severity in thalassemia is often determined by the combined effects of pathogenic variants within the globin gene loci together with established genetic modifiers, producing a continuous spectrum ranging from clinically silent carrier states to severe transfusion-dependent disease. In addition, the same globin gene may be associated with multiple gene-disease pairs that differ in inheritance pattern, molecular mechanism, and clinical presentation. For example, pathogenic variants in HBB may cause recessive βthalassemia, dominantly inherited β-thalassemia, or structural hemoglobin variants (e.g., Hb E (c.79G>A), Hb C (c.19G>A), and Hb S (c.20A>T)), with the resulting phenotype further influenced by variation at modifier loci, including HBA1 and HBA2. Alternative molecular causes, such as pathogenic variants in SUPT5H, may also produce β-thalassemia-like phenotypes. Conversely, pathogenic HBA2 sequence variants contribute not only to HBA2α-thalassemia but also to the broader α-thalassemia spectrum through interaction with variants in HBA1 or α-globin deletions. Consequently, variant interpretation required careful consideration of both the relevant gene-disease pair and the underlying disease mechanism rather than the gene alone. One of the most important conceptual adaptations concerned the role of phenotypic evidence in variant interpretation. Unlike many recessive genetic disorders, heterozygous carriers of thalassemia variants exhibit characteristic hematological abnormalities-including microcytosis, hypochromia, and, in β-thalassemia, elevated HbA 2 -that can provide valuable evidence for variant pathogenicity. The original ACMG/AMP framework applies PS4 to variants enriched in affected individuals relative to controls and considers highly specific phenotypes separately under PP4 [13]. This distinction is less appropriate for recessive forms of thalassemia, where heterozygous carriers are not simply "unaffected controls" and their phenotype may, for some variants, provide the only available case-level evidence for classification. Consequently, the carrier phenotype was incorporated directly into case-level evidence rather than being treated as separate supporting evidence. Accordingly, the Hemoglobinopathy VCEP incorporated rigorous phenotypic requirements into the graded application of PS4 while considering PP4 redundant, thereby avoiding double counting of the same evidence. Importantly, this data is generated routinely through carrier screening programs and diagnostic hematology services worldwide. Unlike many rare genetic disorders, however, much of this evidence remains unpublished because carriers are generally healthy and do not require long-term clinical follow-up. Diagnostic laboratories therefore represent a uniquely valuable source of genotype-phenotype evidence. Broader sharing of anonymized case-level observations through dedicated hemoglobinopathy resources such as IthaPhen Interpretation of functional evidence required careful distinction between assays that directly evaluate variant function and those that describe downstream disease phenotypes. For example, hemoglobin variant detection and quantification inform variant function, whereas red blood cell morphology reflects phenotypic consequence. Consistent with current ClinGen recommendations [15], most published functional studies were applied at Supporting strength because they lacked the validation required to justify stronger evidence levels.

Emerging multiplexed assays of variant effect (MAVEs) may help address these limitations; however, current datasets remain limited to selected regions of the globin loci [59], with substantial evidence gaps for most missense, intronic, and other non-coding variants.

The current specifications provide a robust, dynamic framework for variant interpretation, intended to incorporate future insights. Their structured, disease-specific rules and quantitative thresholds may also facilitate future computational implementation, including AIassisted evidence retrieval, annotation, and application of variant classification criteria, while retaining expert oversight for evidence assessment and final classification [60]. Continued sharing and discussion of high-quality evidence from diagnostic laboratories, together with sustained international Expert Panel curation, will be essential for resolving remaining variants of uncertain significance, refining evidence thresholds, and expanding these recommendations to additional hemoglobinopathy gene-disease pairs. As active recommendations are maintained through the CSpec registry, the Hemoglobinopathy VCEP specifications will continue to evolve as new evidence emerges, ensuring that variant interpretation reflects current scientific and clinical knowledge.

Conclusions

The ClinGen Hemoglobinopathy VCEP has established the first ClinGen-approved diseasespecific ACMG/AMP specifications for the interpretation of sequence variants associated with HBB-β-thalassemia and HBA2-α-thalassemia. By integrating expert consensus with systematic evaluation of disease-specific molecular, clinical, and laboratory evidence, these recommendations provide a robust and transparent framework for harmonized variant 725 interpretation across diagnostic laboratories. Beyond improving the consistency and 726 reproducibility of variant classification, this work establishes a foundation for extending 727 standardized variant interpretation across the broader spectrum of hemoglobinopathies, 728 ultimately supporting more accurate genetic diagnosis, counselling, and clinical care through 729 international collaboration and sustained expert curation. 730
