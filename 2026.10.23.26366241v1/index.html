How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

Abstract

A previously developed static compartment model informed influenza program decisions by estimating the direct impact of influenza vaccination. To improve the estimation of the burden averted by vaccination, we extended this model to consider multiple influenza seasons, allow natural and vaccine-induced immunity to wane over time allow influenza vaccine effectiveness (VE) to differ by hospitalized versus non-hospitalized influenza-associated illness, and measure deaths averted. For model demonstration, we created a synthetic Northern Hemisphere-like and year-round influenza activity dataset spanning four seasons among older adults (>65 years) and assumed VE against symptomatic illness of 37% (17%-53%) and VE against hospitalization of 45% (28%-59%). Under Northern Hemisphere-like activity and an existing vaccination program with an average annual vaccine coverage of 33%, vaccination averted 15.1% (95% UI: 12.2%-16.3%) of influenza-associated illnesses across four seasons; the annual prevented fraction for hospitalized deaths ranged from 14.4% (95% UI: 11.7%-16.8%) to 18.5% (95% UI: 14.9%-21.5%). Under a hypothetical year-round vaccination program with an annual coverage of 20%, vaccination averted 4.9% (95% UI: 3.8%-5.4%) of influenza-associated illnesses across four seasons; the annual prevented fraction for hospitalized deaths ranged from 5.5% (95% UI: 4.3%-6.5%) and 5.8% (95% UI: 4.8%-6.6%). These model extensions allow estimation of vaccine-averted burden across multiple seasons and diverse vaccination program settings, providing information to support influenza vaccination decision-making.

INTRODUCTION

Influenza is a global public health threat, with seasonal epidemics resulting in substantial impacts on health, the economy, and society. An estimated 291,000 to 645,000 influenza-associated respiratory deaths occur globally each year [1]. Seasonal influenza vaccination is an effective method of preventing influenza-associated illnesses and its serious complications such as hospitalization and death [2, 3]. Modeling studies can provide insights into the benefits of vaccination by quantifying the public health value of influenza vaccination programs. Such analyses have suggested that substantial influenza burden can be averted through vaccination in target populations, such as young children and pregnant women [4] [5] [6]. These estimates inform plausible and effective vaccination strategies, including timing of vaccination campaigns and deployment strategies, and for evaluating both existing and hypothetical vaccination programs across subpopulations of interest and time [7]. In turn, they provide public health officials with quantitative estimates to inform decisions about whether to introduce vaccination programs and how to implement to ensure timely protection as influenza activity increases. However, generating such estimates can be challenging in settings without access to specialized infectious disease modeling expertise. Approaches that can be implemented by national public health programs using locally available data may therefore be particularly valuable for translating surveillance data into evidence for vaccination policy and program decisions.

For more than a decade, the United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (U.S. CDC) has used a static compartmental model [7] to estimate the direct impact of annual influenza vaccination [8] [9] [10] [11]. In 2018, Tokars, et al., evaluated this static compartmental model and further refined its implementation to omit an immune-lag adjustment to generate more accurate estimates of influenza cases averted by vaccination when working with monthly data [12]. To expand its use, in 2024 the U.S. CDC collaborated with the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) to develop a point-and-click Microsoft (MS) Excel tool based on the model [13], with a background chapter in the WHO Burden of Disease manual describing its methodology [14]. This simple, transparent, and user-oriented tool enables Ministry of Health officials and other public health practitioners to generate and interpret country-specific estimates of vaccination impact using their own surveillance and vaccination program data, without requiring specialized expertise in transmission modeling. This allows countries to directly evaluate and communicate the impact of their vaccination programs and generate evidence to inform decisions about program introduction, adjustment, or expansion. It has been used to estimate the burden of disease averted by influenza vaccination in several countries across the South American region [4, 5].

Since then, many countries have improved their estimates for key parameters in the model (e.g., influenza-associated disease burden, vaccination coverage, vaccine effectiveness), highlighting growing demand for estimates of the burden averted by influenza vaccination. Previous versions of the static model were primarily designed for evaluating vaccination during a single influenza season in settings with seasonal, single-peak influenza circulation.

We addressed some of the limitations of this initial model in an updated framework that can be applied to a wider range of epidemiologic and programmatic settings by incorporating waning immunity for use under a CC0 license. METHODS

Overview of model framework

Kostova, et al., and Tokars, et al. established a framework to estimate the direct impact of influenza vaccination on influenza-associated outcomes averted [7, 12]. The model operated on a singleseason basis; for multi-year analyses, it modeled each season separately. Under an all-or-none vaccination assumption, vaccine effectiveness (VE) against symptomatic illness is used to partition the vaccinated population into two distinct groups: effectively vaccinated and not effectively vaccinated. These classifications refer to modeled protection against symptomatic illness, not to how the vaccine was administered. Effectively vaccinated individuals were conferred full protection immediately postvaccination, but individuals that were not effectively vaccinated followed the same pathways as the nonvaccinated susceptible population and shared the same risk of infection. The model assumed that individuals were immune for the remainder of the season once infected or vaccinated, with no waning of immunity within the modeled period. We refer to this baseline model as the single-season, no-waning model throughout the paper.

We built our model extension upon this framework (Figure 1). The initial target population was assumed to be non-vaccinated and susceptible. Susceptible individuals who became infected experienced either non-hospitalized illness (i.e., symptomatic cases without hospitalization) or hospitalized illness, and a fraction of the influenza illnesses resulted in influenza-associated death. Nonhospitalized and hospitalized individuals returned to the non-vaccinated susceptible population as immunity waned. Each influenza-associated death was offset by the entry of one new susceptible individual to keep the target population size fixed. We considered this simplification reasonable because influenza-associated deaths were rare relative to the overall target population and were therefore unlikely to materially affect model estimates.

Each month, a proportion of the target population was vaccinated based on monthly vaccine coverage rates. We assumed that vaccination occurred at random, such that the likelihood of being vaccinated was independent of an individual's exposure to, or susceptibility to, infection. This approach reflects how programs measure vaccine coverage in practice, where the denominator was the total population and ensured that the cumulative number of individuals vaccinated by the end of the vaccination campaign matched reported coverage. Among those vaccinated, effectively vaccinated individuals were assumed to be fully protected against symptomatic illness beginning in the month of vaccination. Effectively vaccinated individuals gradually returned to the non-vaccinated susceptible population as vaccine-induced immunity waned. To reflect this, individuals who were not effectively vaccinated shared the same risk of experiencing non-hospitalized illness as unvaccinated individuals but a lower risk of hospitalized illness. The model therefore included separate susceptible compartments for unvaccinated individuals and those who were not effectively vaccinated.

The model used target group-specific monthly influenza-associated hospitalization rates derived from routine national surveillance data as the primary input (Table 1). The non-hospitalized illness-tohospitalization ratio and the proportion of hospitalizations resulting in death were applied to the monthly hospitalization rates to estimate non-hospitalized influenza illness and influenza-associated deaths, respectively. Medically attended illnesses were estimated by applying the proportion of influenza illnesses that were medically attended to the total monthly number of hospitalized and non-hospitalized illnesses. Expanded time horizon beyond 12-months with waning immunity

We extended the single-season, no-waning model beyond the 12-month time horizon of the baseline model; in the updated MS Excel tool, the model can be run for up to 48 months. Restricting the analysis to a single-season may fail to capture the full effects of vaccine protection, particularly when evaluating a year-round vaccine campaign in countries with year-round influenza circulation [19]. In these settings, infections and vaccinations that occurred later in the season could confer protection that is carried into the subsequent season. If we assumed that the entire target population was susceptible at the start of the analysis period, burden estimates in the first modeled year may be inflated. To address this issue, we introduced an initialization period (i.e., year-0), defined as the 12 months preceding the modeled period, which carried immunity forward into year 1. When year-0 specific inputs were unavailable, we approximated year-0 inputs using the seasons included in the analysis by default. We set hospitalizations in year 0 to the average across modeled seasons. We set year-0 vaccination coverage to 0% when the modeled period represented program initiation; when we modeled an existing vaccination program, we set year-0 coverage to the average coverage across the modeled seasons. We specified VE and other model inputs in year-0 using average values over the seasons included. When available, year-0 specific inputs were used in place of the default values.

Extending the time horizon beyond 12 months required specifying assumptions about the duration of immunity. Vaccine-induced immunity declined gradually with time elapsed since vaccination. As the protective effect of vaccination wanes, individuals who were initially effectively vaccinated gradually return to the susceptible population. Effectively vaccinated individuals were tracked by month of vaccination such that each monthly vaccination cohort followed its own waning trajectory based on time since vaccination. We parameterized waning VE as a function of time since vaccination using the cubic function proposed by Ferdinands, et al., which approximated exponential decay (Equation 1) [15]. Under this specification, VE declined to 0% within approximately 8 months after vaccination, with the exact timing dependent on the initial VE [19]. Any calculated VE values below 0% were set to 0%. For consistency with this waning profile and to adopt a conservative assumption [16], we assumed infectionacquired immunity persisted for the same 8-month period, after which individuals returned to the susceptible population.

Differential vaccine effectiveness for hospitalized versus non-hospitalized influenza illness

Influenza vaccination may offer varying levels of protection against different health outcomes. While receipt of the influenza vaccine may not completely prevent symptomatic infection, it may attenuate disease severity and reduce the likelihood of severe outcomes such as hospitalization [17, 18]. To capture this potential benefit while retaining the structure of the original model framework, effectively vaccinated individuals were assumed to be fully protected against symptomatic illness, whereas individuals who were not effectively vaccinated remained at risk of symptomatic illness but could still have a reduced risk of hospitalization. Estimation of averted deaths

Depending on data availability, we estimated deaths averted by vaccination for one or both outcomes: 1) in-hospital influenza deaths, and 2) all influenza-associated deaths. In-hospital influenza deaths were estimated from observed influenza-associated hospitalizations using a multiplier approach [20]. Specifically, the number of in-hospital deaths was estimated by multiplying hospitalized influenza illnesses by the proportion of influenza-associated hospitalizations that resulted in death. This approach assumes that influenza-associated hospitalizations were appropriately estimated and that the proportion of hospitalizations resulting in death was applicable to the target population during the modeled period.

When data were available to account for influenza-associated deaths occurring outside the hospital setting, estimated in-hospital deaths were extrapolated to all influenza-associated deaths rather than modeled as a separate compartmental pathway. This extrapolation used national vital records data by multiplying the estimated number of in-hospital deaths by the inverse of the proportion of influenzaassociated deaths that occurred in hospital among all influenza-coded deaths. This adjustment captured both individuals who were hospitalized at some point and those who were never hospitalized. This extrapolation assumes that the place-of-death multiplier was applicable to the modeled population and period that hospitalization surveillance and vital records data were sufficiently consistent.

Overview of model outputs

For existing vaccination programs, the model estimated the counterfactual burden of influenza disease by severity (e.g., non-hospitalized illnesses, medically attended illnesses, hospitalized illnesses, and deaths) in the absence of vaccination. For this counterfactual scenario, vaccination coverage was set to 0%, such that the entire population followed the non-vaccinated pathways. The model then calculated averted burden as the difference between the hypothetical burden without vaccination and the observed burden with vaccination in place. calculated averted burden as the difference between the observed burden without vaccination and the expected burden. We quantified uncertainty in model estimates using Monte Carlo simulation with 5,000 iterations. In each iteration, monthly hospitalizations were first summed to obtain annual hospitalized illnesses. Expected annual counts of non-hospitalized illnesses, medically attended illnesses, and deaths were then calculated by applying the corresponding multipliers to annual hospitalized illnesses. Annual counts for each outcome were sampled from Poisson distributions, and the outcome multipliers were recalculated from these sample counts and used in that iteration. Sampled annual hospitalized illnesses were then distributed across months according to the observed monthly distribution of hospitalizations. Similarly, the annual number of vaccinated individuals was sampled from a binomial distribution based on the target population size and annual vaccination coverage and then distributed across months according to the observed monthly distribution of vaccine coverage. VE estimates were sampled from a Beta-PERT distribution based on the point estimate and 95% confidence interval for each year or season. In addition to reporting averted burden as an absolute number, we calculated the prevented fraction, expressed as the proportion of averted outcomes to the potential outcomes in the absence of vaccination. We also reported the number needed to vaccinate (NNV) to avert an influenza-associated event, calculated as the total number vaccinated divided by the total number of cases averted. Scenario analyses

As with the single-season, no waning model, the extended model framework maintained evaluation of alternative vaccination scenarios to assess how different deployment strategies influence the estimated averted burden under the same underlying disease burden. Users could specify alternative monthly vaccination coverage while holding other model inputs constant. For existing vaccination programs, the estimated monthly hospitalization rates in the absence of vaccination were used as the underlying disease burden, and alternative vaccination coverage scenarios were evaluated as counterfactual programs. For hypothetical vaccination programs, the observed burden was based on no vaccination, and alternative scenarios were evaluated by changing only the monthly vaccination coverage inputs.

Synthetic dataset for model demonstration

To demonstrate use of the tool and illustrate model outputs, we generated two synthetic datasets, each covering a 48-month period. One dataset represented a setting with seasonal influenza activity typical in the Northern Hemisphere and was used to evaluate averted illness under an existing vaccination program with annual vaccine coverage of 28% to 37% distributed across October through January. The second dataset represented a setting with year-round influenza activity and no existing vaccination program. This dataset was used to evaluate averted illnesses that would occur under a hypothetical vaccination program with annual vaccine coverage of 20%, evenly distributed throughout the year. Throughout the modeled period, we assumed VE against symptomatic illness of 37% (17%-for use under a CC0 license. 53%) and VE against hospitalization of 45% (28%-59%) [21]. The target age group for both datasets was older adults, and hospitalization rates were drawn from global average estimates ranging from 57 to 164 per 100,000 population, with values selected within this range [22]. The non-hospitalized illness-tohospitalization ratio of 17.21 and the proportion of symptomatic illnesses that were medically attended (0.3969) were derived from U.S. influenza burden estimates over six seasons, from 2010-2011 season through 2015-2016 season [23]. The proportion of hospitalized illnesses resulting in death (0.0557) was derived from influenza hospitalizations and in-hospital deaths over three U.S. influenza seasons, from 2010-2011 through 2012-2013 [24]. We assumed a total target population of 600,000 individuals.

Model validation

To assess model validity, we estimated vaccine-averted illnesses, medical attended illnesses, hospitalizations, and deaths using data from the 2017-2018 and 2018-2019 U.S. influenza seasons and compared our estimates with previously published age-specific estimates. Analyses were conducted separately for five age groups: 6 months-4 years, 5-17 years, 18-49 years, 50-64 years, and ≥65 years. Monthly influenza-associated hospitalization rates were obtained from FluSurv-NET [25], adjusted for under-detection, and extrapolated to the U.S. population within each age group. Outcome multipliers were obtained from the same published analyses used for comparison. Monthly age-specific vaccination coverage was obtained from FluVaxView [26]. We used age-specific VE estimates for each season, using overall influenza VE rather than virus-specific VE.

RESULTS

We summarized the synthetic dataset inputs used for model demonstration (Table 1 and Supplementary Tables 1 2). Over the four modeled years, we based the synthetic Northern Hemisphere-like dataset on 2,868 influenza-associated hospitalizations, with activity starting around November and tapering off through March and April (Figure 2A). With existence of an influenza vaccine program, the estimated number of influenza-associated illnesses in the synthetic Northern Hemispherelike dataset ranged from 9,898 (95% UI: 9,569-10,591) to 17,872 (95% UI: 17,335-19,086) across the four seasons (Table 2). Vaccination averted a total of 9,572 (95% UI: 7,666-10,422) influenza-associated illnesses with an average annual vaccine coverage of 33%. Across the four modeled seasons, the estimated seasonal averted burden ranged from 6-10 hospitalized deaths, 99-207 hospitalized illnesses, 623-1,302 medically-attended illnesses, and 1,471-3,073 non-hospitalized illnesses. Across seasons, 12.5% (95% UI: 8.7%-14.9%) to 16.1% (95% UI: 11.2%-19.3%) of all influenza illnesses estimated in the for use under a CC0 license.

absence of vaccination were averted by vaccination. Estimates of the number needed to vaccinate to avert one influenza-associated illness varied by season, ranging from 62 (95% UI: 51-91) to 110 (95% UI: 90-159) persons.

The synthetic year-round influenza activity dataset included 2,580 influenza-associated hospitalizations in the absence of vaccination (Figure 3A). During four years, the estimated number of influenza-associated illnesses in the synthetic dataset with year-round influenza activity ranged from 6,885 (95% UI: 6,720-7,044) to 17,157 (95% UI: 16,902-17,408) per year in the absence of vaccination (Table 3). Under a hypothetical year-round vaccination program with an annual coverage of 20%, a total of 2,311 (95% UI: 1,788-2,530) influenza-associated illnesses were averted over four years. The estimated yearly averted burden ranged from 1-3 hospitalized deaths, 22-55 hospitalized illnesses, 136-339 medically-attended illnesses, and 321-800 non-hospitalized illnesses. Although the number of illnesses averted varied year-to-year, vaccination averted a similar proportion of influenza illnesses estimated in the absence of vaccination across years, between 4.8% (95% UI: 3.1%-5.9%) and 5% (95% UI: 3.4%-5.8%). Estimates of the number needed to vaccinate to avert one influenza-associated illness varied by year, ranging from 140 persons (95% UI: 121-201) to 350 persons (95% UI: 305-493). One of the key features of this tool is that it allows users to evaluate alternative vaccine campaign deployment strategies under the same underlying disease burden by varying the timing and monthly distribution of vaccine coverage. For example, using the synthetic Northern Hemisphere-like dataset, we evaluated vaccination beginning one month earlier or one month later than the baseline strategy (Figure 2B). Starting the vaccine campaign one month earlier resulted in 6.2% additional influenza-associated illnesses averted across all seasons. Using the synthetic year-round influenza activity dataset, we compared year-round vaccine deployment with seasonal campaigns timed to periods typical Southern and Northern Hemisphere vaccination program (Figure 3B-3C). The seasonal campaign starting in April resulted in 22.6% additional influenza-associated illnesses averted across all years. These examples illustrate how the tool can be used to quantify the impact of changes in vaccine delivery relative to influenza activity.

As part of model validation, averted burden estimates from the extended model were broadly comparable in magnitude to the previously published estimates (Figure 4 and Supplementary Tables 3 4 5 6). In the 2017-2018 season, point estimates under the waning VE assumption were consistently higher than the published estimates across age groups and outcomes, although their 95% uncertainty intervals overlapped. Estimates under the constant VE assumption were generally closer to the published estimates. In the 2018-2019 season, point estimates under both VE assumptions were within the 95% uncertainty intervals of the published estimates across age groups and outcomes. For the 6 months-4 years, 50-64 years, and ≥65 years age groups, the 95% uncertainty intervals under both VE assumptions were within the corresponding published uncertainty intervals.

A previously developed static compartment model has been helpful in informing influenza program decisions related to vaccine introduction, expansion, and timing. In this paper, we extended the model in several ways. Collectively, these extensions allow users to evaluate vaccination programs in settings where influenza circulation occurs throughout the year or over multiple years, reflect real-world understanding of waning immunity over time, and variable performance of vaccine by disease severity. We also added the ability to estimate the number of influenza-associated deaths averted, aligning model outputs with commonly reported burden estimates [27]. These adaptations, along with the ability to explore both existing and hypothetical alternate vaccine strategies, allow the model to better reflect how influenza circulates and how vaccination programs operate in real-world settings.

Importantly, these additional capabilities were incorporated while retaining a relatively simple and transparent model structure. The model is intended to provide a practical approach for national public health programs to generate estimates of the direct impact of vaccination using locally available data, without requiring specialized expertise in transmission modeling. Implementation in an accessible MS Excel tool allows users to examine model inputs and assumptions, modify vaccination program scenarios, and generate locally relevant estimates. Additionally, expressing results in terms of prevented fraction and the number needed to vaccinate complements estimates of averted burden and supports communication of findings, particularly since absolute averted burden is sensitive to the underlying influenza attack rate [7]. This combination of expanded analytic capabilities and accessibility enables countries to generate and interpret their own estimates to support vaccination program decisions.

The previous Excel tool implementing the single-season, no-waning model has been used to estimate averted illness in low-and middle-income countries [4, 5]. In parallel, countries have expanded their capacity to generate influenza burden estimates. Strengthened sentinel respiratory disease surveillance systems and standardized methods described in the WHO Burden of Disease manual have supported generation of national influenza burden estimates [28, 29], while PAHO's Network for the Evaluation of Vaccine Effectiveness in Latin America and the Caribbean-influenza (la Red para la Evaluación de Vacunas en Latino América y el Caribe-influenza [REVELAC-i]) consistently generates and publishes influenza vaccine effectiveness estimates for key target populations, providing critical inputs for the model [30] [31] [32]. By incorporating the model extensions described in this paper, national programs can bring these locally generated data together with an updated tool that better reflects changes in immunity and protection over time, addresses local program needs, and supports routine evaluation of influenza vaccine programs. In response to growing interest in such evaluations, PAHO hosted a workshop in Brazil in 2026 that trained 36 representatives from 13 countries on the use of the extended averted burden MS Excel tool, strengthening local analytic capacity [33].

In model validation to published U.S. estimates, estimates derived from the expanded model were consistently within the 95% uncertainty intervals of the published estimates, giving us confidence in the validity of this tool. The higher averted burden estimates under the waning VE assumption likely reflect, in part, the calibration of the waning curve, which results in an initial VE higher than the reported seasonal VE. During these seasons, most vaccination occurred before the main period of seasonal for use under a CC0 license.

influenza activity, consistent with a typical Northern Hemisphere vaccination campaign. As a result, periods of higher VE soon after vaccination overlapped with influenza circulation, resulting in greater estimated protection than under the constant VE assumption. Under the constant VE assumption, estimates were close to those from the U.S. estimates but narrower uncertainty intervals. This reflected differences in how uncertainty was sampled and in how influenza was modeled.

Our model is subject to several limitations. First, model estimates may be sensitive to input values because hospitalization rates from surveillance data were scaled to estimate target population burden. For example, influenza mortality burden derived from this hospitalization-based multiplier approach may underestimate the true burden if the multipliers do not fully capture under-ascertainment of influenza-associated deaths [34]. The more closely the multipliers reflect the health care access, careseeking behavior, surveillance practices, and other societal and contextual differences, the more accurate the estimates are likely to be. Despite this limitation, averted burden estimates may still provide useful approximation of direct vaccination impact.

Second, this model was designed as an extension of the single-season, no-waning model framework. As a result, we did not include a transmission rate, nor did we account for incubation, infectious, and recovery periods that reflect the natural progression of the disease, as is typical in dynamic compartmental models. Instead, the model used a monthly time step and incidence rates, providing a snapshot of the disease burden in each month. This represents an intentional tradeoff between model complexity and accessibility. Dynamic transmission models are better suited to questions requiring estimation of transmission dynamics, indirect effects, or interactions among population groups. In contrast, the static framework requires fewer assumptions and specialized inputs and can be implemented and interpreted by public health programs using routinely available data.

Third, we did not account for the indirect effects of vaccination, such as potential protection of unvaccinated individuals through herd immunity. Although indirect effects can contribute to the overall impact of influenza vaccination, their magnitude remains uncertain because data quantifying indirect protection are limited and may vary by transmission intensity and characteristics of the population [35]. The averted burden, prevented fraction, and number needed to vaccinate produced by the model reflect only the direct impact of vaccination. Nevertheless, direct impact is an important and readily interpretable measure of vaccination program benefit and can provide decision makers with locally relevant estimates of illnesses, hospitalizations, and deaths prevented through vaccination. As the estimates captured direct effects of influenza vaccination and the impact of vaccination is non-linear [36], the results represent minimum estimates of vaccine impact. Fourth, influenza vaccination may attenuate illness severity even when infection occurred [17]. However, a previous meta-analysis found that VE estimates from inpatient settings were not consistently higher than those from outpatient settings [37]. A U.S. study comparing VE in inpatient and outpatient networks reported higher inpatient VE in one season, but this pattern was not consistent across years [38]. Differences in clinical case definitions, enrollment criteria, and underlying patient characteristics may result in different patient populations across settings and could have contributed to these findings. When available evidence indicates that the VE against hospitalization is lower than that against for use under a CC0 license.

symptomatic illness, we assumed no additional reduction in hospitalization risk among individuals who were not effectively vaccinated.

Fifth, natural immunity was modeled as providing protection against influenza for eight months, after which individuals returned to the susceptible population without progressive waning. This assumption did not allow reinfection during the protected period and the model treated infectionderived immunity the same regardless of influenza types or subtypes. Some cohort studies suggested that reinfections with influenza viruses were possible within the same season [39] [40] [41]. Because circulating influenza types and subtypes can differ both within and between seasons, this simplification may overstate infection-derived protection. Also, post-infection seroprotection may persist for much of a season or longer [16]. If prior infection provided protection for longer than assumed in the model, estimates of vaccine-averted burden could be overestimated. Lastly, the model assumed a waning rate for vaccine-induced immunity derived from prior analyses of VE among older adults in the U.S [15]. Prior work suggested a broadly similar pattern of waning VE among children and older adults [42]. We also assumed that VE against severe and non-severe illnesses waned at the same rate, although these may differ in actuality. In addition, vaccine-induced protection that had not yet waned was carried across seasons without accounting for antigenic drift.

CONCLUSION

This work expands a previously developed model for estimating vaccine-averted influenza burden by allowing users to evaluate influenza vaccination programs across multiple seasons, account for waning immunity, consider differences in protection against influenza-associated severe and nonsevere outcomes, and estimate deaths averted. By better reflecting key features of influenza epidemiology and vaccination programs while retaining a transparent and accessible model structure, the extended model provides national public health programs with a practical approach to generate their own estimates of vaccine-averted burden using locally available data and to evaluate and communicate the impact of influenza vaccination strategies.
