To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 14 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. Title: An interpretable cell-centric representation of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma matches multiple-instance learning for clinical outcomes and leads to novel pathologist-driven histological biomarkers Authors and affiliations: Maxence Pelloux 1 , Catherine TL Tran 2 , Jumanah Baig 3 , Abdelhakim Khellaf 2 , Sally Lang Huang 2 , Olivier Tung Nguyen 2 , Maëlle Batardière 3 , Sarah-Slim Diwan 3, 4 , Zhiyuan Yang 5 , Jia Lin Li 2 , Vyky Vu 2 , Simon F Roy 6, 7 , Geneviève Soucy 2 , Bich Ngoc Nguyen 2 , Basile Tessier-Cloutier 4 , Mahdi S Hosseini 5 , David JHF Knapp 3, 8 , Séverine Valmary-Degano 9 , Vincent QH Trinh 2,3,10 . 1: Centre Hospitalier Universitaire Grenoble Alpes, Grenoble, France.

2: Centre Hospitalier de l'Université de Montréal, Montreal, Quebec, Canada.

ABSTRACT

Current state of the art computational pathology foundation models attain satisfying accuracy most general pathology tasks including detection of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) but fail to provide sufficient explainability, operating as "black boxes". Some interpretability effort has been made, with models providing attention maps that project back on the slide the models high attended areas that pushed for the given prediction result, without further naming or reasoning explanation which can limit pathologists trust toward the prediction. We developed C3PRO, a cellcentric framework attempting to provide deeper understanding and explainability, by using embeddings extracted using a pathology foundation model, from cell centered tiles containing both the cell and its close neighborhood, which were then clustered into recurrent morphological phenotypes. A spatial graph was additionally constructed on top of the back-projected vocabulary to account for recurrent motifs. The evaluation of the model performance involved a cohort of 809 patients (825 diagnostic biopsies) using nested cross validation. C3PRO was benchmarked against four different multiple instance learning frameworks and achieved on-par performance for diagnostic (0.958 AUC), survival (0.617) and resectability (0.696) prediction, while providing additional insights compared to the strongest baseline models. Each clustered phenotype was probed and extracted as a representative tile mosaic and annotated by two expert pathologists, constituting a phenotype vocabulary available for statistical analysis. These phenotypes were scored by pathologists with physical brightfield microscopy and replicated the independent statistical significance impact on overall survival prediction from diagnostic biopsies.

STATEMENT OF SIGNIFICANCE

Pathology foundation model multiple-instance-learning frameworks cannot be audited by the pathologists. We demonstrate that a representation constructed from identifiable cell phenotypes is on par with state-of-the-art models for diagnosis, prognosis, and resectability in pancreatic cancer. This approach also uncovers prognostic stromal states that expert observers cannot differentiate using light microscopy.

INTRODUCTION

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is infamously known as part of the most lethal solid malignancies, with a five year overall survival under 13% across all stages, 1 mostly explained by late symptomatic manifestation, aggressive clinical behavior and resistance to systemic therapy. 2 As for most malignant entities pathology plays a key three-step role the care of these patients: diagnosis on the often limited biopsy material, prognostic stratification (although very limited) to guide the intensity and timing of therapy, and participation in the assessment of resectability. Each of these steps reported to being subject to substantial inter-observer variability, whether in grading biopsies, assessment of precursor lesions or tumor regression grading on resection specimen after neoadjuvant treatment. 3 Some attempts to improve or provide additional input to prognostic prediction was made through deep-learning models built on conventional clinico-pathological parameters, but these rarely exceed a concordance index of 0.70. 4 Objective, reproducible and interpretable computational tools are needed.

Digital whole-slide imaging (WSI) has enabled the application of deep learning to pathology, at first via fully-supervised convolutional neural networks (CNN) that required substantial and costly expert annotation, 5 then through weakly supervised frameworks such as multiple-instance learning (MIL) operating at the slide level with no need for extensive pixel-level annotations. 6 These pipelines rely on pathology foundation models, pre trained on large datasets using self-supervision, such as UNI, 7 Virchow, 8 Prov-GigaPath 9 and H-Optimus-0, 10 which are transferable and now used to produce embeddings for a wide range of downstream tasks such as slide-level diagnostic and prognostic predictions.

In order to provide these slide level predictions, the information carried by the extracted tileembeddings representing a fraction of the overall tissue must be aggregated using different approaches such as attention-based MIL, 11 transformer architectures, 12 or state-space models. 13 Graph neural networks (GNN), that build graph dependencies on cellular or tile level representation encode tissue architecture and cellular connections explicitly, 14 which present as a good fit for cell-centered representations given that the composition and structure of the tumor microenvironment shape PDAC behavior. 15 Independently of the aggregation method, these models fail to provide clear explainability and predictions are difficult for pathologists to verify. A first solution serving as the main interpretive tool is the attention heatmap, which comes with limitations, as attention is an imperfect proxy for prediction drivers, 16 and do not or partially account for biological reasoning. Fundamentally, a heatmap signals without naming: showing high yield prediction areas without elaborating on their content. As an example a high attention region for the diagnosis of most malignancy could include neoplastic epithelium, desmoplastic stroma or an immune infiltrate, which all carry different implication. Furthermore, a model whose reasoning cannot be probed is difficult to audit for systematic errors or to integrate into practice especially in a field such as pathology where the responsibility relies on the practitioner and not the model, therefore requiring in order for the model to be adopted in daily routine practice. Cell centered approaches also offer a path to improved interpretability, by changing the unit of analysis from an arbitrary grid applied to the tissue slide to tiles centered on a biological nameable entity. 17 Clustered into recurrent phenotypes these presentations can then be inspected, and therefore annotated, constructing a vocabulary in the spirit of concept-based models, 18 that emerge unsupervised from the data. These morphological phenotypes could also for example relate to molecular subtypes of PDAC, which carry prognostic value 19 and are known to have detectable morphological signatures on hematoxylin-eosin sections. 20 We introduce C3PRO (Computational Cell-Centric Prognosis and Resectability Outcome) a novel cell-centric approach that builds an interpretable representation of the tissue from clustered foundation-model embeddings extracted from cell-level tiles centered on detected nuclei centroids, reflecting the given cell and its close neighborhood. The pipeline then builds a vocabulary of recurrent cell phenotypes using clustering methods followed by a spatial graph constructed on top of the back-propagated clusters (Figure 1). We make three contributions: Applied to PDAC biopsy digital WSIs, (1) C3PRO matches state-of-the-art MIL approaches for diagnosis, prognosis and resectability, providing higher interpretability while preserving equal performance. (2) Each of the clustered cell-phenotype is displayed as a mosaic of representative cells and annotated by two expert-pathologists, resulting in a morphological vocabulary which is then confronted with conventional histopathological review of the digital slides. (3) Downstream statistical analysis identifies cellular phenotypes associated with survival and resectability. It also shows that recurrent spatial neighborhoods provide no additional prognostic information beyond cellular composition.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study cohort

We assembled a retrospective cohort of pancreatic biopsy whole-slide images, all from the Centre Hospitalier de l'Université de Montréal (CHUM), from a registry of 1791 patients who were subject to pancreatic core needle biopsy. We applied the following exclusion criteria: absence of essential clinical data, non-PDAC histological type (including neuroendocrine tumors and other non-ductal neoplasms), pre-invasive neoplastic lesions without carcinoma (including IPMN and PanIN), and non-representative samples with insufficient tissue for histological analysis. The resulting cohort consisted of 809 patients with 825 slides. Hematoxylin-eosin-stained biopsies were sampled by endoscopic ultrasound-guided techniques (EUS-FNB) and scanned at 40x magnification.

One important detail is that the slides covered two different acquisition eras corresponding to a change of slide-scanner between 2021 and 2022, which will be referred subsequently as "prescanner" era: comprising 635 patient biopsies acquired from 2017 to 2021; and "post-scanner" era: with 174 patients ranging from 2022 to 2024. Therefore, a systematic source of appearance variation was anticipated and accounted for in both fold construction and clustering, as detailed below. All clinical, pathological and survival follow-up data were obtained under protocol 2025-12351, approved by the Centre de recherche du Centre Hospitalier de l'Université de Montréal.

Cohort characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Cell detection and tile-embedding

For each whole slide image, the cellular nuclei were segmented using CellViT++, 17 a foundationmodel-based segmentation pipeline based on a Segment Anything Model backbone. The slides were processed at 40× magnification with an overlapped 1,024 × 1,024-pixel tile grid (including a 128-pixel tile overlap) resulting in per-slide nucleus centroids and contours as the output. For each detected cell a 224 × 224-pixel vignette centered on the nucleus centroid was extracted from the slide and embedded using UNI2-h, 7 a pathology foundation model pre-trained on over 100,000 whole-slide images. Each tile was thus converted into a 1,536-dimension embedding vector capturing a given cell morphology and its local microenvironment. Because adjacent cells share part of their vignette, their embeddings are expected to covary. Across the cohort, this generated approximately 41 million cellular embeddings.

Cell phenotype vocabulary

The different cellular phenotypes were built by clustering the single-cell feature embeddings using spherical K-means (with K = 50, chosen as described below, and 15 epochs). Two separate clustering spaces were built for each of the two differing cohorts (the prognosis cohort consisting of invasive carcinoma only, and the diagnosis sub-cohort comprising additional slides who were diagnosed as negative for PDAC). This ensured that the cluster indices were specific to each cohort and not comparable across them. Each vocabulary was fitted on the slides of the task it serves, so that its fifty clusters are spent on the morphological variability of that population. Spherical K-Means was implemented using L2-normalized feature vectors, preventing cluster assignment to be influenced by embedding magnitude, and making it sensitive only to direction in the 1536D spherical space. Thus, removing the inductive bias induced by foundation-model representation whose norm tend to be influenced by the pre-training objective. The number of desired phenotypes was determined a priori and not tuned on the endpoints, ensuring sufficient phenotypic granularity while taking into account the need for each cluster to be subsequently reviewable by expert pathologists. A post hoc sweep analysis was still performed with K values ranging from 10 to 100, on the diagnostic vocabulary, and using criteria blind to all clinical endpoints. It found no optimum value with silhouette and clustering stability both decreasing linearly with K, and silhouette remaining close to zero throughout the sweep (0.047 to 0.070), which is an expected behavior for such a continuous embedding space. Therefore, the chosen K value reflects a granularity decision rather than the recovery of a discrete number of cell types as you would expect with transcriptomics. K = 50 fell within the stability plateau and produced the most robust granularity across clusters. The same value was applied to the prognostic vocabulary, for which the K sweep was not performed due to insufficient computational resources. Domain shift was also considered to prevent appearance shifts between scanner eras, by applying mean-centering per era before normalization. The streaming K-means method is mathematically equivalent to batch K-means, and processes one slide at a time so the full corpus of 41 million cells for the prognostic cohort, and 12 million cells for the diagnosis subcohort, never completely resides in memory. The output of this step is, for each slide, a 50-dimension vector of cluster proportions which is used as bag-level features for downstream classification and prediction tasks, along with per-cell cluster assignments to build the spatial graphs.

Spatial graph and survival model

A spatial cell graph was built for each slide using PyTorch, with each detected cell nucleus serving as a graph node, with a one-hot encoding of its phenotype cluster. The graph edges connected each cellular unit to its six nearest neighbors on the digital slide image, thus capturing the local cellular neighborhood. A graph attention network (GAT) was trained on top of these graphs to predict a per-slide hazard score using a Cox partial-likelihood target. The network was made up of three attention convolutional layers: two attention heads in the first two layers and one in the third; with ELU activation and dropout; followed by global mean pooling to convert the output into a 32dimensional slide-level embedding; which was finally passed through a two-layer perceptron to produce a log-hazard. The model was optimized using Adam with a cosine-annealing schedule, with early stopping on a held-out validation subset. The 32-dimensional slide embeddings generated by the attention model were then used as image-derived features for the downstream survival analyses.

Diagnostic model

For the diagnostic task, which involved separating cancer from non-cancer tissue, a post-scanner sub-cohort of 294 slides (195 malignant and 99 benign, from 274 patients) was used to build a specific phenotype vocabulary. Gradient boosting was applied to the slide-level cluster proportions vectors to predict the diagnosis, which allowed us to model per-cluster contributions with SHAP values. Cross-validation was performed with patient-grouping to ensure that slides from the same patient did not appear in both training and evaluation folds (some patients having multiple slides tied to them).

Multiple instance learning baselines

Comparison of C3PRO's performance with other weakly supervised frameworks was performed against four different well established architectural baselines: an attention-based MIL (ABMIL), 11 a clustering constrained attention MIL (CLAM), 21 a transformer based model (TransMIL), 12 and a state space model (2DMamba). 13 For each of these baselines the same foundation model was used (UNI2-h), and evaluation was carried on the same patient folds. The main difference between the four baselines and our cell-centric pipeline is the construction of the tiles which correspond to an arbitrary grid that covers the entire tissue. Using the same foundation model and data partition means that performance variation can most likely be attributed to the representation of the data and its aggregation rather than encoder, cohort or data split differences. For each baseline a per-slide risk score was computed for survival and evaluated against the cell-centric models using the same concordance-index convention.

Evaluation and statistical analysis

For prognosis and resectability, 5x5 cross-validation was used with the same 25 patient folds applied to every model. To avoid patient leakage, we made sure that all slides from a given patient would end up in the same fold by generating folds grouped by patient identifier. We also made sure to balance the two scanner eras by stratifying by acquisition year. For diagnosis prediction we used a separate partition (StratifiedGroupKFold, grouped by patient identifier). Two design features were specifically introduced to account for optimistic bias: (1) the GNN output vectors were generated out-of-fold, meaning that for each of the twenty-five folds a separate network was trained on the folds specific training set, guarantying that no test patient would contribute to the GNN output used to evaluate it. All feature standardization and model fitting were similarly confined to the training split of each fold.

The metrics used for performance evaluation were Harrell's concordance index averaged across the twenty-five folds and generated per fold on the held-out test set; Area under the ROC curve for diagnostic and resectability prediction. Model comparisons were carried out using Wilcoxon test on paired scores with Holm correction applied for multiple comparisons. Patients were split into risk tertiles based on prediction scores. Survival was compared using the log-rank test.

Recurrent motif discovery

Each phenotypic entity (cluster) was depicted as a mosaic composed of tile samples representative of all slides including the cluster with a minimum radius separation between selected samples. This ensured both inter-and intra-slide representativity, making sure that the tile mosaic would span across the whole cohort rather than a few slides. Once sampled, the fifty mosaics were reviewed by independent expert pathologists blinded to survival curves and effect, characterizing the dominant cellular type and the architectural pattern.

We also explored whether recurrent spatial motifs carried additional survival insights, in a similar manner as the cellular-neighborhood framework introduced by multiplexed imaging. 22 We retrieved for each cell the ten nearest neighbors and their cluster were extracted and summarized in a proportion vector of the fifty phenotypes. These neighborhoods were clustered across all cells and all slides using K-means into ten recurrent neighborhoods which were then used to build neighborhood composition vectors for each slide. and tested against overall survival using Cox regression with and without adjustment for the six clinical variables and Benjamini-Hochberg correction applied.

Because a neighborhood dominated by one cellular phenotype would carry redundant information as that phenotype's abundance, every significant neighborhood was tested with a Cox model with the slide proportion of its dominant cellular cluster, to distinguish genuine effect carried by architecture versus redundant effect from cellular composition. The neighborhood size was also varied through six values (four, six, ten, twenty, thirty, and fifty nearest cells) while keeping a fixed number of neighborhoods. Benjamini-Hochberg correction was also applied across all tests.

Conventional histopathological review

To confront our vocabulary to routine practice clinical criteria an expert pathologist reviewed 466 slides and evaluated five criteria (examples in Supplementary Figure S1): preserved acinar parenchyma, tumor cellularity, stromal morphology, lymphocytic infiltrate and tumor necrosis.

We matched vocabulary entities to their corresponding criteria and fixed them before any analysis of the readers annotations: per-slide summed proportions of the acinar, neoplastic and immune phenotypes, and for stromal morphologies the share of dense stroma in a maximum 400-pixel radius around tumor cells. Tumor necrosis was added after the initial analysis of the other four criteria; its counterpart is the only phenotype whose descriptions by both readers mention necrosis.

Concordance was computed with Spearman correlation for relative values and area under the ROC curve for ordinal and binary criteria. Each criterion was evaluated alongside the six clinical variables for prognosis, with nested Cox models on the survival folds to compare with the phenotype proportions.

Implementation

The pipeline was implemented in Python, using CellViT++ for cell detection, UNI2-h for feature extraction, a GPU streaming spherical K-means for clustering, PyTorch Geometric for the graph attention network, and scikit-survival, scikit-learn, XGBoost and SHAP for modeling and interpretation. All stochastic components used a fixed seed.

RESULTS

Cohort characteristics

The cohort consisted of 809 patients with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (consisting of 825 slides), from a unique center. The median age at diagnosis was 70.3 years (IQR 63.4-76.7), with a slight male predominance at 55.5%. Most tumors were moderately differentiated (grade 2, 53.3%), 20.0% were grade 1 and 22.4% were grade 3. The majority of tumors were located in the pancreatic head (62.2%), 25.2% originated from the body and 8.7% from the tail. 41.5% of cases reported large vessel involvement, in line with the fact that only 21.1% were subsequently subject to surgical resection. The median tumor size was 3.0 cm (IQR 2.5-3.8). Neoadjuvant therapy (FOLFIRINOX) was administered to 15.5% of patients. Median follow-up was 55.8 months, 89.7% of patients had died, and the median overall survival was 10.3 months (95% CI 9.3-11.4).

This coincides with the known high rate of mortality, mostly imputable to the late-stage, unresectable nature of the PDAC population. Some clinical and biological variables carrying known prognostic value in PDAC such as CA 19-9, were not uniformly recorded and are thus not reported; while some variables were not included by choice, due to their posteriori nature in order to fairly reproduce the setting of the pathologist reading the biopsy sample (pathological TNM stage, nodal status, resection and margin status).

Diagnostic performance and its morphological basis

C3PRO achieved a per fold mean area under the ROC curve of 0.958 (standard deviation 0.043; pooled out-of-fold 0.952) for the diagnostic task, with a sensitivity of 0.964 and specificity of 0.838 at a probability threshold of 0.5. The MIL baselines reached a similar performance range (pooled out-of-fold: CLAM 0.955, ABMIL 0.951, 2DMamba 0.935, TransMIL 0.919), with sensitivities and specificities at the same threshold of 0.954 and 0.869 (CLAM), 0.949 and 0.869 (ABMIL), 0.959 and 0.818 (2DMamba), and 0.985 and 0.576 (TransMIL). Since the diagnostic partition was split in five folds the per-fold paired testing is inadequate and underpowered; hence the pooled outof-fold discrimination was directly compared using the DeLong test on the 294 slides. Our cell centered approach was statistically indistinguishable from 3 baselines: CLAM (Figure 2A; ΔAUC -0.002), ABMIL (ΔAUC 0.002), and 2DMamba (ΔAUC 0.017), staying non-significant after Holm correction; and significantly surpassed only TransMIL. The cell-centric representation therefore matched the strongest MIL baselines without penalty, while making its decision inspectable, as detailed below.

Additionally, the cluster-based nature of the pipeline makes the diagnostic prediction inspectable, first by using the SHAP scores of the proportion model, which allows the identification of strong separators of malignancy. The diagnostic task was dominated by a single phenotype contributing six times more than the next cluster with a secondary tier of phenotypes carrying the remaining discriminative power (Figure 2B-D; Figure 3). Taking advantage of this, we observe that malignancy drivers are composed of carcinomatous structures, on desmoplastic stroma, and on necrotic areas, reproducing key diagnostic cues used in routine practice, while preserved pancreatic parenchyma, both exocrine and endocrine, mature stroma, and other incidental structures such as vessels and duodenal mucosa contribute in the opposite direction. Another reassuring observation is that uncommon histological types such as signet-ring cell carcinoma and undifferentiated carcinoma, also were strong predictors of malignancy even though they were less represented in the dataset, meaning that prediction is not confined to the conventional ductal morphology. Lastly, we also notice that chronic pancreatitis is not attributed to malignancy prediction, another reassuring fact since it is the principal differential diagnosis and a classical source of false positives on biopsy material. Refitting the diagnostic model without the most discriminating phenotype still maintained high performance with a slightly lowered area under the curve from 0.958 to 0.938 per fold (0.952 to 0.923; DeLong p = 0.017), the remaining phenotypes still carried most of the discrimination (Figure 2D).

Prognostic performance: parity with MIL

The overall survival was modeled in 809 patients with 5×5 nested cross-validation. The clinicalonly model used six clinical variables only available at biopsy, excluding surgical status which is unknown at diagnosis and mediates prognosis rather than predicting it. This clinical baseline achieved a concordance index of 0.575. Adding the graph embedding raised it to 0.617, an increment of 0.042 present in 24 of the 25 folds (Figure 4A). Both image-only representations exceeded the clinical baseline (cluster proportions 0.588, graph embedding 0.602; Figure 4B).

Adding the surgical status improved the best model concordance to 0.671, as previously said, a post-biopsy variable and only used as a sensitivity analysis.

Against MIL baselines, with concordance computed per patient under the same conditions, the image-only graph embedding (0.607) was comparable to 3 of the 4 baselines except TransMIL, which it exceeded (ABMIL 0.604, CLAM 0.603, 2DMamba 0.595, TransMIL 0.540); cluster proportions alone (0.593) were slightly below CLAM (-0.010, p = 0.041) and ABMIL (-0.011, p = 0.059). Adding the six clinical variables did not change the observed pattern (C3PRO 0.622, ABMIL 0.621, CLAM 0.618, 2DMamba 0.612, TransMIL 0.581; Figure 4C) with C3PRO matching state-of-the-art MIL, while representing a framework more open to pathological inspection.

Combining the clinical variables with the graph embedding outperformed their combination with the cluster proportions by 0.010 (0.617 versus 0.607; 18 of 25 folds), while adding the graph on top of the proportions added only 0.002 (19 of 25 folds): the information carried by the graph overlaps substantially with the proportions. This is consistent with the quasi-null result obtained in the recurrent neighborhoods search. The proportional hazards assumption was not rejected for the champion model (global Schoenfeld p = 0.80; tumor size and large vessel involvement deviated individually); a gradient-boosting model using the six clinical variables and cluster proportions, free of that assumption, reached a similar concordance (0.605 against 0.607 for the Cox model on the same inputs).

Prognostic stratification

Patients were stratified into risk groups based on the out of fold risk score tertile acquired from the champion model, which created clear separate clinical risk groups with median overall survival of 16.9, 10.8, and 5.9 months (Figure 4D; global log-rank p = 2×10 -20 ). Statistical significance was reached for all three pair comparisons, which for example included the high risk versus intermediate risk comparison, therefore, showing clear separation between the groups rather than an artificial sub-stratification of a uniform poor prognosis group. Even though this stratification is retrospectively determined, it still points that the model can identify prognostic subgroups with clinical relevance. This three-strata separation was also performed on each component of the champion model (e.g. clinical variables alone, cluster proportions alone, and graph embeddings alone); and found that each separated the cohort in distinct tertiles (Figure 4E), with the two non-clinical components producing marginally wider separations. The combined clinical-plus-graph model produced the widest separation of all.

Resectability

With a prevalence of 20.7%, resectability prediction was evaluated on the same cohort and folds as the prognostic prediction. The clinical-variables-alone model reached an AUC of 0.681, and adding the graph embeddings improved it to 0.696. The image-only models were weaker (cluster proportions 0.644, graph embedding 0.627) and the four MIL baselines weaker still (ABMIL 0.613, CLAM 0.614, 2DMamba 0.578, TransMIL 0.551). Again, C3PRO was at parity with the strongest baselines and beat only TransMIL. The graph embeddings and cluster proportions performed near-identically (0.696 versus 0.697). Again, the morphological added value remained small, which is expected for a decision that mostly relies on radiological criteria.

Interpretability: phenotype clusters and recurrent motifs

All fifty morphological phenotypes were displayed as mosaics, composed of representative cluster tile samples, and subsequently characterized by two expert pathologists blinded to the survival and resectability associations. Without specifically specifying annotation categories, we observed a high level of agreement in the terminology used by both reviewers for 11 of the significant 14 clusters: such as neoplastic epithelium, desmoplastic stroma, and normal exocrine parenchyma.

Where the labels differed, the focus was on the level of detail rather than the crude identity of the phenotype: one reviewer differentiated desmoplastic from collagenous fibrosis, or immature from mature stroma, where the other used a single term.

Fourteen clusters were significantly associated with overall survival after adjustment for the clinical covariates (Figure 5), with a stable effect for all clusters. With surgical status added as a seventh covariate, seven phenotypes were significant, six of them among the fourteen. The adverse prognosis clusters included multiple sub-phenotypes of carcinoma and stroma: Poorly differentiated (hazard ratio 1.20), discohesive tumor cells (1.10) and moderately differentiated carcinoma (1.20), were associated with worse prognosis. This predictive contribution is additional to the histological grade which was already included in the clinical covariates. Well differentiated PDAC also pushed toward adverse outcome but did not reach statistical significance (1.07, q = 0.19). On the opposite end of the spectrum, the protective phenotypes included preserved exocrine and acinar parenchyma (0.88 and 0.89). In contrast, a chronic pancreatitis phenotype rich in immune cells was adverse (hazard ratio 1.11, 95% CI 1.03-1.20), and remained so after adjustment for tumor burden, the summed proportion of the tumor phenotypes ( 1.11, 1.04-1.20). An emphasis should be made about the stromal phenotypes, since entities were identified on both ends of the predictive spectrum: some adverse (1.14, 1.12, 1.10) and some protective phenotypes (0.87, 0.87, 0.89). While one pathologist gave most of the stromal phenotypes the same label, the second characterized two of the pejoratives as desmoplastic and called the protective ones loose, mixed and immune-rich stroma, hinting at the poorly documented and reproducible prognosis influence of the stromal compartment maturity.

Regarding resectability, two clusters were in line with their prognostic value and showed significant effect after clinical adjustment, supporting the biological rather than incidental nature of these associations: The first cluster consisted of poorly differentiated carcinoma and was associated with lower odds of resection (0.61 OR) and shorter survival (1.20); the second significant cluster, vascular structures with immune cells, was associated with both increased odds of resection (1.32) and longer survival (0.83).

The study of recurrent spatial neighborhoods in contrast found almost no additive prognostic value compared to the crude phenotypic composition. Thirty-one neighborhoods were significantly associated with survival across the six neighborhood-sizes and two different vocabulary granularities (K = 10 and K = 50 phenotypes) that we tested. 30 of the 31 neighborhoods were highly dominated by a unique cluster and were statistically indistinguishable from the proportion of that given cluster regarding prognosis. The remaining neighborhood carried a modest prognostic association (1.10 HR). Therefore indicating that most of the predictive signal coming from this cohort resided in the tissue composition rather than the spatial relation between the different phenotypes.

Conventional histopathological criteria against the phenotype vocabulary

We asked one expert pathologist to review and characterize quantifiable and qualitative routine histopathological criteria for 441 patients of our main cohort, showing no detectable difference in survival (median of 10.3 months in both datasets, log-rank p = 0.81) but a higher share of low grade carcinoma (26% against 13% of grade 1 PDAC; p < 0.001). We observed high agreement on pathologist-model paired quantitative morphological counterparts, ρ = 0.89 for areas characterized as preserved acinar parenchyma, and 0.68 for tumor cellularity, and moderate agreement for ordinal or binary criteria, with areas under the curve of 0.80 for necrosis, 0.71 for dense stroma, 0.67 for immature stroma, and 0.67 for lymphocyte density (κ 0.35, 0.34 and 0.31 for necrosis, stroma and lymphocytes; Figure 6).

After clinical adjustment tumor cellularity (hazard ratio 1.31, 95% CI 1.19-1.45) and preserved acinar parenchyma (0.86, 0.78-0.96) remained significant for prognosis, lymphocyte density did not. Necrosis had the strongest effect (hazard ratio 1.91, 1.37-2.66; median survival 5.5 against 10.8 months) which also persisted after adjustment for tumor cellularity. The stromal compartment showed heterogeneous prediction, with mixed stroma being the most adverse pattern, without significant prognostic group separation (log-rank p = 0.16). The same observation was made for the matched vocabulary groups. In nested Cox models, the five criteria raised the concordance index of the clinical model from 0.582 to 0.624, and the phenotype proportions to 0.603. On this subset, five expert-scored criteria captured at least as much prognostic information as the phenotype proportions. The graph embedding, however, combined with the same clinical variables, matched the expert criteria (0.628) and added a small increment beyond them (0.010; 18 of 25 folds; Holm p = 0.019).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we aimed to explore whether a cell-centric representation of tissue slides could match traditional and state of the art multiple instance learning pipelines across diagnosis, prognosis and resectability prediction, using the same UNI2-h encoder, while providing improved reasoning transparency for the pathologist. Our framework achieved performance statistically indistinguishable from three MIL baselines and exceeded the fourth. We did not aim to surpass them but instead aimed toward increased routine practice value through legibility by producing decomposable and auditable predictions. Since the same UNI2-h encoder was used, the key distinguishing factor for this representation is explainability, our cell-centric method provides a morphological vocabulary alongside prediction score for each entity it comprises. This is a substantial shift compared to the baselines whose explainability relies mostly on attention heatmaps. In diagnosis, the prediction was dominated by a single phenotype and a small secondary tier. This is also consistent with the biological reasoning that pathologists use in routine practice, with diagnostic decision focusing on a rather small number of histological diagnostic criteria, it also illustrates the high-interpretability nature of our method, being allowed to decompose the model reasoning rather than only displaying an opaque attention map back onto the slide (Figure 2B-D; Figure 3). The prediction did not, however, depend on that phenotype alone: without it, performance remained high, the remaining phenotypes carrying most of the discrimination. This architecture also allowed us to explore further into the spatial cellular arrangement field through the use of graph neural networks and found limited prognostic additive value beyond crude phenotypic composition. The graph embedding added 0.042 of C-index to clinical variables but only a marginal 0.010 to cluster composition, and constituted our champion model for prognosis; for resectability it matched the cluster proportions (0.696 against 0.697). Of the 31 recurrent niches that were significant for prognosis, across six neighborhood scales, 30 were redundant with the proportion of the dominant phenotype. This should be interpreted as specific to our setting (i.e. PDAC biopsies from a single center) and not as a general law; and within it, cellular composition was the main carrier of prognostic information rather than spatial arrangement of the tissue. Each vignette, although centered on a cell, already captures much of its immediate surroundings, and the six nearest neighbors often lie within overlapping vignettes, so that the graph largely re-encoded a local field already present in the embeddings. Larger-scale architecture was not explored, the largest neighborhood tested comprising fifty cells, and may still carry prognostic information, which would require dedicated features such as tissue topology. On the subset of patients reviewed by a pathologist, the graph embedding matched five expert-scored criteria, each combined with the clinical variables (0.628 against 0.624), and added a small increment beyond them (0.010; 18 of 25 folds), which phenotype proportions did not.

Fourteen phenotypes were associated with prognosis after adjustment for clinical covariates, deliberately excluding surgical status in order to maintain an identical setting to that of practicing pathologists receiving a biopsy (seven phenotypes were significant when it was added); resectability associations were almost entirely explained by clinical confounding. They closely reproduced established pathology criteria with neoplastic epithelium and desmoplastic stroma carrying adverse prognosis weight, and preserved acinar parenchyma being protective. All the above associations are in line with basic pathology reasoning, where replacement of functional parenchyma by tumor and desmoplasia is synonym of disease progression. To our surprise, one phenotype corresponding to chronic pancreatitis, carried an adverse prognosis (HR 1.11), independently of tumor burden, which could perhaps reflect obstructive pancreatitis upstream of the tumor.

Beyond these expected associations, two takeaways add to the established pathology criteria. First, the neoplastic epithelium remained adverse even though histological grade was included in the adjusted covariates. This was the case for moderately and poorly differentiated PDAC, as well as discohesive tumor cells. The well-differentiated phenotype however had a smaller, non-significant effect, consistent with the known prognostic value of the differentiation gradient. Our representation adds the quantity of tumor in the sampled tissue as a prognostic factor, which conventional reporting does not record. This is consistent with the conventional review, in which the pathologist-scored tumor cellularity remained prognostic after clinical adjustment (hazard ratio 1.31). Second, the stromal compartment contained phenotypes showing opposite prognostic direction, that the first reader described almost identically, while the second called two of the adverse ones desmoplastic and the protective ones loose, mixed or immune-rich. This is reminiscent of the recognized but poorly reproducible grading of stromal maturity. 23, 24 With these observations we can generate the hypothesis that the embedding space captures stromal states that are not entirely recognized by routine pathology description, pointing towards the need for deeper molecular stromal subtyping and validation. This was also supported by the conventional histopathology review, where the association of stromal morphology with survival was heterogeneous, with mixed stroma (which was implemented as the "intermediate" stromal category) being the most adverse pattern, and remained non-significant on the reviewed subset.

Thus, a three-tier grading for stromal morphology fits poorly with the survival pattern observed here, supporting the hypothesis that further immunohistochemical or molecular validation is needed for the stromal compartment.

Against the conventional review, the cluster vocabulary closely reproduced the pathologist's quantitative scores (acinar parenchyma and tumor cellularity), and moderately the ordinal ones, without annotation. It carried no more prognostic value than the five criteria evaluated by the expert pathologist, which bounds the overall vocabulary contribution. The vocabulary does not discover additional prognostic information; it offers the expert's reading at the scale of a whole cohort, with finer resolution.

These results are subject to several limitations, first the cohort was built from a single institution, external validation on independent cohorts is needed to show whether our method is robust to scanner and center variations. In particular, the per-era mean-centering would have to be reestimated on the slides of any new scanner or center. Second, the conventional histopathological review was carried out by a single pathologist, aware of the study, which was also involved in the analysis of the phenotypes annotation, therefore these results should mostly be interpreted for agreement evaluation rather than independent validation. Third the clinical model lacks several variables of established prognostic value such as CA 19-9 levels, formal TNM stage and nodal status, that were not uniformly available, therefore undermining the clinical variable's prognosis performance. Fourth, the paired tests were performed on overlapping cross-validation folds, which means that the 25 measured C-index are not independent, although leaving the parity conclusions intact. Fifth, the feature embeddings used to build the vocabulary were extracted and annotated on this same cohort, even though unsupervised and never using the outcomes, it should be replicated on independent data. Sixth, embedding each of the 41 million cells is computationally heavier than tile-level multiple-instance learning, which adds computing time, energy use and environmental cost, to be weighed against the expected benefit, particularly for diagnosis, where conventional reading is already fast and accurate.

Future perspective and on-going work include external validation which constitutes the immediate priority, followed by molecular profiling of the phenotypes and correlation with already known molecular subclasses of PDAC such as basal-like and classical molecular subtypes, through spatial transcriptomics.

Finally, no component of the framework is tied to the pancreas, each processing step can be applied to any tissue, and a new vocabulary will be learned without prior labeling. The downstream prediction layers are also standard models. Therefore, the same approach could be applied to any organ, disease or clinical question.

CONCLUSION

A representation of pancreatic histology built from individual cells, their foundation-model embeddings and a vocabulary of recurrent phenotypes predicts diagnosis, survival and resectability as well as attention-, transformer-and state-space-based multiple-instance learning, while every feature it uses is a phenotype a pathologist can view and name. Parity is its precondition, interpretability its contribution. What it reveals is modest in effect size, coherent with established pathology, and in the stroma more finely resolved than routine description. External validation is required before any clinical claim, but a model whose reasoning can be expressed in the vocabulary of morphology is one whose errors a pathologist can audit and challenge.
