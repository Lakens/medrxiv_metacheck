Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Gingivitis is highly prevalent among older adults and is associated with systemic diseases.

Accessible and effective interventions are needed, particularly for underserved populations facing barriers to conventional dental care, such as older adults. This study evaluated the efficacy of a novel, multi-component lozenge (PTx800) designed to modulate the oral environment through blockade of sugar-to-acid and sugar-to-biofilm production of the oral microbiota. A six-week, double-blind, randomized, active placebo-controlled pilot trial was conducted with older adults (final sample n=20) residing in Cleveland, Ohio. Participants were randomized to receive either PTx800 or an active placebo lozenge containing sugar alcohols, used three times daily. Primary outcomes included Modified Gingival Index (MGI), Plaque Index (PI), Bleeding on Probing (BOP), Periodontal Probing Depth (PPD), and changes in oral pathogen load assessed by quantitative PCR. The PTx800 group demonstrated a 100% reduction in BOP, achieving complete resolution of bleeding sites, compared to an 82% reduction in the active placebo group. PPD was stabilized in the PTx800 group and slightly worsened in the control group. Microbiological analysis revealed a significant reduction in the total bacterial load in the PTx800 group, with pronounced decreases in key red complex periodontal pathogens, including Porphyromonas gingivalis (Pg), Tannerella forsythia (Tf), and Treponema denticola (Td). PTx800 lozenges significantly improved clinical and microbiological measures of gingival health in older adults compared to active placebo. These findings suggest that PTx800 is a promising, non-antibiotic approach for managing oral inflammation and dysbiosis in aging populations, with potential implications for reducing oral pathogens strongly linked to systemic diseases.

INTRODUCTION

Gingivitis is an inflammatory condition characterized by swollen, red, and bleeding gums.

It is the first stage of periodontal disease, the most common chronic inflammatory condition affecting aging populations. Severe periodontitis is the leading cause of tooth loss and impacts a significant percentage of adults over 65 years of age [1]. The burden of these diseases is not distributed equally. Older adults, particularly those from minority communities and lower socioeconomic backgrounds, face substantial barriers to accessing professional dental care, such as financial cost, lack of dental insurance, transportation difficulties, and a perceived lack of need [2]. These challenges, together with a high prevalence of tooth loss, infrequent dental visits, and numerous barriers to care, underscore the urgent need for accessible, affordable, and effective interventions that can be implemented outside of a traditional care setting.

The consequences of untreated oral inflammation extend far beyond the oral cavity. A robust body of evidence has established the "oral-systemic link," wherein dysbiosis of the oral microbiome is implicated in the pathogenesis and progression of numerous systemic diseases prevalent in aging [3]. Periodontal pathogens and their inflammatory byproducts can enter the bloodstream, and contribute to conditions such as cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, respiratory disease, and neurodegenerative disorders including Alzheimer's disease [4]. A central actor in this process is Porphyromonas gingivalis (Pg), a keystone pathogen that not only drives local periodontal ligament destruction but also acts as a "conductor" to orchestrate systemic inflammation [5]. The ability of Pg and its virulence factors to translocate to distal sites highlights the oral cavity and subgingival mucosa as a critical reservoir of pro-inflammatory anaerobes [6].

Current management strategies for gingivitis and periodontitis rely primarily on mechanical biofilm disruption (scaling and root, debridement) and the use of antiseptics and antibiotics. While these may provide a temporary reduction in pathogen burden, they are not curative. Systematic reviews show an average 9% reduction in BOP using conventional approaches to biofilm disruption [7]. In addition, antimicrobials can pose risks with long-term or frequent use [8]. The increasing threat of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) necessitates the development of novel non-antimicrobial approaches [9]. PTx800 was identified from highthroughput screening of Generally Recognized As Safe (GRAS) ingredients on American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) dental caries and periodontitis biofilm isolates. Compounds were categorized into sugar-to-acid blockade, sugar-to-biofilm blockade, selective disruption of pathogen growth, and preservation of commensal growth. PTx800, a dual ingredient mixture of cyclodextrin and vitamin B6, was selected for its superior activity in sugar blockade and selective growth activities with invitro biofilm formation and sugar challenge growth assays.

This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of PTx800 in reducing clinical and microbiological markers of gingivitis in a population of older adults drawn from a county that included senior centers serving clients in urban, low-income neighborhoods as well as more affluent suburban centers. The primary hypotheses were that, compared to an active placebo containing sugar alcohols, the PTx800 lozenge would lead to a statistically significant reduction in clinical signs of gingival inflammation and bacterial counts of keystone periodontal pathogens.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

.

Study Design and Ethical Approval

A six-week, double-blind, randomized, active placebo-controlled experimental study was conducted in partnership with the Benjamin Rose Institute and its senior centers, i.e., the Rose Centers for Aging Well (RCAW). This study was registered on ClincalTrials.gov (NCT03693027; Registration date October 2, 2018). The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Benjamin Rose Institute on Aging and adhered to the ethical principles for medical research involving human subjects outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki [10]. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment, and all data were anonymized to maintain confidentiality.

Participant Recruitment and Screening

Participants were recruited from July to October 2018. Enrollment was completed in December 2018. Two phases of the study were designed. In the first phase, a survey was conducted with 122 senior center participants regarding their oral health habits/hygiene, perceptions of dental health and dental visits including barriers to dental care. The focus of this paper is on the second phase of this study, i.e., the randomized clinical trial to test the efficacy of PTx800 in reducing clinical and microbiological markers of gingivitis in an underserved population of older adults.

The eligibility criteria for the second phase were divided into three steps. In the first step, investigators excluded participants who had legal guardians, used tobacco within the past two years, and had full dentures. Of the 122 participants from Phase I, 52 met the initial eligibility criteria for the study; however, only one participated.

Additional participants were recruited from senior centers across northeast Ohio via flyers and outreach events. Interested respondents completed the Short Portable Mental Status Questionnaire (SPMSQ), a validated cognitive screening tool assessing orientation, memory, and attention; individuals with five or more errors were excluded [11]. Those who passed the cognitive screen underwent an on-site dental screening.

In total, 230 individuals were contacted, of which 141 met the criteria for study participation, prior to the dental screening. Of these, 67 were deemed ineligible after the dental examination, leaving a potential sample of 74 older adults. Of the 74, 47 older adults either passively or verbally refused to participate, and 27 completed the study for a response rate of 36%.

Randomization and Intervention

Participant randomization was generated using a computer software program in a 1:1 allocation sequence. The randomization sequence was generated by a person not involved in recruitment or clinical evaluations. The coordinating site received one package containing 60 individual envelopes identified by participant number. Sequence concealment was maintained using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. Each sealed envelope contained the assigned intervention details (lozenge name, formulation, and manufacturer lot number). To maintain blinding for both participants and the study coordinator, the bottle labeling for both active and placebo lozenges was coded. Neither the personnel enrolling participants, the personnel assigning interventions, the dental examiner, nor the study participants had access to the master randomization sequence or knowledge of intervention assignments throughout the trial.

The sample of 27 eligible participants were randomized to the PTx800 group (intervention group, n=15) or the active placebo lozenges (control group, n=12). The PTx800 lozenge (manufactured by Solara Labs, Miami Gardens, FL) contained a proprietary blend of cyclodextrin, pyridoxal-5-phosphate, calcium carbonate, and sodium bicarbonate, along with inactive ingredients (mannitol, sorbitol, modified cellulose, natural mint flavor, magnesium stearate, stevia leaf extract). The active placebo lozenge (manufactured by Solara Labs, Miami Gardens, FL) contained sugar alcohol and other inactive ingredients but lacked the primary active components. Both lozenges were manufactured under cGMP as mandated by the US FDA and were identical in appearance and taste. Participants, study personnel, and the testing laboratory were blinded to the treatment allocation.

Study Procedure and Participant Adherence to Intervention

Study visits occurred at baseline (Visit 1), three weeks (Visit 2), and six weeks (Visit 3).

At Visit 1 (baseline) and Visit 3 (post-test), participants were surveyed about their oral health conditions and habits, physical and mental health, and well-being. This study focuses on the clinical oral health assessments, including MGI, PI, BOP, and PPD measurements that were performed during each visit by a licensed dental professional. Oral rinse samples were also collected at each visit.

Participants were instructed to let one lozenge dissolve in their mouth for 60-120 seconds after each meal, three times per day, for six weeks. They were asked to maintain their usual oral hygiene habits but abstain from using mouthwash. Adherence was monitored via self-reported daily logs, and lozenge counts at each visit; consumption of ≥70% of assigned lozenges was required for inclusion in the final analysis. Participants were contacted periodically over the duration of the study to address any questions or concerns. Adverse events were systematically assessed at each visit.

Clinical Oral Health Assessments

Modified Gingival Index

Modified Gingival Index (MGI) scoring is a visual assessment used to quantify the level of gingival inflammation [12]. The scoring system rates mild and moderate inflammation where0=absence of inflammation,1=mild inflammation or with slight changes in color and texture but not in all portions of gingival marginal or papilla,2=mild inflammation, such as the preceding criteria, in all portions of gingival marginal or papilla,3=moderate, bright surface inflammation, erythema, edema and/or hypertrophy of gingival marginal or papillary, and4=severe inflammation of erythema, edema and/or marginal gingival hypertrophy of the unit or spontaneous bleeding, congestion or ulceration. The MGI is obtained by adding the values of each tooth and dividing by the number of teeth examined. A score from 0.1-1.0 indicates mild inflammation, 1.1-2.0 indicates moderate inflammation, and 2.1-3.0 indicates severe inflammation.

Plaque Index

The Turesky Plaque Index (PI) measures plaque accumulation using a disclosing solution and is scored on a 0 to 5 scale in which a score of0=no plaque,1=separate flecks of plaque,2=continuous band of plaque less or equal to 1mm,3=continuous band of plaque greater than 1mm but less than 1/3 of crown height,4=continuous band of plaque greater than or equal to 1/3 but less than or equal to 2/3 of crown height, and5=continuous band of plaque greater than 2/3 of crown height. Each tooth received six individual scores at mesial, middle, and distal aspect for both the facial and lingual surfaces. Each participant's score was calculated by adding the scores at each site and dividing by the number of sites evaluated. Higher scores denote greater plaque accumulation [13].

Bleeding on Probing

Bleeding on Probing (BOP) was assessed using a periodontal probe with a 0.5 mm diameter tip inserted into the gingival crevice, swept from distal to mesial around the tooth at a depth of approximately 1mm and at an angle of approximately 60° while in contact with the sulcular epithelium. Each of 4 gingival areas (disto-buccal, midbuccal, mesio-buccal and mid-lingual) around each tooth was assessed in this manner waiting approximately 30 seconds before recording the number of gingival units which bleed, according to the following scale:0=absence of bleeding after 30 seconds,1=bleeding observed after 30 seconds, and2=immediate bleeding observed. The number of bleeding sites was used to calculate the gingival bleeding score [14].

Periodontal Probing Depth

Periodontal Probing Depth (PPD), also known as pocket depth, was measured as the distance in millimeters from the gingival margin to the base of the pocket at six sites per tooth/implant using standardized disposable double-ended explorer periodontal probes (Practicon, Greenville, NC) calibrated to 1mm increments [15, 16]. Measurements were obtained by inserting the probe parallel to the root surface along the periodontal pocket axis until slight resistance was met at the base of the pocket, following established periodontal assessment protocols. The deepest measured value per anatomical section (mesial, mid, distal) was recorded for clinical evaluation.

Sample Collection

Oral rinses were collected from participants at baseline and six weeks. Participants were instructed to rinse thoroughly with 4mL of sterile water for 15 seconds, after which the rinse was expelled into a sterile collection tube. All samples were immediately labeled and time stamped.

Samples were stored at 4°C at the study site before being transported on ice to Oral DNA Labs for testing within 24 hours. Processing and analysis were performed by Oral DNA Labs according to FDA registered test methods for MyPerioPath® qPCR test (Oral DNA Labs, Eden Prairie MN).

Assessment of Outcomes

Primary outcomes for the clinical trial included changes in clinical oral health measures (MGI, PI, BOP, PPD) and reductions in oral pathogen burden. Secondary outcomes included general health and lifestyle factors.

Clinical Outcomes Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Baseline characteristics were compared between the PTx800 and control groups using Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables (MGI, PI, BOP, PPD and microbiological CFU counts) and chi-square tests for categorical variables (e.g., gender, smoking status and treatment group). To assess relationships within the dataset, Kendall's rank correlation coefficients were used to evaluate associations between clinical measures and pathogen concentrations (CFU counts). The Difference-in-Differences (DiD) method was applied to compare temporal changes between cohorts for each pathogen using a linear model at baseline and six-week timepoints. For microbiological data, CFU counts for pathogens, including Aa, Cr, Cs, Ec, En, Fn, Pm, Pg, Pi, Td and Tf were log-transformed before analysis to normalize distributions. Participants with CFU levels of zero at both baseline and endpoint were excluded from pathogen-specific analyses. Mann-Whitney tests were used to compare CFU counts and relative abundances within and between cohorts. For pocket depth, statistical comparisons were conducted both using per-person averages across all measured teeth and using all individual tooth measurements. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen's d formula for statistically significant findings (p < 0.05). Missing data were handled using multiple imputation, consistent with the study protocol. All statistical tests were two-sided, and an alpha level of 0.05 was used to assess significance.

DISCUSSION

This randomized controlled pilot trial provides preliminary evidence that PTx800 lozenges improve clinical and microbiological markers of gingival health in older adults over a six-week intervention period. The most striking finding was the complete elimination of BOP in the PTx800 group -a 100% reduction compared to an 82% reduction in the active placebo control group. This difference was statistically significant (p=0.04) and clinically meaningful, as BOP serves as a sensitive indicator of active gingival inflammation and is strongly predictive of periodontal disease progression [14, 17, 18]. The achievement of complete BOP resolution in six weeks is noteworthy given that this cohort consisted of community-dwelling older adults with pre-existing gingivitis, many of whom reported systemic health conditions that typically complicate oral health management.

Clinical Significance of Bleeding on Probing

The superior performance of PTx800 in eliminating BOP represents a substantial clinical advancement for managing gingivitis in older adults. BOP reflects acute inflammation within the gingival sulcus and is considered one of the most reliable early indicators of periodontal tissue destruction [17, 18]. The complete resolution observed in the PTx800 group suggests that the intervention not only reduced surface-level inflammation but also addressed underlying inflammatory processes at the tissue level. This is particularly significant because chronic gingival inflammation, if left untreated, can progress to periodontitis-a condition affecting a substantial proportion of older adults that has been linked to numerous systemic health complications [19, 20].

In contrast to BOP, which reflects acute inflammation, the Modified Gingival Index captures more chronic, visual signs of inflammation and showed only modest reductions in both groups (PTx800 36%, Control 39%). This discrepancy between BOP and MGI improvements is expected and informative. BOP is a more sensitive measure of deep tissue inflammation and vascular permeability changes that often precede visible alterations in gingival appearance. The more pronounced improvement in BOP compared to MGI suggests that PTx800's therapeutic effects manifest first at the vascular and inflammatory level before producing changes in gross gingival morphology [14, 21]. A longer study duration may be required to observe significant improvements in MGI scores as chronic inflammation resolves and the tissues undergo remodeling.

The probing depth measurements provide additional context for interpreting these clinical improvements. Periodontal probing is a widely studied method for assessing disease status, with research having established the relationship between probe penetration and underlying clinical and histologic signs of inflammation [16, 21, 22]. The PTx800 group demonstrated a slight average decrease in pocket depth (-0.47%), suggesting stabilization or modest improvement of periodontal health, while the control group experienced a slight average increase (+0.19%), potentially indicating a subtle progression of disease severity over the study period. Although both changes were modest, the divergent trends are clinically relevant-particularly in an aging population where progressive pocket deepening often signals advancing periodontitis [23]. The stabilization achieved by PTx800 is especially meaningful given that many participants in this group had baseline systemic conditions, such as arthritis and xerostomia, that typically impair oral hygiene practices and accelerate periodontal disease progression [19, 20].

Microbiological Impact: Targeting the Red Complex Pathogens

The clinical improvements in the PTx800 group were accompanied by profound shifts in the oral microbiome composition. Quantitative PCR analysis revealed that PTx800 markedly reduced the load of the red complex pathogens (Pg, Tf, Td) which are strongly associated with destructive periodontitis and considered keystone pathogens in periodontal disease progression [8, 24, 25]. These anaerobic bacteria colonize subgingival plaque biofilms, produce potent virulence factors including proteolytic enzymes and lipopolysaccharides, and orchestrate dysbiotic microbial communities that drive chronic inflammation and tissue destruction [5, 26]. The reduction of these pathogens from baseline to week six in the PTx800 group, while levels remained stable or increased slightly in the control group, demonstrates PTx800's capacity to modulate the pathogenic oral microbiome toward a healthier, more balanced state.

Of particular significance is the reduction in Porphyromonas gingivalis, which serves as a biomarker of gingival and periodontal infection and represents one of the most extensively studied periodontal pathogens. P. gingivalis possesses unique virulence characteristics that enable it to evade host immune responses, manipulate complement pathways, and persist in subgingival biofilms despite the host's inflammatory response [5, 24]. Beyond the oral cavity, accumulating evidence implicates P. gingivalis in the pathogenesis of several systemic disorders, most notably Alzheimer's disease and cardiovascular disease [27]. Studies have demonstrated that P. gingivalis can translocate from periodontal tissues to distant anatomical sites including the cerebrospinal fluid, brain parenchyma, coronary arteries, and liver [28, 29]. The bacterium's gingipain proteases have been detected in the brains of Alzheimer's disease patients, and experimental models demonstrate that oral infection with P. gingivalis can induce neuroinflammation, amyloid-beta deposition, and cognitive impairment [29]. Similarly, P. gingivalis DNA and viable bacteria have been identified in atherosclerotic plaques, and periodontal infection has been associated with increased cardiovascular disease risk [22, 30].

The demonstrated ability of PTx800 to reduce P. gingivalis burden therefore carries implications that extend far beyond oral health. Given the growing recognition that periodontal pathogens-particularly P. gingivalis-may contribute to systemic disease pathogenesis, interventions that effectively reduce these bacteria in the oral cavity could potentially mitigate their systemic dissemination and associated disease risks. This possibility is especially relevant for older adults, who experience higher rates of both periodontal disease and systemic conditions such as Alzheimer's disease and cardiovascular disease [29] [30] [31]. Further research is needed to determine whether sustained reduction of P. gingivalis can reduce biomarkers of systemic inflammation, slow cognitive decline in at-risk populations, or improve cardiovascular outcomes.

In addition to the red complex organisms, PTx800 also reduced Eikenella corrodens, a pathobiont found in subgingival plaque that has been associated with various oral and systemic infections [32, 33]. The intervention also eliminated Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans (Aa), a pathogen associated with aggressive periodontitis, from the PTx800 group where it was uniquely detected at baseline [34]. By the six-week endpoint, Aa was non-detectable in all PTx800 participants. Together, these findings suggest that PTx800 helps re-establish microbial balance by selectively reducing key pathogens and pathobionts that thrive in dysbiotic biofilm communities, particularly those residing in difficult-to-access subgingival niches. However, due to the small sample size, these preliminary findings should be interpreted with caution and warrant validation in larger, tong-term clinical trials.

Proposed Mechanisms of Action

The mechanisms underlying PTx800's clinical and microbiological effects likely involve multiple complementary pathways. PTx800 contains a proprietary blend of botanical extracts and bioactive compounds that have demonstrated antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory properties in previous studies. The formulation's ability to reduce red complex pathogens suggests direct or indirect antimicrobial activity against these anaerobic species, potentially through disruption of biofilm architecture, interference with bacterial adhesion, or modulation of the subgingival environment to favor commensal over pathogenic species. Unlike broad-spectrum antibiotics, which face growing challenges with resistance developmentincluding among periodontal pathogens like P. gingivalis-PTx800 appears to work through alternative mechanisms involving nutrient stress, that may be less susceptible to resistance evolution [35]. This is particularly important given that P. gingivalis shows a growing propensity for antibiotic resistance, necessitating interventions that can modulate microorganisms through non-antibiotic means [8].

The lozenge delivery format may enhance PTx800's efficacy by promoting sustained contact between active ingredients and oral tissues. As the lozenge dissolves slowly over several minutes, bioactive compounds are distributed throughout the oral cavity via saliva, potentially reaching subgingival sites that are difficult to access with conventional oral hygiene practices [25, 36, 37]. The formulation may also contribute to the observed reduction in BOP by dampening the host's overactive immune response to bacterial challenge, thereby reducing vascular permeability and tissue damage while allowing immune-mediated clearance of pathogens to proceed [14, 19, 21].

Active Placebo Design and Control Group Improvements

A unique and important aspect of this study's design was the use of an active placebo control comprised entirely of sugar alcohols (xylitol, sorbitol, and erythritol). This represents a rigorous test of PTx800's efficacy, as sugar alcohols themselves possess documented oral health benefits [38]. Xylitol in particular has been shown to inhibit Streptococcus mutans growth, reduce plaque formation, and enhance remineralization. Sugar alcohols are non-fermentable by most oral bacteria, thus creating an unfavorable environment for acidogenic species, and may exert mild antimicrobial effects [39]. The substantial improvements observed in the control group-including an 82% reduction in bleeding on probing and approximately 40% reduction in gingival inflammation-attest to the therapeutic activity of the sugar alcohol base.

Furthermore, using a lozenge three times daily stimulates saliva flow, which provides multiple oral health benefits including mechanical cleansing of bacterial debris, acid buffering, delivery of antimicrobial components (lysozyme, lactoferrin, immunoglobulins), and maintenance of the oral mucosal barrier [40] [41] [42]. Enhanced saliva flow is particularly beneficial for older adults, many of whom experience medication-induced xerostomia that predisposes them to increased plaque accumulation and gingival inflammation [43]. Additionally, the "Hawthorne effect," whereby study participation itself motivates participants to improve their oral hygiene behaviors, likely contributed to improvements in both groups [44].

PTx800 significantly surpassed the combined benefits of sugar alcohols, saliva stimulation, and enhanced hygiene motivation, underscoring its specific therapeutic action. The study results demonstrate an unequivocal benefit from the PTx800 bioactive ingredients, validating the formulation's therapeutic rationale and suggesting that the observed effects cannot be attributed solely to the delivery vehicle or study participation effects.

Oral Health in the Aging Populations

This study addresses an important public health concern: the oral health needs of the aging population. Older adults face multiple challenges to maintain optimal oral health, including agerelated declines in manual dexterity, reduced saliva flow, comorbid systemic conditions, limited access to dental care, and cumulative lifetime exposure to oral diseases [43]. Gingivitis and periodontitis affect a substantial proportion of older adults, with prevalence increasing with age.

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey has consistently documented that severe periodontitis affects approximately one in five adults aged 65 and older in the United States, with even higher rates among certain demographic subgroups [45].

The burden of periodontal disease in older adults is compounded by its bidirectional relationships with systemic health conditions that are highly prevalent in this age group.

Periodontal disease has been associated with cardiovascular disease, diabetes, respiratory diseases, various cancers, rheumatoid arthritis, and cognitive decline including Alzheimer's disease [22, 29, [46] [47] [48]. Interventions that effectively manage gingival inflammation and periodontal pathogen burden in older adults may yield benefits that extend beyond oral health, potentially influencing systemic inflammatory status and chronic disease trajectories. PTx800 represents a particularly appropriate intervention for older adults because it is delivered as a lozenge that requires no specialized technique, minimal manual dexterity, and can be easily integrated into daily routines. This accessibility is crucial for older adults with arthritis or other conditions that impair the fine motor control required for effective toothbrushing and flossing. The formulation's ability to reduce subgingival pathogens with no mechanical intervention suggests it may partially compensate for inadequate plaque removal, offering a valuable adjunctive therapy for individuals who struggle with conventional oral hygiene practices.

Oral Health Disparities in the Aging Population

The study population reflects important demographic characteristics relevant to oral health disparities. Participants were predominantly female (90% overall) and racially diverse, with approximately 45% identifying as Black or African American. This composition is significant because oral health disparities persist across socioeconomic, racial, and ethnic lines in the United States, with minority populations and individuals of lower socioeconomic status experiencing disproportionately high rates of periodontal disease, tooth loss, and barriers to dental care access [49]. African American and Hispanic older adults, in particular, have been documented to experience higher prevalence and severity of periodontitis compared to their White counterparts, even after adjusting for socioeconomic factors [49]. The successful demonstration of PTx800's efficacy in this population is particularly meaningful, as it suggests the intervention may help address oral health disparities by providing an accessible, effective option for communities that face barriers to traditional dental care. The lozenge format requires no prescription, specialized equipment, or professional administration, making it a feasible option for integration into community health programs, senior centers, or public health initiatives targeting underserved older adults.

Baseline Health Characteristics and Study Robustness

A notable feature of this trial was the imbalance in self-reported baseline systemic health conditions between groups. The PTx800 group had a substantially higher burden of conditions including hypertension (75% vs. 30% in controls), arthritis (43% vs. 13%), and dry mouth complaints (32% vs. 0%). These conditions are known to negatively impact oral health through multiple pathways. Arthritis impairs the manual dexterity required for effective toothbrushing and flossing, directly compromising mechanical plaque removal [48].

Many antihypertensive medications cause xerostomia, reducing the protective effects of saliva and increasing susceptibility to plaque accumulation and gingival inflammation. Dry mouth itself is associated with increased risk of dental caries, candidiasis, and periodontal disease due to diminished antimicrobial proteins and buffering capacity [41, 43].

Despite participants in this group having a greater baseline health disadvantage, PTx800 demonstrated significant clinical and microbiological improvements. This strengthens confidence in the intervention's efficacy and suggests that PTx800 can be effective even in populations with comorbidities that typically predict poorer oral health outcomes. This finding has important clinical implications, as older adults with multiple chronic conditions are a population in need of accessible oral health interventions that can be effective despite physical limitations.

Study Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged when interpreting these findings. First, participant recruitment and retention proved challenging. This may have been due to our strict inclusion criteria alongside the documented challenges in recruiting recruitment and retention of older adults in clinical trials [50]. Consequently, the small sample size (n=20 total; 11 intervention, 9 control) reduces statistical power and limits the generalizability of the results. Nevertheless, because older adults are underrepresented in clinical trials [51], generating preliminary safety and efficacy data in this population provides valuable groundwork for larger, fully powered follow up studies. While the statistically significant differences between groups are encouraging, the study would benefit from replication in larger cohorts to confirm these findings and enable subgroup analyses by demographic characteristics, baseline disease severity, or comorbidity profiles. The six-week study duration, while sufficient to detect meaningful changes in inflammatory markers and microbial composition, may not be able to detect the long-term effects on attachment loss, bone resorption, or tooth retention. Extended follow-up studies are needed to determine whether the observed improvements are sustained over time and whether they translate into clinically meaningful reductions in periodontitis progression or tooth loss.

The imbalance in baseline systemic health conditions, while potentially strengthening the case for PTx800's efficacy as discussed above, introduces confounding variables that complicate interpretation. Future trials should stratify randomization by key health conditions or employ propensity score matching to ensure more balanced groups. The study was conducted in Northeast Ohio with senior center participants, which limits generalizability to other geographic regions, cultural contexts, or populations with different oral health literacy levels or access to dental care.

While this study assessed clinical and microbiological outcomes, it did not measure potential systemic effects such as changes in circulating inflammatory markers, cognitive function, or cardiovascular parameters. Given the proposed associations between periodontal pathogens and systemic diseases, future studies incorporating these measures would provide valuable insights into whether oral health improvements translate into systemic health benefits.

Clinical Implications

The results of this trial have practical implications for clinical practice and patient care.

PTx800 lozenges represent a promising adjunctive therapy for older adults with gingivitis, particularly those with physical limitations, xerostomia, or other conditions that impair conventional oral hygiene practices. The lozenge format offers ease of use and direct delivery of bioactive ingredients throughout the oral cavity including difficult-to-reach subgingival sites.

For dental practitioners, PTx800 could be recommended as part of a comprehensive oral health maintenance strategy, particularly for patients who struggle with plaque control despite instruction in proper brushing and flossing techniques. The intervention may be especially valuable during the early stages of gingivitis when intervention can prevent progression to periodontitis and irreversible tissue damage. Given the active placebo control design, clinicians can be confident that PTx800 provides benefits beyond sugar alcohols alone.

For public health practitioners and policymakers, PTx800's demonstrated efficacy in an underserved population suggests potential applications in community-based oral health programs targeting health disparities. The intervention's accessibility and ease of use make it suitable for distribution through community health centers, senior centers, or other public health initiatives, potentially reaching older adults who face barriers to traditional dental care.

The observed reduction in periodontal pathogens, particularly P. gingivalis, raises the possibility that interventions targeting oral health may have broader health implications for older adults. While this study did not assess systemic health outcomes, the documented associations between periodontal pathogens and conditions such as Alzheimer's disease and cardiovascular disease suggest that managing oral pathogen burden could be an overlooked strategy for systemic disease prevention. This merits further study in geriatric medicine and preventive health care, potentially elevating oral health from a quality-of-life concern to an integral component of chronic disease management.

Implications for Future Research

The findings of this study open several important avenues for future investigation. First, larger, multicenter randomized controlled trials with extended follow-up periods are needed to confirm PTx800's efficacy, assess the durability of microbiome modulatory effects, and evaluate whether clinical improvements are maintained with continued use or after discontinuation. Such studies should include diverse populations across age ranges, geographic regions, and health status profiles to establish generalizability.

Second, mechanistic studies are warranted to elucidate the specific pathways through which PTx800 exerts its antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory effects. In vitro studies examining PTx800's effects on periodontal pathogen growth, biofilm formation, virulence factor expression, and host inflammatory responses would provide valuable insights. Animal models of periodontitis could assess tissue-level effects and mechanisms of action in vivo.

Third, given the strong associations between P. gingivalis and systemic diseases, particularly Alzheimer's disease and cardiovascular disease, future trials should investigate whether sustained reduction of this pathogen through PTx800 supplementation influences systemic disease outcomes. Studies in populations at risk for cognitive decline could assess whether PTx800 reduces circulating inflammatory markers, slows progression of cognitive impairment, or decreases biomarkers of neuroinflammation. Similarly, trials in individuals with cardiovascular risk factors could evaluate whether oral pathogen reduction translates into improved endothelial function, reduced atherosclerotic progression, or favorable changes in cardiovascular inflammatory markers.

Fourth, implementation studies are needed to understand how PTx800 could be integrated into public health programs, community health centers, or dental practices serving older adults, particularly in underserved communities. Cost-effectiveness analyses comparing PTx800 supplementation to standard periodontal therapies would inform policy decisions and insurance coverage considerations.

Finally, studies examining the oral microbiome composition at a deeper taxonomic resolution using metagenomic sequencing would provide insights into whether PTx800 selectively reduces pathogenic species while preserving or promoting beneficial commensal bacteria, which would support its characterization as a microbiome-modulating rather than a broadly antimicrobial intervention.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, this randomized controlled pilot trial provides compelling preliminary evidence that PTx800 lozenges significantly improve clinical and microbiological markers of gingival health in older adults with gingivitis. The intervention achieved complete elimination of BOP, stabilized pocket depth, and substantially reduced red complex periodontal pathogens, outperforming an active sugar alcohol placebo control. These findings are noteworthy given the study population's elevated systemic disease burden and the critical role of oral health in the aging population. The reduction of P. gingivalis and other red complex bacteria may have implications extending beyond oral health, given these pathogens' associations with Alzheimer's disease, cardiovascular disease, and other systemic conditions. PTx800 represents a promising, accessible intervention for managing gingivitis in older adults, particularly those in underserved communities or with physical limitations that impair conventional oral hygiene. Further research is needed to confirm these findings in larger populations, investigate long-term outcomes, and explore potential systemic health benefits of reducing periodontal pathogen burden.
