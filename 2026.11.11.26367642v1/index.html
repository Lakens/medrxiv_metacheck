Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text
p ≥ .05 Model fit was determined by combining the chi-square test (p ≥ .05 indicating excellent fit) and TLI (≥ .95 indicating excellent fit), yielding either excellent or poor model fit.

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

Abstract

Background: EMFRA is a brief scale developed to assess physical, cognitive, emotional, and social functioning in older adults. This study examined its initial psychometric properties.

Methods: A cross-sectional study was conducted with 201 older adults aged ≥65 years without a diagnosis of dementia, recruited from the community, day-care and nursing centres from Madrid. Dimensional structure was examined through bootstrap parallel analysis (PA), bootstrap exploratory graph analysis (EGA), and factor analysis (FA), followed by progressive item refinement. Internal consistency, test-retest reliability in a subsample (n = 22), SEM, MDC95, floor and ceiling effects, and associations with SPPB, MoCA, Barthel Index, and HADS were assessed.

Results: The 21-item version did not reproduce the four hypothesized domains, yielding a threedimensional solution with limited stability. A nine-item version comprised two factors, Autonomy and Psychological Distress, although FA fit was limited (TLI = 0.80; RMSEA = 0.13). A low hierarchical omega (0.409) did not support a total score, whereas factor reliability was adequate (ω = 0.840 and 0.785). Test-retest ICCs were 0.82 and 0.78, with wide 95% CIs.

No floor or ceiling effects were observed. Autonomy correlated with SPPB (r = 0.751), MoCA (r = 0.697), and Barthel (r = 0.56), and Psychological Distress with HADS (r = 0.625).

Conclusions: The nine-item EMFRA shows preliminary evidence of a two-factor structure whose factors should be scored separately. Its temporal reliability and measurement error remain uncertain, and external validation is required before clinical implementation.

1. Introduction.

Functioning in older adults cannot be adequately characterized by chronological age or the presence of individual health conditions. People of similar ages may differ substantially in their physical and mental capacities and in their ability to perform everyday activities. The World Health Organization's framework for healthy aging defines functional ability through the interaction between intrinsic capacity and the environment in which a person lives (Beard et al., 2016). Intrinsic capacity encompasses the physical and mental capacities of an individual, with locomotion, vitality, cognition, psychological capacity, and sensory function proposed as its principal domains (Cesari et al., 2018). Functional ability has a broader meaning because it also reflects the environmental conditions that enable or restrict the expression of these capacities.

The distinction is relevant to measurement: scores describing impairments in specific capacities cannot be assumed to represent an individual's functioning across different everyday contexts.

The operationalization of these constructs remains inconsistent. Schwab et al. (2025) documented substantial variation in the definitions, dimensions, and assessment approaches used to examine functional capacity and functional ability in older populations. Such differences complicate comparisons between studies and raise questions about the meaning of summary scores obtained from heterogeneous indicators. Chang et al. (2024) developed a functional ability index incorporating intrinsic capacity and environmental characteristics, illustrating how the World Health Organization's conceptual framework can inform the selection and organization of measurement indicators. Evidence from the ICOPE-Care program further indicates that declines in intrinsic capacity occur in different combinations rather than following a uniform pattern across older adults (De Souto Barreto et al., 2024). These findings support the clinical relevance of identifying specific areas of difficulty, although the dimensions represented by an assessment instrument and the interpretation of its scores require separate justification.

The International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) provides a framework for examining functioning through body functions and structures, activities, participation, and contextual factors (World Health Organization, 2001). Its conceptual scope extends beyond the identification of impairments and recognizes that functioning is influenced by the circumstances in which activities are performed. The Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) guidance applies a related concern to clinical assessment by recommending the identification of declines in intrinsic capacity and the evaluation of social and support needs to inform subsequent care planning (World Health Organization, 2024). These frameworks provide a rationale for multidimensional assessment, but they do not prescribe a single measurement structure. The selection of domains, the relationships expected among their indicators, and the interpretation of the resulting scores remain decisions that must be justified for each assessment instrument.

The Evaluation of Multidimensional Functioning and Risks in Aging (EMFRA) scale was developed as a brief assessment of functioning and potential health-related risks in older adults.

Its original conceptual framework comprised four domains: physical function, cognitive function, emotional status, and social situation. Item development was informed by the ICF and by evidence concerning factors associated with adverse health outcomes in older populations.

An initial pool of 24 items underwent evaluation by 15 experts, followed by cognitive interviews with 10 clinicians and 10 older adults. The resulting 21-item version incorporated modifications intended to improve the relevance and comprehensibility of its content (Fierro-Marrero, Reina-Varona, Pardo-Montero, et al., 2025). This development process provided preliminary evidence of content validity for the proposed assessment framework. The empirical relationships among the items, the dimensionality of their responses, and the reliability of the resulting scores had not yet been examined.

The distinction between content validity and other sources of measurement evidence is particularly relevant to EMFRA. Content validity concerns the extent to which the content of an instrument is relevant, comprehensive, and comprehensible for its intended construct, . population, and purpose (Mokkink et al., 2025; Terwee et al., 2018). The internal structure of item responses addresses a different question: whether the observed associations among indicators support the proposed organization of the scores. These properties must be considered in relation to the instrument's conceptual development. As discussed by Boateng et al. (2018), decisions concerning item selection and dimensionality can alter the content represented by a scale. This issue deserves attention when an instrument combines indicators of clinically distinct aspects of functioning. Limited correlations between items may reflect difficulties in representing a common latent dimension, but they may also arise because the indicators describe different attributes. Reducing the number of items on statistical grounds may improve the internal consistency of the retained scores while restricting their conceptual coverage. The adequacy of such decisions depends on the intended measurement model and the interpretation assigned to the resulting dimensions.

The evaluation of EMFRA scores therefore requires empirical examination of dimensionality without assuming that the four domains established during content development necessarily correspond to four latent factors. Exploratory factor analysis (FA) and exploratory graph analysis (EGA) can provide complementary information about the organization of item responses, although their findings must be interpreted in relation to the conceptual framework and the characteristics of the data (H. F. Golino & Epskamp, 2017). Reliability estimates address the consistency or stability of scores under specified measurement conditions. Their interpretation depends on the population, the score structure, and the measurement procedure.

Evidence concerning the relationships between EMFRA scores and established clinical measures can further inform their interpretation, provided that the expected associations are grounded in the constructs represented by the respective measures. These sources of evidence address distinct measurement questions and should not be treated as interchangeable (Prinsen et al., 2018).

The present study aimed to examine the psychometric properties of scores derived from the 21item EMFRA scale in older adults. The internal structure of item responses was investigated . using complementary exploratory approaches, and internal consistency and test-retest reliability were examined for the resulting scores. Score distributions, including floor and ceiling effects, were also assessed. Relationships with established measures of physical performance, cognitive functioning, emotional symptoms, and functional independence were investigated to obtain evidence relevant to score interpretation. The analyses also examined the correspondence between the empirical dimensional structure and the conceptual framework established during the development of EMFRA.

2. Methods.

>Methods.

2.1. Sample.

Participants were recruited from community and healthcare settings in the Community of Madrid, Spain. Recruitment was conducted across settings serving individuals with different health conditions, levels of functional independence, and care needs, with the aim of including a heterogeneous older adult population. Recruitment sites included Clínica Universitaria La Salle; five STIMA day-care centers located in Princesa, Embajadores, Aluche, Moratalaz, and Hortaleza; and the Valdeluz Arroyomolinos nursing home. A total of 204 older adults aged 65 years or older were recruited, of whom 201 were included in the analyses. The remaining three participants were excluded because they did not complete the assessment due to time constraints.

Eligibility was determined using two criteria: (1) age ≥65 years and (2) absence of a medically established diagnosis of dementia. No other eligibility restrictions were applied regarding pain, chronic conditions, mobility (even including subjects with wheelchair use), functional independence, or cognitive functioning. Individuals with cognitive difficulties were therefore eligible if they had no medical diagnosis of dementia.

This study followed the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Hospital Clínico San Carlos, Madrid, Spain (nº 23/398-E), with all . participants being informed about the aim and methods of the study and signing the informed consent.

2.2. Assessments.

Following written informed consent, participants provided sociodemographic information through verbally administered questions covering age, sex, educational level, years of formal education, marital status, and employment status. Anthropometric information, including height, weight, and body mass index (BMI), was also collected. Clinical history was documented using open-ended questions addressing previous or current pain, surgical procedures, and the presence of musculoskeletal, neurological, psychological, metabolic, or other health conditions. The conditions reported by participants were subsequently coded and grouped into musculoskeletal, neurological, psychological, metabolic, and other categories.

Physical functioning tests were applied including the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) (Guralnik et al., 1994), a global cognitive function measure with the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) 8.1 Spanish version (Gómez-Moreno et al., 2022; Nasreddine, 2018; Nasreddine et al., 2005), depression and anxiety traits with the Spanish version of the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) (Herrero et al., 2003; Herrmann, 1997), and independency in basic activities of daily living with the Spanish version of the Barthel Index (Cabañero-Martínez et al., 2009).

The Evaluation of Multidimensional Functioning and Risks in Aging (EMFRA) scale was also administered. The instrument combines self-reported information with cognitive and physical performance tasks, including assessments of handgrip strength and gait speed (Fierro-Marrero, Reina-Varona, Pardo-Montero, et al., 2025). The time required to complete the EMFRA assessment was recorded.

In addition, a second assessment of the EMFRA scale was provided after 2 to 10 days from the initial evaluation to explore test-retest reliability properties of the scale.

2.2.1. Response distribution of EMFRA items

A descriptive analysis of response distributions of EMFRA items was reported to check on possibly skewed responses. Mean, SD, minimum, maximum values, quantiles of (0-2 point responses) would be reported, along with the skew and kurtosis. In addition, the frequency of response categories would be reported.

2.2. Item-level test-retest reliability

Item-level test-retest reliability was evaluated in participants who completed both EMFRA assessments. For each item, contingency tables were constructed to examine the distribution of responses across the two administrations. Agreement was quantified using Gwet's weighted AC2 coefficient (Gwet, 2008a (Gwet, , 2008b)), which is less sensitive than Cohen's kappa to certain imbalances in response-category prevalence and marginal distributions (Gwet, 2008a).

Radical weighting matrices were applied to all items except Item 2 (gait speed), for which a custom weighting matrix was specified to assign greater penalties to disagreements involving the "unable to walk" category. Agreement coefficients were interpreted using the conventional classification proposed by Landis & Koch (1977), recognizing that these thresholds were originally developed for kappa statistics and were used here as descriptive guidelines.

Items exhibiting very low test-retest agreement were considered for potential exclusion during the subsequent psychometric evaluation. The weighting schemes, item-level contingency tables, and corresponding agreement estimates are reported in the Supplementary Material (SM).

2.3. Structural validity.

>Structural validity.

2.3.1. Correlation matrix, and matrix performance.

. Preliminary analyses examined the correlation structure of the EMFRA items using polychoric correlations, given the ordinal nature of the response categories and the assumption of underlying continuous variables. Under these conditions, polychoric correlations may provide more appropriate estimates of the associations between items than Pearson or Spearman correlations (Ekström, 2011a (Ekström, , 2011b;; Holgado-Tello et al., 2010; Pearson & Pearson, 1922).

Previous research has also examined their performance in dimensionality analyses involving ordinal variables (Ekström, 2011b; Markos & Tsigilis, 2024).

Pairwise 3 × 3 contingency tables were inspected to identify response-category combinations with zero frequencies. A continuity correction of 0.5 was applied when necessary. The resulting correlation matrix was examined for symmetry and positive semidefiniteness (PSD) through inspection of its eigenvalues (Zhang, 2011).

For descriptive purposes, the polychoric and anti-image matrices were reported both in full and filtered for low correlation values (-0.29 to 0.29) (Akoglu, 2018; Chan, 2003). Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) and item-level measures of sampling adequacy (MSA) were reported. In line with Lorenzo-Seva & Ferrando (2021), lower KMO or MSA values were not considered as criteria for rejecting factorability, or elimination of items, as these indices may decrease with low zeroorder correlations, low number of items, and with higher number of factors.

2.3.2. Combined dimensional structure analysis.

The dimensional structure of EMFRA was analysed using a two-way method employing a customized Horn's Parallel Analysis (PA) employing bootstrap resamples, based on factors, and the bootstrap Exploratory Graph Analysis (EGA) (Finch & Monahan, 2008; H. F. Golino & Epskamp, 2017; Horn, 1965). Horn's PA and EGA currently present the best, and similar performance among the different factorial methods for accurately detecting the number of factors in instruments with similar characteristics as EMFRA. The use of the best possible method based on the context of the research (sample size, expected number of items per factor, number of factors, correlation between factors, and type of correlation matrix, among other issues), is suggested in previous research (H. F. Golino & Epskamp, 2017). Based on this, different simulation studies indicate a good performance of the widely employed PA in multidimensional scales, as well as of EGA (H. Golino et al., 2020; H. F. Golino & Epskamp, 2017; Markos & Tsigilis, 2024). Overall, the performance of both methods is similar, and it has not yet been established in which specific context they clearly differ (H. Golino et al., 2020; H. F. Golino & Epskamp, 2017; Markos & Tsigilis, 2024). Additionally, both methods present excellent performance under simulations with asymmetrical response distributions while employing polychoric correlation matrices (Markos & Tsigilis, 2024). Authors such as Garcia-Garzon et al. (2019), suggest the possibility of employing both methods in combination. In addition, both methods would be conducted employing bootstrap resamples to consider the stability of the detected dimensions across other samples.

Both procedures incorporated bootstrap resampling to examine the stability of the estimated dimensional solutions across resamples of the observed data. Given the absence of an established decision framework for integrating the results of bootstrap PA and bootstrap EGA, dimensional retention was evaluated using four considerations: (1) convergence between the dimensional solutions identified by PA and EGA; (2) the performance of the corresponding factor-analytic solution, including global model fit and residual-based indices; (3) the stability of the EGA solution, assessed through the frequency with which the number of communities was recovered across bootstrap resamples and the structural consistency of those communities; and (4) the relative improvement of successive solutions in relation to the preceding considerations. These sources of evidence were interpreted jointly, rather than treating any single global fit index or conventional cutoff as a definitive criterion for dimensional retention.

2.3.3. Bootstrap PA and factor analysis

A customized two-step bootstrap PA procedure was conducted, given the benefits associated with considering population-based parameters (Finch & Monahan, 2008). A null eigenvalue distribution was generated through 1,000 random permutations, and a bootstrap distribution of sample eigenvalues was independently generated through 1,000 bootstrap resamples of the observed data, both computing polychoric matrices and extracting factor-based eigenvalues via the Minimum Residual method. Factor retention required that the lower bound of the bootstrap eigenvalue distribution (P5) exceeded the 95th percentile of the permuted null distribution (P95). The number of retained factors subsequently guided a FA employing the Minimum

Residual method with Oblimin rotation and 1000 iterations for parameter estimation.

Dimensional performance was evaluated using multiple indices, including the R² , chi-square statistic, TLI, RMSEA, and RMSR, following established cutoff criteria (Hu & Bentler, 1999).

Model fit was determined by combining the chi-square test (p ≥ .05 indicating excellent fit) and TLI (≥ .95 indicating excellent fit), yielding either excellent or poor model fit. Error fit was determined by combining RMSEA and RMSR, with RMSEA values ≤ .05 indicating good, ≤ .06 acceptable, and > .06 poor residual fit, and RMSR values ≤ .05 indicating good, ≤ .06 acceptable, ≤ .07 adequate, ≤ .08 marginal, and > .08 poor residual fit. When both indicators within each category converged, a single classification was assigned; when they diverged, a composite range label was reported reflecting the worst-to-best classification across indicators (e.g., poor-to-acceptable residual fit).

Item-level performance was evaluated using communalities, factor loadings, and cross-loadings obtained from the oblimin-rotated factor solution. An item was considered a candidate for elimination if it met at least one of the following criteria: (1) low factor loadings, defined as absolute loadings below 0.30 on all factors; (2) substantial cross-loadings, defined as absolute loadings of at least 0.50 on two or more factors; or (3) low communality, defined as a value below 0.30.

2.3.4. Model performance through Bootstrap and empirical EGA.

The network structure in EGA was estimated using the graphical LASSO (glasso) model, which has shown superior performance compared with the alternative TMFG network estimation method (Christensen, Garrido, Guerra-Peña, et al., 2023; H. Golino et al., 2020). Community detection was performed using the walktrap algorithm for multidimensional solutions, while the Louvain algorithm was specified for cases where a unidimensional structure was detected (Christensen, Garrido, Guerra-Peña, et al., 2023; Christensen, 2024).

Bootstrap EGA with 1,000 resampling iterations was employed to evaluate the stability of the dimensional solution. Dimensional performance was assessed through the frequency distribution of the number of communities identified across bootstrap iterations, reporting the modal solution and its difference in frequency relative to the second, third and fourth most frequent solutions. Structural consistency of the empirical EGA solution was also assessed, defined as the proportion of iterations replicating the same community assignment as in the empirical solution.

Item performance was evaluated based on: 1) replicability of each item to its empirically assigned community across bootstrap iterations, with instability defined as a replicability rate ≤0.75; 2) item-level diagnostics identifying minor dimensions (MiD), two-item dimensions (TwD), and multidimensional behaviour (MuD) (Christensen, Garrido, Guerra-Peña, et al., 2023); 3) redundancy assessed through weighted topological overlap (wTO) using the MBR method, with a cut-off of ≥0.25, in addition, to the zero-order and partial correlation between both items (Christensen, Garrido, & Golino, 2023); 4) low community loadings across all communities, defined as an absolute loading <0.30; and 5) high cross-loadings across communities, defined when both primary and secondary absolute community loadings were ≥0.50, computed via the revised net loads method with Oblimin rotation.

2.3.5. Model acceptability and item reduction criteria.

The results of FA and EGA were considered jointly to evaluate dimensional solutions and guide item reduction across successive models. Model acceptability was assessed by integrating the four sources of evidence described previously. These indicators were considered collectively, without requiring every global fit index to satisfy a fixed cutoff for dimensional retention. When the available evidence indicated limitations in a given solution, item-level diagnostic indicators were examined to identify candidates for elimination and guide subsequent model refinement.

Item reduction followed a predefined hierarchy based on the identification of unfavourable diagnostic indicators across FA and EGA. Items identified as problematic by both methods were given the highest priority for potential elimination, followed by items identified exclusively through FA and, subsequently, those identified exclusively through EGA. Identification as a candidate did not necessarily imply elimination, as decisions were made within the broader evaluation of successive dimensional solutions.

A summary table provided in SM documented the candidate items identified at each stage of model refinement, their corresponding FA and EGA diagnostic indicators, and the decisions to retain or eliminate each item.

2.3.6. Factor naming and score calculation.

The final model fulfilling the dimensional criteria (with FA and EGA) was reported with the previously mentioned measures. Additionally, factor scores for the factors identified through PA and FA were calculated employing the ten Berge method for preserving the correlation structure between latent factors, and therefore preserving the obliquity (Estabrook & Neale, 2013; Ten Berge et al., 1999). Factor scores were then calculated as linear combinations of item responses and their corresponding weights. To ensure interpretability, items with negative regression weights were reverse-coded prior to score computation using the transformation 2 , so that higher scores consistently reflected higher levels of the latent construct.

Based on the loadings, a name for each detected factor was proposed based on research team consensus and a literature search.

2.4. Factor-level internal consistency.

. Internal consistency was estimated using McDonald's omega coefficient, which does not require the assumption of tau-equivalent item contributions (Shaw, 2021). Reliability was examined for the complete item set and for the subsets of items primarily associated with each retained factor. For the complete item set, a Schmid-Leiman hierarchical decomposition was used to examine the contributions of general and group factors to score reliability. The reported indices included omega total (ωt), representing the proportion of composite-score variance attributable to the common factors collectively, and hierarchical omega (ωh), representing the proportion attributable specifically to the general factor. Additional omega estimates for the general and group factors were reported to characterize their respective contributions to reliable score variance. Explained common variance (ECV) was also calculated to quantify the proportion of common variance accounted for by the general factor and to inform the assessment of essential unidimensionality.

Subscale-level internal consistency was estimated separately for the subsets of items with their primary loadings on each retained factor. Items with negative scoring weights were reversecoded for the item-subset calculations, as specified in the scoring procedure. Omega coefficients above 0.70 were interpreted as indicative of acceptable internal consistency, using this threshold as a conventional guideline rather than an absolute criterion (McNeish, 2018). 2.5. Factor-level test-retest reliability, standard error of measurement, and minimum detectable change.

Test-retest reliability was estimated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC 2,1) following the framework of Shrout and Fleiss (1979) and current reporting recommendations (Koo & Li, 2016). ICC was considered adequate when >0.75 (Fleiss, 1999). Standard error of measurement (SEM) was calculated employing formula 5.7 indicated by de Vet et al. (2011), and Minimal Detectable Change at the 95% confidence level (MDC95) was computed with SEM, the square root of the number of measurement time points (2), and the critical 97.5th percentile value of Student's t distribution with the included sample size (Vet et al., 2011).

2.6. Factor-level floor-ceiling effects.

Floor and ceiling effects would be analysed checking on whether more than a 15% of subjects reached the minimum and maximal score within the factor (McHorney & Tarlov, 1995; Vet et al., 2011).

Factor floor and ceiling effects were evaluated for each factor score under three independent, non-mutually exclusive criteria. First, exact floor and ceiling effects were considered when the observed factor score equalled exactly the minimum or maximum possible scale value. Second, floor and ceiling effects were assessed by examining whether subjects scored at the minimum (0) or maximum (2) on all items showing the highest loading on each factor. Third, factor scores falling within the lowest or highest 10% of the maximum possible scale value were also considered indicative of floor or ceiling effects. A floor or ceiling effect was considered present when more than 15% of subjects met any of these criteria (McHorney & Tarlov, 1995; Vet et al., 2011). For each criterion, the absolute and relative frequency of subjects meeting the condition was reported. Additionally, the corresponding raw score range and its equivalent percentage of the maximum possible scale score were indicated as a reference for interpreting the magnitude of each criterion.

2.7. Factor-level concurrent validity

Pairwise analyses between factor scores and relevant clinical measures would be analysed.

These would include physical function with SPPB, global cognitive function with MoCA, anxiety and depression traits separately and combined with HADS, and independency in basic ADL with the Barthel index. The relationship with these predictors and the factor score would be analysed with an Ordinal Regression Model (ORM) employing a logistic distribution. ORM provides a semi-parametric framework that does not require the outcome distribution to be continuous and is robust to extreme values when compared to ordinary least squares method (Harrell, 2015). The relationship would be accounted with each clinical measure considering the Nagelkerke R², and Pearson's linear correlation coefficient. In addition, regression coefficients (β), and bootstrap 95% percentile confidence intervals (2,000 resamples), and Spearman correlation coefficients would be reported.

3.10. Factor-level concurrent validity.

The ordinal regression analyses showed significant associations between both EMFRA factors and all clinical variables, although the strength and direction of these relationships differed notably across predictors.

For Factor 1 (Autonomy), the strongest positive association was observed with physical performance (SPPB) (R² = 0.53, r = 0.75), indicating a strong positive relationship. This suggests that a substantial proportion of the variability in autonomy is explained by lower-extremity physical function. However, this relationship may be partially influenced by mathematical coupling, as gait speed contributes to both SPPB and the Autonomy factor.

A strong positive association was also found with global cognitive function (MoCA) (R² = 0.47, r = 0.70), and a moderate one with independence in basic ADLs (Barthel Index) (R² = 0.32, r = 0.56). In contrast, depressive symptoms showed a moderate negative association (R² = 0.18, r = -0.41), while anxiety (R² = 0.02, r = -0.12) and total HADS (R² = 0.09, r = -0.29) showed negligible-to-small relationships. These findings indicate that Autonomy is primarily related to physical functioning, global cognitive function, and independence in ADL domains, with a limited contribution from emotional factors, see Table 2, and Figure 9.

The principal finding was a nine-item solution comprising two dimensions, Autonomy and Psychological Distress, which differed from the four domains proposed during instrument development. The retained factors showed internal consistency and associations with external measures consistent with the proposed interpretation. However, limitations of global FA fit, explained variance, and in the precision of test-retest estimates indicate that the psychometric evidence remains preliminary.

EMFRA was originally developed to assess physical, cognitive, emotional, and social aspects of functioning in older adults (Fierro-Marrero, Reina-Varona, Pardo-Montero, et al., 2025). Its development involved an initial pool of 24 items distributed across four domains, subsequently reduced to 21 items before the present psychometric evaluation. The empirical results did not reproduce the proposed four-dimensional organization. Instead, both bootstrap PA and bootstrap EGA converged on a three-dimensional solution with poor fit and limited dimensional stability.

This discrepancy may be particularly attributable to the limited item pool per domain, with only five-to-six items per domain, which likely provided an insufficient coverage to represent each intended dimension. Some overlap between domains may also be expected, given the interrelated nature of functioning in later life. A more robust development approach would have involved generating a larger item pool from the outset, enabling the construction of both an extended and a reduced version of the scale while better preserving the intended dimensional coverage.

Following a progressive item refinement process, the Reduced Model C composed of nine items, showed a stable structure. PA and EGA converged on a two-dimensional solution, with two communities recovered in 82.6% of bootstrap samples and structural consistency of 80.3% and 92.4% for the respective dimensions. Nevertheless, FA performance remained limited (TLI = 0.80; RMSEA = 0.13). Global fit indices have shown poor performance as criteria for.

selecting the number of factors in exploratory FA, where conventional cutoffs are not sufficiently reliable (Montoya & Edwards, 2021). This fact allows the presence of poor model fit with a consistently recovered dimensional structure, as observed in Reduced Model C. In addition, bootstrap methods in both PA and EGA allow to internally validate the model structure across possible samples. These considerations support evaluating PA and EGA convergence, dimensional stability, and FA fit jointly, but they do not justify disregarding the remaining misfit (Garcia-Garzon et al., 2019). Reduced Model C therefore represents the most defensible configuration among those examined, and its performance was considered acceptable given the exploratory stage of scale validation.

Notably, the two main factors remained relatively stable between the full 21-item model and Reduced Model C, suggesting that the detected structure was not an artefact of the item reduction process. Nevertheless, item elimination narrowed the set of indicators available to describe functioning. Indicators grouped within these factors in the full model, such as gait assistance, social interaction, balance loss, memory difficulty, concentration difficulty, and irritability, were not retained, and the four originally proposed domains are no longer represented as separate dimensions. Because the content validity evidence obtained during instrument development (Fierro-Marrero, Reina-Varona, Pardo-Montero, et al., 2025) refers to the original item set, the comprehensiveness of the nine-item version should be additionally analysed through a new content validity process (Terwee et al., 2018).

The Autonomy factor comprised gait speed, handgrip strength, socioludic activity, attentioninhibition, and money arithmetic. These indicators combine aspects of physical performance, cognitive abilities relevant to everyday tasks, and social participation. This cross-domain composition is compatible with a construct related to functional independence, particularly in instrumental ADL, although the few items will only provide a partial coverage of the whole construct assessment. A relevant feature of this factor is the integration of performance-based tests, and self-reported measures addressing everyday functioning. This may be relevant given the discordance reported between self-reported and directly measured functioning in older.

adults, where older adults may over or even underestimate their physical performance depending on certain individual features (Liu et al., 2026; Schmidle et al., 2023). Therefore, the inclusion of performance-based indicators within Autonomy may reduce such biases.

The Psychological Distress factor comprised sadness, loneliness, life satisfaction, and fatigue.

The first three indicators reflect related aspects of emotional experience and subjective wellbeing, although they do not necessarily represent identical psychological processes. Fatigue introduces a broader component whose interpretation requires particular caution. In older adults, fatigue may be associated with physical health conditions, activity limitations, and psychological symptoms (Yu et al., 2010). In the present sample, fatigue showed its strongest association with sadness (ρ = 0.52), while its association with balance loss was also notable (ρ = 0.41). This pattern suggests that the multidimensional scope of fatigue, including psychological and even physical components may contribute to Psychological Distress. In the full model, this factor additionally included balance loss, memory difficulty, concentration difficulty, and irritability, a grouping compatible with the reported co-occurrence of emotional and cognitive difficulties in older adults (Biringer et al., 2005; Dufouil et al., 1996). However, these indicators were not retained, and the narrower final configuration provides a more specific basis for interpreting the factor.

The internal consistency findings have important implications for score interpretation. Although ωt was 0.864, ωh (0.409), and ECV (0.327) indicated that a substantial proportion of the reliable variance was not attributable to a single general factor (Reise, 2012). The present results consequently provide limited support for calculating a single EMFRA score intended to represent general functioning. This is particularly relevant in geriatric assessment, where domain-specific profiling is essential for targeted assessments (Brown et al., 1988; Fierro-Marrero, Reina-Varona, Paris-Alemany, et al., 2025). Reliability was instead good for Autonomy (ω = 0.840) and acceptable for Psychological Distress (ω = 0.785), both exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.70 (McNeish, 2018), supporting the separate use of both factors. These subscale coefficients also include variance shared with the general factor (Reise, 2012), but given the weakness of this factor, most of their reliable variance is likely specific to each dimension, which is also observed through the modest interfactor correlation (Phi = -0.316) reported in SM. Finally, the proportion of total item variance explained by each factor was modest (29.7% for Autonomy and 21.1% for Psychological Distress). This is not unexpected given the multidimensional nature of both constructs and the limited number of items retained per factor in a brief screening instrument.

Test-retest reliability findings were less conclusive. Although the factor-level ICCs were 0.82 for Autonomy and 0.78 for Psychological Distress, their respective 95% CIs (0.613-0.923 and 0.532-0.901) indicate considerable uncertainty in the underlying reliability estimates. At the item level, Loneliness (AC2 = 0.46, 95%CI 0.19-0.74) and Life Satisfaction (AC2 = 0.39, 95%CI 0.12-0.67) showed the lowest agreements. They were retained because the imprecision of their estimates did not allow taking a definitive decision, and because they presented substantial loadings on Psychological Distress, so their exclusion could have substantially altered the composition of the factor.

Nevertheless, their structural contribution should not obviate their limited temporal reliability, a relevant fact for item exclusion. Regarding SEM and MDC95, Autonomy showed values of 0.187 and 0.551 respectively (approximately 7% and 20.5% of the theoretical score range) and 0.297 and 0.872 respectively for Psychological Distress (approximately 11% and 32%). With the current data, the latter values indicate that considerable changes over time would be required to reliably confirm any change in the factor score. Taken together the current evidence is insufficient to support the use of either factor for monitoring individual changes over time.

Associations with established measures provided initial evidence consistent with the proposed names of the factors. Autonomy showed strong associations with SPPB (r = 0.751) and MoCA (r = 0.697), together with a moderate association with Barthel (r = 0.56). These findings correspond to the physical and cognitive content of the retained indicators. However, the association with SPPB should be interpreted with caution, as gait speed contributes to both measures and this overlap may have inflated the correlation. The moderate association with.

Barthel is consistent with the partial coverage of basic ADL within Autonomy. Psychological Distress showed a stronger association with HADS (r = 0.625) than with measures of physical performance, global cognitive functioning, or independence in ADL. This pattern is compatible with the emotional content of the factor and provides preliminary evidence of differentiated relationships with external constructs.

No pronounced floor or ceiling effects were observed for either factor. This finding is relevant given the characteristics of the sample, which was predominantly composed of participants with preserved independence in ADL (Barthel score: 90.02 ± 17.84), and low levels of depressive and anxiety traits (HADS depression: 3.9 ± 3.37; HADS anxiety: 5.38 ± 3.89). In such a sample, scores could be expected to cluster near to the ceiling of Autonomy and the floor of the Psychological Distress. The absence of these effects suggests that both factors discriminate adequately among older adults with high autonomy and good psychological well-being. Future studies should examine whether this also holds in sample with a poorer health state.

4.1. Clinical implications.

The nine-item EMFRA may be useful for the preliminary characterization of functioning in settings where comprehensive geriatric assessment is difficult to implement, such as primary care. Its two-dimensional structure allows clinicians to screen for autonomy, and psychological distress separately, helping to identify which dimensions may require a more thorough assessment, and to orientate referral decisions, for instance toward physiotherapy, occupational therapy, or psychology, without substantially increasing assessment burden. In addition, combining performance-based and self-reported items may reduce the biases associated with relying on self-report alone (Liu et al., 2026; Schmidle et al., 2023). Nonetheless, diagnostic thresholds, predictive accuracy, administration time, and feasibility across settings have not yet been established. EMFRA should therefore not currently be used for individual clinical classification, nor for monitoring changes over time.

4.2. Study Limitations and Future Research

The dimensional analyses were conducted in one single sample, without validation in an external sample. Bootstrap methods, allowed to internally validate the structure, but only within the available data. Moreover, participants predominantly showed independence in ADLs and relatively good physical and cognitive functioning, and these sample characteristics may have shaped the observed structure. Both the three-factor structure of the 21-item version and the two-factor structure of the 9-item version, should therefore be examined in external samples that include older adults with greater physical dependency, cognitive impairment, or psychosocial difficulties, across different settings.

A second limitation concerns the naming of both factors. The labels Autonomy and Psychological Distress were assigned based on the contribution of each item to its factor, and on the available literature, but no formal content validation was performed. Future studies should examine whether the retained items adequately represent the proposed dimensions.

A particularly important limitation is the small subsample used to assess test-retest reliability SEM and MDC95. Only 22 participants completed the repeated assessment, which resulted in imprecise estimates. This imprecision limited decisions on whether to retain or exclude items with low reliability, and prevented a confident estimate of the minimum change in factor scores required to be considered a true change.

Finally, other psychometric properties were not examined, such as the predictive validity, responsiveness, or how the floor or ceiling effects of EMFRA compare with those of other instruments assessing the same constructs. Future research should also determine whether EMFRA can be feasibly implemented in routine healthcare settings and whether it improves clinicians' decisions about further assessment or referral.

5. Conclusions.

. The present study provides the first psychometric evidence of EMFRA.

The original 21-item version did not reproduce the four hypothesized domains but yielded a three-dimensional solution. Item refinement provided a nine-item version composed of two dimensions, Autonomy and Psychological Distress, on which bootstrap PA, and bootstrap EGA, converged and showed improved dimensional stability, although with limited fit. Both factors showed adequate internal consistency, no floor or ceiling effects, and associations with established clinical measures consistent with their interpretation. EMFRA should be scored and interpreted through its two factors separately, and not through a single total score. Test-retest reliability, SEM and MDC95 remain uncertain. Future studies should confirm the dimensional structure in an external sample, provide further psychometric properties, and evaluate its feasibility and performance across clinical settings.
