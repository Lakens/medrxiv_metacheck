How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Abstract

Ultra-processed food (UPF) intake has been linked to adverse health outcomes.

However, NOVA processing groups are hard to assign consistently, and many national food composition databases do not include them. Nutrient-based classification models could fill this gap, but their performance across countries and databases is uncertain.

FoodProX is a machine-learning classifier that predicts NOVA groups and a continuous food-processing score (FPro) from nutrient composition. To test whether such models transfer across databases, we retrained it on 182,344 mostly European Open Food Facts products. We evaluated it on 45,526 held-out Open Food Facts products and, without retraining, on 2,971 NOVA-labelled foods from a US database (FNDDS). We then applied it to the Finnish national food composition database (Fineli), which has no NOVA labels.

Four-class accuracy was 0.855 on held-out products and 0.763 in FNDDS, with errors concentrated at the NOVA 3/4 boundary. UPFs were identified well, with precision-recall performance of 0.940 and 0.964, respectively (on a 0-1 scale), well above the chance levels of 0.463 and 0.711. On the same items, our FPro scores ranked foods similarly to those of the originally released US-trained FoodProX (Spearman ρ = 0.81 in FNDDS and 0.79 in Fineli), although our scores were lower on average. Sodium and sugars were the strongest predictors. Low fibre and iron also pushed predictions towards NOVA 4. In Fineli, the model assigned 55.9% of the 4,092 scored items to NOVA 4. We found no earlier NOVA or FPro annotations for this database. Nutrient-based models are most reliable for detecting ultra-processing and ranking processing intensity, and less reliable for assigning all four NOVA groups. Experts also agree poorly when assigning NOVA groups, so part of this difficulty may lie in the NOVA definitions themselves.

Introduction

Across Europe, ultra-processed foods (UPFs) now make up a large and variable share of energy intake (12.6-39.3%), with striking between-country differences (Sweden highest, Italy lowest) [1] and market shifts even in traditionally Mediterranean-diet countries (i.e., Greece) [2]. Yet classification systems remain inconsistent in distinguishing these products from less processed ones. This matters because unclear boundaries make it harder to apply processing-based dietary guidance consistently and to compare findings across studies [3] [4] [5] [6] [7].

High UPF intake has been associated with adverse health outcomes, including diabetes, inflammation and cognitive decline [8] [9] [10] [11] https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?0jxkcO. [12] [13] [14][15] The NOVA classification, widely used for categorization, is based primarily on the nature and purpose of industrial processing rather than nutrient composition, although the two dimensions substantially overlap. For example, UPFs frequently share a nutritional profile with hyperpalatable foods, including combinations of sugar, fat, salt, and carbohydrates (32.9-50.2%, across 17 countries) [16]. At the same time, foods classified as UPFs can differ substantially in their nutritional profiles, including fortified products (e.g., vitamin D milk) and plant-based alternatives [17, 18]. Moreover, the boundary between ultra-processed (NOVA 4) and processed (NOVA 3) foods is not always clear-cut . These discrepancies raise questions about the extent to which nutrient composition alone can reliably distinguish processing categories, and whether nutrientbased classification approaches can capture the full range of NOVA groups.

Recent reviews have highlighted NOVA's limitations and called for improved reference methods and more consistent group assignments to strengthen research and policy [19, 20]. This is particularly relevant for national food composition databases, many of which lack systematic processing classification, thus limiting research into the independent contributions of food processing and nutrient composition to health outcomes. Manual assignment of NOVA groups is labour-intensive and may introduce subjective biases, complicating comparisons across datasets. Machine learning has opened new ways to classify foods. Menichetti et al. [21] developed FoodProX, a classifier that predicts NOVA groups from nutrient composition to foods using a US database. The model also generates a continuous food processing score (FPro) for each food, ranging from 0 for a food the model is confident is unprocessed (NOVA 1) to 1 for a food it is confident is ultra-processed (NOVA 4). Unlike the four NOVA groups, FPro can rank foods by degree of predicted processing, including foods within the same group. However, it remains unclear how well such nutrient-based classifications generalize across food databases from different countries, given their differences in food composition and nutrient reporting.

To address this question, we retrained Menichetti et al.'s FoodProX classifier on predominantly European Open Food Facts data, evaluated how well it transfers to heldout European and an independent US database, and applied it to the Finnish national food composition database (Fineli), which lacks NOVA classification and a processing score.

Model Architecture and Training

We trained a nutrient-based random-forest ensemble to predict the four NOVA groups using ten nutrients per 100 g from Open Food Facts [22]. Products with valid NOVA labels and complete nutrient values were split into stratified training and held-out test sets before any modelling; negative-value removal, zero replacement and log transformation were then applied to each partition. Because some NOVA groups are much rarer than others, we added synthetic examples of the rarer groups to the training set (SMOTE) [26]. The final model averages the predictions of five sub-models, each trained on a different fourfifths of the training set. Before testing on held-out data, we estimated accuracy by training on four-fifths of the training set and testing on the remaining fifth, five times in turn (fivefold cross-validation). Following the original FoodProX code, the synthetic examples were added before this split, so the fifths used for testing also contained some of them. The held-out test set was set aside before any synthetic examples were created and contains none. The primary model was evaluated on the held-out Open Food Facts test partition and, without retraining or recalibration, on the independent FNDDS 2009-10 database.

Full architecture specifications, class-balancing procedures, and sensitivity analyses are provided in Supplementary Methods S2-S4.

Comparison with the released FoodProX classifier.

We compared our Europeantrained classifier with the FoodProX classifier released by Menichetti et al. (2023) [21].

That classifier was trained on US foods (FNDDS 2009-10) and uses twelve nutrients: nine of our ten (all except energy) plus vitamin C, vitamin A and cholesterol. In FNDDS, we compared our predictions with the released classifier's predictions supplied with the data table, for the 2,971 labelled foods. In Fineli, we ran the released classifier ourselves on the 3,892 of 4,156 foods (93.6%) that report all nutrients of both panels. In both databases we report three measures of agreement: how often the two models assign the same NOVA group; how often they agree on whether a food is ultra-processed (NOVA 4 versus NOVA 1-3); and the correlation between their FPro scores (Spearman and Pearson).

Details of input harmonisation, model execution and comparison metrics are provided in Supplementary Methods S6.

Model interpretation.

We assessed the contribution of individual nutrients using permutation importance and SHAP analyses on held-out Open Food Facts data [27, 28].

Detailed procedures, model configurations, sample sizes, and interpretation metrics are reported in Supplementary Methods S8. We computed permutation importance for both model configurations (primary and sensitivity); we computed SHAP values only for the sensitivity (class-balanced) configuration, whose top-ranked nutrients match the primary model (Supplementary SR4).

Evaluation and interpretation measures

We summarised four-class classification performance using accuracy, F1 score, and recall. The reference NOVA labels are not ground truth: Open Food Facts assigns them automatically from each product's ingredient list and additives [22] and FNDDS supplied them with the original study [21]. All three measures therefore quantify agreement with these reference labels. Accuracy is the proportion of foods assigned to their reference NOVA group; recall is the proportion of foods in a reference group correctly identified by the model; and the F1 score combines recall with precision, the proportion of predicted group assignments that are correct [29]. Permutation importance measures the reduction in classification accuracy after a nutrient is randomly shuffled, whereas SHAP values describe the contribution of each nutrient to individual model predictions [27, 28]. For the binary task of distinguishing UPFs (NOVA 4) from all other foods, we report the area under the precision-recall curve (AUC-PR) and the area under the receiver-operatingcharacteristic curve (AUC-ROC). AUC-PR summarises the trade-off between precision and recall across thresholds and should be interpreted alongside the prevalence of UPFs, whereas AUC-ROC summarises discrimination independently of class prevalence [30, 31]. We assessed agreement between the European-trained and released FoodProX models using the proportion of matching assignments and correlations between continuous FPro scores. Full definitions, formulas, and implementation details are provided in Supplementary Methods S4 and S8. S6). In FNDDS, four-class and binary agreement were similarly 76.5% and 82.9%, respectively, with FPro scores correlating at Spearman (𝜌 = 0.813) (Supplementary Table S5). We do not report the original classifier's FNDDS accuracy as a benchmark because FNDDS was used in its original model development and therefore does not provide an independent comparison.

Results

Agreement with the released FoodProX classifier

Nutrient composition separates the poles of the NOVA scale, not its middle

On the held-out Open Food Facts test set (n=45,526), four-class accuracy was 0.855.

The classifier performed well for NOVA 1, NOVA 2, and NOVA 4 (F1 = 0.92, 0.91, and 0.87, respectively), but performance was weaker for NOVA 3 (F1 = 0.77; Figure 3a; Supplementary Table S2). Misclassifications were concentrated at the processed/ultraprocessed boundary: NOVA 3↔4 errors accounted for 71.6% of all errors in the held-out test set (Figure 3b). Thus, nutrient composition differentiated the less processed and ultra-processed ends of the NOVA scale more reliably than the intermediate processed category. Nutrient composition therefore carries a strong signal separating MPFs from UPFs and a weak one for the intermediate "processed" category. This same boundary is also where recall degrades most when the model is transferred to the US food supply (NOVA 3 recall 0.76 → 0.44; Figure 3a, Table S4). It is likewise where our model and the independently trained original classifier disagree most (Figure 2b), and where the five sub-models disagree most about the unlabelled Finnish foods (Supplementary Figure S7).

Discrimination of UPFs

Because the practically consequential question is usually whether a food is ultraprocessed, we also evaluated the binary task of separating NOVA 4 from NOVA groups 1-3 across all decision thresholds (Figure 4, Table S3). On the Open Food Facts test set, the AUC-PR curve was 0.9399, compared with a prevalence baseline of 0.463 (AUC-ROC = 0.9513). In FNDDS the AUC-PR was 0.9635 against a baseline of 0.7109 (AUC-ROC=0.9191). The higher FNDDS figure reflects a higher prevalence of UPFs in that database, not better discrimination: measured against its own baseline, the European held-out result is the stronger of the two. The contrast with the four-class results is informative: the model is only moderately accurate at reconstructing the full NOVA taxonomy but is a strong detector of ultra-processing specifically. When applied without retraining to FNDDS, four-class accuracy declined to 0.763, driven mainly by poorer recovery of NOVA 3 (recall 0.76 → 0.44), whereas recall for NOVA 1 and NOVA 4 changed little (0.95 → 0.88 and 0.86 → 0.82; Figure 3a). F1 fell for NOVA 1-3 (0.92 → 0.65, 0.91 → 0.58 and 0.77 → 0.44) but not for NOVA 4 (0.87 in both databases). For NOVA 1, the loss was almost entirely in precision (0.90 → 0.52), because 271 US NOVA 3 and NOVA 4 foods (9.1% of FNDDS) were assigned to NOVA 1; NOVA 2 lost both precision and recall and rests on only 54 foods. The unchanged NOVA 4 F1 reflects lower recall offset by higher precision (0.87 → 0.93), consistent with its higher prevalence in FNDDS (71% vs 46%).

Discrimination of UPFs remained high (Figure 4; Supplementary Tables S2-S4).

Application to the Finnish national food composition database

Class and score distribution. Of 4,156 Fineli items, 4,092 (98.5%) had complete values for the ten-nutrient panel and were assigned a NOVA class and FPro score. The model assigned 20.9% of scored items to NOVA 1, 2.8% to NOVA 2, 20.4% to NOVA 3, and 55.9% to NOVA 4. Mean FPro was 0.642 (median 0.738; SD 0.269), and its distribution was bimodal (Figure 6; Supplementary Figure S6), consistent with the model's tendency to separate the poles of the scale confidently.

Face validity.

Because Fineli has no reference NOVA labels, its predicted classifications cannot be evaluated against a ground truth. Their credibility therefore rests on the held-out and external validation results, agreement with the independently trained released FoodProX classifier, and qualitative face validity within the Finnish database. Food groups derived from item names showed the expected ordering of median FPro scores: fruits and berries were lowest (0.211), followed by vegetables and legumes (0.246), whereas composite dishes and ready meals (0.797) and sweets and snacks (0.867) were highest (Supplementary Figure S8). In addition, 64.8% of fats and oils were assigned to NOVA 2, compared with 2.8% of all Fineli items, consistent with NOVA's category of processed culinary ingredients.

FPro also generally tracked formulation and preparation within food families (Figure 7).

Scores started from 0.02 for raw chicken breast to 0.95-0.97 for nuggets, industrial meatballs, and cold cuts; from 0.06 for salmon fillet to 0.90 for fish fingers; and from 0.005 for rolled oats to 0.92-0.99 for oat biscuits. However, the ladders also show the limits of a nutrient-only score. Across all Fineli items named "Yoghurt, flavoured" (n = 51) and "Ice cream" (n = 27), the two families overlapped almost completely (median FPro 0.87 and 0.96; both ranging from 0.52 to 0.99), and home-made and industrial oat biscuits had the same median (0.92; n = 3 and 4). Single-item contrasts therefore depend on the recipe chosen: the ice cream in Figure 7 lies near the bottom of its family (4th percentile) and the flavoured yoghurt near the top (98th percentile). These cases show that FPro captures nutrient patterns associated with processing, rather than directly observing ingredient lists, industrial formulation, or processing operations.

Discussion

We retrained the FoodProX approach of Menichetti et al. (2023) [21] on European Open Food Facts data, evaluated it on held-out Open Food Facts products and, without retraining, on the US FNDDS database, and then applied it to the Finnish Food Composition Database (Fineli). To our knowledge, this work also provides the first NOVA and continuous food-processing-score (FPro) annotations for Fineli. Nutrient-based models reliably identified UPFs across databases, but their ability to reconstruct the full NOVA groups was limited, particularly at the processed/ultra-processed boundary.

Model classifier performance

On Open Food Facts products, the model had never seen, accuracy was 0.855 (Table S2), about six percentage points below the cross-validation estimate (0.911; Table S1).

That estimate is optimistic for two reasons. First, following the original FoodProX code, synthetic examples of the rarer NOVA groups were created before the training data were divided into fifths, so some synthetic training foods were blended from foods later used to test the model. Second, the estimate gave all four NOVA groups equal weight, whereas the held-out products keep their natural proportions; weighting the groups equally, heldout accuracy was 0.880, which closes about half of the gap. By contrast, the model identified UPFs far better than chance (AUC-PR 0.940 against a baseline of 0.463).

These results indicate that the classifier substantially outperforms the baseline and achieves higher precision-recall performance in the target NOVA classes. (Table S3, Figure 4). This performance also held when we compared the European test set (Open Food Facts (AUC-PR = 0.9399) with FNDDS (AUC-PR = 0.9635) (Table S3, Figure 4).

Interestingly, the classifier's errors were not distributed evenly across the NOVA classification but concentrated at a single boundary. Confusion between processed foods (NOVA 3) and UPFs (NOVA 4) accounted for 71.6% of all misclassifications in the test set, whereas confusion between the two ends of the scale was much less common (NOVA 1 → 4, 3.1%; NOVA 4 → 1, 7.3% of errors). The same pattern emerged across the other analyses. When transferred to the US food supply, recall for NOVA 3 fell from 0.76 to 0.44, whereas recall for NOVA 1 and NOVA 4 changed little (0.95 to 0.88 and 0.86 to 0.82; Figure 3a, Table S4). Among Finnish foods for which our European-trained model and the independently trained original classifier disagreed, 62.5% of disagreements involved the NOVA 3/4 distinction (Figure 2b). Similarly, all models' disagreement on NOVA prediction (ensemble uncertainty) in Fineli data was greatest for foods with intermediate processing scores (Figure S7). The convergence of these findings across two continents, three databases, and two independently trained models suggests that the NOVA 3/4 boundary poses a reproducible challenge for nutrient-only classification rather than reflecting an idiosyncrasy of a single training set. This difficulty follows from how NOVA defines the two groups. Processed foods (NOVA 3) are made by adding salt, sugar or oil to MPFs, as in cheese, cured meats or freshly baked bread, whereas UPFs (NOVA 4) are defined by industrial ingredients and cosmetic additives, such as emulsifiers, flavourings and colourings, with no domestic culinary equivalent [32].

These additives are used in small amounts and barely change a food's nutrient profile, and both groups tend to be high in salt, sugar or fat: a bakery loaf and a packaged loaf with emulsifiers can have almost the same nutrients yet fall on opposite sides of the boundary. Our model reflects this, as high sodium pushes foods towards both NOVA 3 and NOVA 4 (Supplementary SR4). These results corroborate evidence from experts and the public about the occasional difficulty of distinguishing NOVA groups 3 and 4 [6, 7, 33, 34].

Hence, our work shows that FoodProX can be generalized across food-composition systems and that its performance is task-dependent. Nutrient composition appears to provide a robust signal for distinguishing UPFs from less processed foods, whereas it provides a weaker basis for resolving the intermediate NOVA 3 category (and, under transfer, NOVA 2). Accordingly, the model should be interpreted as a strong detector of ultra-processing and a useful ranking tool for processing intensity, rather than as an equally reliable substitute for expert assignment across all four NOVA categories.

These findings have implications beyond food-composition database annotation. For nutritional epidemiology, a reproducible nutrient-based UPF indicator could improve the coverage and comparability of exposure assessment when ingredient lists, or expert NOVA labels are incomplete. Moreover, as argued by Romero Ferreiro et al. [35] nutrient information and the NOVA labels can be combined to better inform nutrient quality. Our results also illustrate how structured nutrient data can complement ingredient-based and language-model approaches [36, 37], particularly when product descriptions are unavailable or inconsistently recorded. However, these applications should treat modelderived annotations as probabilistic measurements rather than ground-truth labels.

Linking food processing to disease prevalence is not a simple or straightforward task, and caution is warranted [38, 39]. Hence, we should preserve uncertainty around foods whose nutrient profiles do not clearly distinguish between adjacent NOVA categories.

What FPro measures across food databases

Having established that nutrient composition supports strong UPF detection but only partial resolution of the NOVA scale, we next ask how the resulting FPro scores transfer across food databases. When applied to FNDDS without recalibration, NOVA assignment accuracy declined by approximately nine percentage points, from 0.855 to 0.763 (Table S2), whereas discrimination of UPFs remained comparatively strong (AUC-ROC = 0.919) (Table S3). Transfer also altered the error distribution: in the European evaluation, misclassifications occurred predominantly between adjacent NOVA classes, whereas in the US data, a substantial proportion of true NOVA 3 and NOVA 4 foods were assigned to NOVA 1 (Figure S4).

More importantly, transfer affected absolute FPro values more than ranking (relative order). The model systematically assigned lower FPro values to US foods than did the original classifier [21], with mean scores of 0.678 and 0.774, respectively, for the same items (Table S5). A similar downward offset was observed in Fineli, with corresponding means of 0.642 and 0.704 (Figure 2, Table S6). These differences could reflect genuine variation between food supplies, differences in reference labels, or the reduced tennutrient input panel. The present analyses cannot distinguish among these explanations.

Nonetheless, the relative ordering of foods was considerably more stable, with Spearman correlations of 0.79-0.81 between the two models (Table S5, S6). This establishes a key distinction: absolute FPro values should not be assumed directly comparable across models or databases, whereas the rank ordering of foods by processing intensity is much more robust. These results might also reflect differences in regulatory approaches between the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), the World Health Organization (WHO), and the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO). Despite coordination efforts and similarities in risk assessment procedures, differences remain in which additives are recognized as safe [40, 41] which could affect the nutrient composition of the same products. This highlights the importance of applying FoodProX across different nutrient databases.

In Fineli, the classifier assigned 55.9% of records to NOVA 4 and produced a strongly bimodal FPro distribution; ensemble uncertainty was low for most records, with 80.4% having uncertainty below the threshold (𝜎 FPro < 0.02) (Supplementary Figure S7). An important limitation is the absence of independently assigned NOVA reference labels for Fineli. Consequently, the accuracy of the resulting annotations cannot be directly established, and agreement between classifiers or low ensemble uncertainty should not be interpreted as independent validation. For example, the model assigned 64.8% of fats and oils to NOVA 2, corresponding to NOVA's category of processed culinary ingredients, and generated plausible within-family processing gradients: FPro increased from 0.02 for raw chicken breast to 0.95-0.97 for nuggets and cold cuts, and from 0.005 for rolled oats to 0.92-0.99 for oat biscuits (Figure 7). These gradients should not, however, be interpreted as direct measurements of industrial processing. Although processing can alter nutrient profiles, industrial processing is a highly complex set of operations that includes ingredient choices, additive functions, and technological steps [42] that nutrients alone may not fully capture. Our model therefore ranks foods by nutrient patterns associated with processing, not by observed processing operations or ingredient list features [21]. This approach rests on an assumption supported by earlier work: in unprocessed foods, each nutrient varies within a characteristic natural range, and processing tends to move nutrient concentrations outside that range, for example through sugar added to a flavoured yoghurt or fibre lost when grain is refined [21, 43]. However, the overlap between flavoured yoghurts and ice creams illustrates this limitation: both families span the same FPro range (0.52-0.99), so single-item contrasts such as the 0.99 and 0.67 in Figure 7 reflect individual recipes rather than a systematic difference between food types, because FPro reflects nutrient content rather than formulation or processing steps directly. Finally, the Fineli class proportions describe the database, not the Finnish diet. Each item counts once, however often it is eaten, so a rarely eaten ready meal weighs as much as rye bread or milk, and the database's coverage of composite dishes and branded products shapes the proportions further. The 55.9% of Fineli items assigned to NOVA 4 is therefore a share of foods listed, not of foods eaten, and cannot be compared with dietary estimates such as the UPF share of the diet in Finland (29.6%) [1] or the United States (53.4%) [44]. It is closer in kind to studies of the food supply, which document the dominance of UPFs in food systems and retail [45] [46] [47] [48] [49]. Estimating UPF intake in Finland would require linking the annotation to dietary intake data with consumption weights.

Processing or Nutrients?

The NOVA classification was driven by a narrow set of nutrients. Permuting sodium reduced accuracy by 18.1% relative to baseline, and permuting total sugars by 10.1%, whereas total energy and saturated fat contributed least (3.9% and 2.4%, respectively; Figure 5). Sugar was the dominant signal for the UPF probability, sodium for the unprocessed and processed probabilities, and protein for culinary ingredients (Fig S5a).

We found that two of the three components (sugar and salt) do indeed carry most of the predictive signal, but fat does not (Figure 5, S5). Most importantly, across the foods examined, the rank correlation with the UPF probability was +0.87 for sugars and +0.84 for sodium (Table S8). These findings demonstrate that the classifier relies strongly on nutrient characteristics associated with NOVA categories, rather than directly identifying the industrial processes or ingredients used in food production. The dominance of sugar and sodium is consistent with the substantial, though incomplete, overlap between ultraprocessed and hyper-palatable foods, indicating that nutrient profiles capture an important component of ultra-processing without being synonymous with it [16, 50]. In a nationally representative US study, UPFs supplied 89.7% of energy from added sugars, and greater processing has been linked to higher glycaemic responses [24, 51]. Similarly, higher UPF intake has been associated with incident hypertension [52], consistent with sodium reduction being an established strategy for hypertension prevention [53] [54] [55] [56] [57].

These findings provide a broader nutritional context for the patterns identified by our classifier, but do not establish that its predictions capture processing-related health risks independently of nutrient composition.

Lastly, iron and dietary fibre showed negative associations with predicted UPF probability, with rank correlations of -0.59 and -0.57, respectively (Table S8). These associations indicate that the model does not rely exclusively on high sugar, sodium or fat content, but also captures other differences in nutrient composition across NOVA groups. Instead, it seems to capture features consistent with degradation of the food matrix, such as lower fiber and mineral density, which are reflecting compositional changes associated with food processing [58, 59].

In our view, the NOVA Classification is reasonably contested, with good arguments for and against [60, 61]. On the one hand, not all UPFs are created equal, with a lot of variability between items and a lack of high-quality evidence for plant-based alternatives [62]. Nevertheless, UPFs and processed diets are also less likely to provide a sufficient amount of micronutrients [63], and when they do, they might cause higher energy intake [64]. One UPF study also found that vegan youth (16-24 years old) in Sweden consumed more energy and nutrients like salt [65], whereas a UK-based RCT observed that participants lost more weight on an MPF diet versus a UPF diet that matches national recommendations [12]. NOVA is also hard to apply consistently by hand: French nutrition specialists agreed poorly when classifying the same foods, even when ingredient information was given (Fleiss' κ 0.32 and 0.34, respectively, where 1 indicates full agreement) [7]. Their results corroborate recent evidence that suggests that NOVA may not the best tool to evaluate food processing [66], but without more ingredient information from the food industry, NOVA remains the easiest to apply. This limits what any evaluation of our annotation can show. However, one could test the Fineli annotation directly by having nutrition professionals, each assign a random sample of Fineli foods to NOVA groups without seeing the model's predictions. The model's agreement with such labels should then be judged against how well the professionals agree with one another.

Thus, whether we use NOVA or other methods to identify processing levels, they provide information on national dietary consumption patterns, the utility of functional health foods [70], and a link to global food system transformation [19] driven by corporate influence [67] [68] [69].

Conclusion

We retrained a nutrient-based NOVA classifier (FoodProX), tested it on European and US food databases and used it to annotate the Finnish national food composition database (Fineli). It consistently ranked foods by predicted processing intensity and detected UPFs from nutrient information but could not equally resolve all four NOVA groups. Once linked to dietary intake data, the Fineli annotation could support future nutritional epidemiological research in Finland. Our findings delineate the theoretical and practical boundaries of nutrient-only NOVA proxies. Rather than serving as universal class classifiers, these models function most reliably as binary detectors of ultra-processing and as continuous ranking tools for processing intensity. The reduced accuracy at the NOVA 3/4 boundary underscores that nutrient profiles alone cannot fully encode the procedural and additive criteria central to NOVA's definition of ultra-processing. To understand how food relates to disease risk across the spectrum from MPFs to UPFs, we need to understand its chemical complexity [70]. A key improvement for FoodProX would be to train it on food composition databases that include detailed additive and ingredient information (e.g., E170: calcium carbonate). The model's agreement with such labels should therefore be judged against how well the professionals agree with one another. Consequently, model-derived annotations should be treated as probabilistic measurements, with key boundaries interpreted as provisional and used alongside, not in place of, expert judgment and ingredient-based methods.
