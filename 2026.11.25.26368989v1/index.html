Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Abstract

Lesion location is a key predictor of post-stroke outcomes in adults, but its relevance for predicting childhood stroke outcomes is unclear. Recent developmental neuroscience work suggests that the gross spatial organization of large-scale brain systems may be generally conserved across development, raising the possibility that damage to common anatomical substrates might underlie chronic domain-level impairments after both adult-onset and childhood stroke. If so, then it should be possible to leverage adult lesion-symptom relationships to predict chronic childhood stroke outcomes. We tested the hypothesis that lesion-symptom models trained to predict sensorimotor and language impairments in patients with adult-onset brain lesions can be translated to predict chronic sensorimotor and language outcomes after childhood stroke.

We trained multivariate lesion-symptom models to predict sensorimotor (Grooved Peg-Board Test: N=463; median age=53.6, IQR=17.9 years) and language (Token Test: N=379; median age=53.2, IQR=19.4 years) impairments in patients with adult-onset brain lesions. We applied the trained models to lesion segmentations from childhood stroke survivors enrolled in the SCOUTS Study (N=469; median age=9.3, IQR=11.0 years), resulting in outcome predictions that we compared against chronic (6+ month) measures of sensorimotor and language impairment derived from the Pediatric Stroke Outcome Measure and the Recurrence and Recovery Questionnaire.

The adult lesion-symptom models generated outcome predictions that robustly correlated with pediatric impairment scores (sensorimotor: rho=0.43; language: rho=0.42; PFWE <0.001), enabled classification of pediatric impairment status (both AUC=0.76; PFWE=0.004), and afforded unique predictive power in multivariable pediatric outcome models (sensorimotor: adjusted OR=3.6, 95% CI=2.4-5.2; language: adjusted OR=3.0, 95% CI=1.67-5.30). Lesion-symptom models trained using pediatric lesion and outcome data captured robust lesion-symptom relationships (both rhocv=0.40, P<0.001), but yielded no advantage over the adult model predictions when compared directly via cross-validation in the pediatric cohort. Further, the adult and pediatric models made similar predictions when applied to the same lesion data even after adjusting for common effects of lesion size and laterality, suggesting a general convergence towards common anatomical signals present in both children and adults.

Lesion-symptom models trained in patients with adult-onset brain lesions can be translated across outcome measures and developmental stages to predict chronic sensorimotor and language impairments after childhood stroke. These results establish lesion location as an important predictor of childhood stroke outcomes, demonstrate the feasibility of translating adult lesion-symptom models for chronic outcome prediction in children, and support the broader conclusion that the critical anatomy underlying post-stroke sensorimotor and language impairments may be generally conserved across development.

Introduction

Long-term outcomes vary substantially among children who survive an arterial ischemic stroke 1- 4 , and nearly 25% of childhood stroke survivors exhibit moderate-to-severe neurological impairments two years after stroke onset 3 . This variability has been linked to multiple factors including age at stroke onset 3, 5, 6 , lesion size 7, 8 , the affected vascular distributions 6 , and the presence of underlying chronic disease conditions 2, 8, 9 . However, findings reported in the research literature are mixed and often inconsistent 9, 10 , and clinicians remain limited in their ability to predict which children will experience chronic impairments in critical domains such as sensorimotor and language function 9 , creating substantial uncertainty for patients and caregivers and potentially leading to missed opportunities for early intervention.

Lesion location is an important determinant of chronic outcomes after adult-onset stroke [11] [12] [13] [14] [15] , and machine learning models trained to capture lesion-symptom relationships in patients with adultonset brain lesions can predict outcomes in independent samples of adult stroke survivors [13] [14] [15] . However, large-scale studies linking lesion location to childhood stroke outcomes are scarce, and the value of lesion location as a predictor of childhood stroke outcomes is unclear 3, 6, 9, 10, [16] [17] [18] [19] . Given the cost and difficulty of collecting high-quality childhood stroke datasets, it would be of great benefit if adult lesion-symptom models could be directly translated to pediatric applications. While it cannot be assumed that the lesion-symptom relationships identified in adults will generalize to children due to developmental differences in brain organization and response to injury 1, 9, 10 , there is evidence that the gross spatial organization of large-scale brain networks may largely resemble that observed in adults 20 by two years of age 21 or earlier 22, 23 , suggesting that the core anatomical infrastructure underlying behaviorally relevant networks may be generally conserved from early childhood to adulthood.

Recent work by our group found that lesion locations associated with post-stroke epilepsy in adults also confer increased risk of seizures and epilepsy in children 24 , demonstrating that at least some lesion-symptom relationships are conserved across development. We also observed that lesion locations associated with chronic sensorimotor and language impairments in childhood stroke survivors appear generally consistent with those documented in adults 25 , supporting the plausibility of translating adult lesion-symptom models to pediatric applications. However, statistically significant lesion-symptom associations are not guaranteed to confer meaningful predictive utility at all [26] [27] [28] [29] [30] , much less when generalizing lesion-symptom models across clinical populations and developmental stages. Further, matched outcome measures may often not be available for adult and pediatric patient samples, making cross-outcome model generalization an additional de facto pre-requisite for successful and practically useful model translation. While our prior results are encouraging, the feasibility of generalizing this translational approach beyond pathological outcomes such as post-stroke epilepsy remains unclear and must be determined empirically, motivating the current study.

Here, using lesion and chronic outcome data obtained from the Iowa Lesion Registry and the Seizure and Children's Outcomes after Stroke (SCOUTS) study 24 , we aimed to comprehensively test the hypothesis that adult lesion-symptom models can be translated to predict chronic sensorimotor and language outcomes after childhood stroke. Specifically, we hypothesized that outcome predictions generated by applying adult lesion-symptom models to pediatric lesion data would (1) show robust correlations with chronic pediatric impairment scores, (2) enable classification of chronic pediatric impairment status, and (3) provide unique predictive value even after accounting for other factors such as age at stroke onset, left and right hemispheric lesion volumes, and time from stroke onset to chronic assessment. We also explored whether lesion-symptom models trained in adults vs. children captured common or divergent structurefunction relationships.

Materials and methods

Adult datasets

Adult datasets were obtained from the Iowa Lesion Registry. Patients were included in the Registry if they had focal acquired brain lesions, neuropsychological testing 3 months or more after the onset of the lesion, and research-quality structural neuroimaging. Patients were excluded from the Registry if they had pre-existing intellectual disability or neurological/psychiatric disorders. Patients with performance data for the Grooved Peg-Board Test (N=463) or the Token Test (N=379) were included in this study. Patients with surgical resections for epilepsy were excluded. Clinical and demographic characteristics of the adult datasets are summarized in Table 1.

Pediatric dataset

The pediatric dataset was obtained from a large multi-site pediatric cohort of 528 children (age 29 days to 18 years) with confirmed arterial ischemic stroke who were prospectively enrolled in either the VIPS 31 or VIPS II 32 studies. Chronic stroke outcomes were collected in the Seizures and Children's Outcomes after Stroke (SCOUTS) study 24 . Clinical imaging was collected and arterial ischemic stroke was centrally confirmed via the VIPS studies; long-term stroke outcomes were collected through the SCOUTS study. Neonates were not included in the parent studies (VIPS I and II). Children with chronic (>=6-month post-onset) neurological assessment data and lesion segmentations obtained from neuroimaging scans were included here (N=469). Clinical and demographic characteristics of the pediatric dataset are summarized in Table 2.

Standard protocol approvals, registrations, and patient consents

The SCOUTS study was approved by an Institutional Review Board (IRB) at Vanderbilt University Medical Center, which served as the IRB of record for all participating domestic sites. Local IRB approved the study at all participating sites outside the United States in the SCOUTS study and at each enrolling site for the VIPS and VIPSII studies. This analysis was approved by the University of Iowa Institutional Review Board (Project ID 201705278). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or parents of children, as applicable, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. In some instances, chart review was performed for follow-up data in participants that had previously consented to research, and the need for re-consenting participants was waived as the research was deemed minimal risk. Follow-up data were collected through chart review with a waiver of consent for minimal risk research, or through standardized parental questionnaires.

Cognitive and behavioral measures

Adult outcomes

The Grooved Peg-Board Test was used to define a measure of sensorimotor impairment by subtracting the t-score for the body side contralateral to the lesion from the t-score for the body side ipsilateral to the lesion, such that larger difference scores reflect worse performance for the contralateral body side. For patients with bilateral lesions, the smaller of the two t-scores was subtracted from the larger t-score. Difference scores were used to reduce the influence of interindividual performance variability while amplifying variability attributable to the lesion, providing a single impairment score that could be jointly analyzed in all patients regardless of lesion laterality. The Grooved Peg-Board Test was chosen because it is the only sensorimotor measure available for many patients in the Iowa Registry, and because it correlates with chronic sensorimotor impairment scores from the Pediatric Stroke Outcome Measure (PSOM) 33 , which was used to define the pediatric outcomes here.

The Token Test was used as a measure of language impairment. It is a sensitive measure of auditory language comprehension commonly used to screen for aphasic disorders. As noted below, expressive and receptive language scores were highly correlated in the pediatric dataset, leading us to collapse them into a single measure of general language impairment. Therefore, we used a single adult language outcome for the primary analyses. The Token Test was selected because it is well-represented among our adult patient sample, it has been shown to be highly effective for identifying aphasic patients regardless of clinical sub-type 34 , and because multivariate lesion-symptom models of Token Test performance have been previously found to implicate distributed left fronto-temporal structures consistent with the broader language system 13, 14, 35.

Pediatric outcomes

Chronic sensorimotor and language outcomes were assessed using the PSOM 33 . The PSOM is a validated measure of neurological impairment based on clinical assessment that scores deficits in five domains: right and left sensorimotor, expressive language, receptive language and cognitive/behavioral 33 . When PSOM data were not available, the related Recurrence and Recovery Questionnaire (RRQ) 36 , a parent-administered assessment that correlates strongly with the PSOM 36 , was used. Both assessments are scored on a 4-point ordinal scale. Higher scores indicate greater impairment.

We defined two primary outcomes corresponding to measures of chronic sensorimotor and language function. Mirroring the adult sensorimotor outcome, the pediatric sensorimotor outcome was defined as the sensorimotor score corresponding to the body side contralateral to the lesion; for bilateral lesions, the worst of the two scores was used. The expressive and receptive language sub-domain scores were highly correlated (Spearman rho=0.73, P<0.001), leading us to define a single summary outcome as the worst score across the two sub-domains. This allowed us to retain the 4-point score structure of the original measures, while summing or averaging the scores would not, preserving consistency with the PSOM/RRQ, which are commonly used for both clinical and research purposes, and with the sensorimotor outcome, allowing identical analysis strategies for each outcome. The "worst" language measure strongly correlated with the sum of the sub-domain scores (Spearman rho=0.99, P<0.001), consistent with a measure of general language impairment.

Neuroimaging data

Detailed descriptions of the neuroimaging data collection and image processing workflows for the adult 13, 14 and pediatric 24 cohorts are reported elsewhere.

For the pediatric cohort, clinical imaging and clinical presentation data were independently reviewed by a board-certified neuroradiologist (M.W.) and a pediatric vascular neurologist (H.J.F.) to confirm stroke. Typical pediatric stroke MRI protocols included T1, FLAIR, gradient echo, diffusion weighted imaging (DWI), and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) sequences. Lesions were manually traced in native subject space using FSL, with primary reliance on DWI and ADC. The native MRI and co-registered lesion mask were linearly aligned and non-linearly warped to an MNI152 template brain using ANTs. Enantiomorphic normalization 37 was used to improve registration for unilateral lesions, and cost-function masking 38 was used for bilateral lesions. Lesion segmentation and template registration quality was reviewed by experts, including A.D.B. and J.B., and any errors were manually corrected. Investigators involved in lesion tracing and editing were blind to the outcomes. Total and hemispheric lesion volumes were estimated as 1mm isotropic resolution voxel counts in template space since this removes variability due to factors such as head size or brain volume. Lesion segmentations were resampled to 2mm isotropic voxel resolution prior to lesion-symptom modeling analyses.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using MATLAB R2025b (The MathWorks).

Multivariate models -adult datasets

We trained separate partial least squares regression (PLSR) models to predict Grooved Peg-Board and Token Test outcomes based on lesion location using the Iowa Brain-Behavior Modeling Toolkit 35 , available at (https://github.com/jcgriffis/ibb_toolkit), similar to prior work 14, 35, 39 . Five repetitions of nested 5-fold cross-validation were used for initial model evaluation, with the inner loop used to optimize the number of PLS components, and the outer loop used to evaluate prediction performance 14, 35 . Cross-validation folds were stratified according to the quartiles of the outcomes to ensure adequate representation of impairment levels across training and test folds 14, 35 . Cross-validation performance was summarized by computing the full-sample cross-validation correlation between the average (across repetitions) test fold predictions and the true observed scores 35, 40 . A final model was trained on the full adult dataset for each outcome using five repetitions of 5-fold cross-validation to optimize the number of PLS components, and the fully-trained model was applied to the pediatric lesion data to obtain out-ofsample predictions for each child. For the Grooved Peg-Board model, all lesions were rendered symmetric by flipping across the midline to increase power, analogous to other work 25, [41] [42] [43] . For patients with bilateral lesions, the flipped lesions included voxels that were lesioned in either hemisphere.

For our primary analyses, we generated predictions using only positive model weights (i.e. voxels predicting impairment), motivated by the consideration that the "symptom-positive" anatomy aligns with the expected direction of lesion-symptom causality, and by the concern that "symptom-negative" model weights for lesion sites associated with good outcomes in the training sample may reflect overfitting driven by systematic spatial biases in the lesion locations associated with good outcomes 44 . That is, since the existence of "critical regions" where damage causes good outcomes is usually implausible 44, 45 , we focused on only voxels where damage predicted deficits. For completeness, results obtained using the full model weights are reported in the supplement.

Multivariate models -pediatric dataset

We trained and evaluated pediatric PLSR lesion-symptom models analogously to the adult models. Since this required us to model the ordinal outcomes as continuous variables, we recoded them to have equal intervals between each level. We used a regression, rather than classification, approach since this provided consistency between the adult and pediatric models and allowed the models to learn more granular lesion-symptom relationships by preserving the full range of impairment severities during training. Since only deficit-predicting voxels were used to generate the adult model predictions in the pediatric dataset, we mirrored this during cross-validation of the pediatric models to ensure that the test fold predictions could be directly compared against the adult model predictions; test fold predictions generated using only the deficit-predicting model weights were re-scaled to the appropriate score range using an intermediate linear regression model fit within the training folds. Analyses using full model weights are also reported in the supplement. As with the adult models, full-sample crossvalidation correlations were computed to summarize cross-validation performance, but we report them as both linear and rank correlations due to the ordinal outcome scales. As with the adult Grooved Peg-board model, lesions were rendered symmetric prior to training the contralateral sensorimotor outcome model.

Hypothesis-driven analyses

First, we tested whether the pediatric outcome predictions made by the adult lesion-symptom models correlated with the true pediatric outcome scores by computing Spearman rank correlations between the predicted and observed scores. We also computed partial rank correlations while covarying left and right hemispheric lesion volumes, age at stroke onset, and time from onset to neurological assessment to verify that associations could not be fully attributed to other variables. Statistical significance was evaluated using parametric P-values, and bootstrapping (1000 iterations) was used to compute 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Second, we tested whether the predictions made by the adult lesion-symptom models could be used to classify pediatric impairment status as "unimpaired" (i.e. scores of 0 or 0.5) vs. "impaired" (i.e. scores of 1 or 2). We constructed receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) curves using the adult model predictions and impairment labels, and computed the area under the curve (AUC) 28 . These analyses were repeated after regressing the same covariates described above out of the adult model predictions. Statistical significance was evaluated via permutation testing (1000 iterations), and bootstrapping (1000 iterations) was used to compute 95% CIs.

Third, we fit multivariable ordinal logistic regression models to estimate cumulative adjusted odds ratios (aOR) associated with the severity of impairments predicted by the adult models while accounting for the effects of other factors. This involved first fitting "base" ordinal logistic regression models in the pediatric sample, with the covariates described above as the initial predictor set. Then, we used likelihood ratio tests to evaluate the change in model fit resulting from adding the adult model predictions to these "base" models, and we estimated aORs along with 95% CIs for each of the standardized predictors in the models.

Since the above analyses correspond to different tests of the same research hypothesis, i.e. that adult lesion-symptom models can predict chronic childhood stroke outcomes, Bonferroni-Holm correction was used to control the family-wise error rate at 0.05 across all of the above tests.

Since we previously identified a moderating effect of age at stroke onset on the relationship between left hemispheric lesion volumes and expressive language impairments 25 , we verified that results for the language outcome did not differ when the "base" model included this moderation effect. We also explored whether age uniquely moderated the association between impairment severity predicted by the adult model and chronic language outcomes by performing an additional analysis in which an interaction term between the adult model predictions and age was added to the "base" model that already included the moderation effect for left hemisphere lesion volume, and we tested whether this improved the model fit using a likelihood ratio test.

Finally, we used a cross-validation approach to test whether training lesion-symptom models directly within the pediatric dataset conferred an advantage over simply applying the adulttrained models to the pediatric lesion data. Logistic ridge regression models classifying pediatric impairment status were trained and evaluated using an analogous cross-validation procedure as for the lesion-symptom models. Here, we used classification models, rather than regression models, since classification performance is better-aligned with how lesion-informed prognostication models would likely be applied in clinical settings 14 , but results obtained using regression approaches are reported in the supplement. For each outcome, we trained and evaluated a "base" model that included the same predictors as the "base" ordinal logistic regression models described above along with "expanded" models that additionally included either (a) the domain-matched adult lesion-symptom model predictions or (b) the test fold predictions obtained from cross-validation of the domain-matched pediatric lesion-symptom models (matched by fold and repetition). Cross-validation partitions were identical for all models, including the lesion-symptom models, to allow direct comparison of performance estimates. To reduce the impact of class imbalances, misclassification costs for impaired patients were set to be proportional to the magnitude of the class imbalance as in prior work 14, 35 . Dependent samples t-tests were used to compare average test fold AUCs between each pair of models 14, 35 . Bonferroni-Holm correction was applied to the results of the cross-validation analyses.

Post-hoc analyses

We computed partial Pearson correlations (i.e. covarying hemispheric lesion volumes) between (a) the predictions obtained by applying the adult lesion-symptom models to the pediatric lesion data and (b) the test fold predictions obtained from cross-validation of the pediatric lesionsymptom models to evaluate the similarity of model predictions generated using the same lesion inputs, and we tested whether they were more similar for domain-matched vs. domainmismatched models using Steiger z-tests. We also computed spatial correlations to evaluate the similarity of hemispheric weight topographies encoded by the adult and pediatric lesionsymptom models for the language outcome. These analyses were considered descriptive, and so they are reported with uncorrected P-values.

Results

Lesion and outcome data

Voxel-wise lesion frequencies for each adult brain lesion dataset are shown in Figure 1A, and for the pediatric stroke dataset in Figure 1B, demonstrating extensive lesion coverage for all datasets.

Roughly half (53%) of the pediatric patients exhibited at least mild impairment (i.e. PSOM > 0) on the sensorimotor outcome, while nearly one-third (27%) of the pediatric patients exhibited at least mild impairment (i.e. PSOM > 0) on the language outcome (Figure 1C). Ages at stroke onset varied widely in the pediatric stroke dataset, effectively sampling across the full childhood stroke age range (i.e. 29 days to 18 years of age; Figure 1C). Outcome assessments were mostly obtained within the first 4 years of stroke onset 25 , and 98% (n=459) of children were 2 years or older at the time of assessment.

Cross-validation of adult lesion-symptom models

We used cross-validation to evaluate the adult lesion-symptom model performance in the adult datasets. The correlations between the predicted and observed outcomes were statistically significant for both adult lesion-symptom models with moderate-to-large effect sizes (Grooved Peg-Board: rcv=0.36, P<0.001; Token Test: rcv=0.71, P<0.001), indicating that the models captured robust lesion-symptom relationships that successfully generalized to held-out patients during cross-validation. Summaries of the full weight model topographies are shown in Figure 2, emphasizing structures canonically associated with sensorimotor and language function, respectively.

Adult-to-pediatric model translation

We applied the deficit-predicting weights from the adult lesion-symptom models (Figure 3A) to the pediatric lesion data to generate predicted outcomes for each child. Results obtained using full model weights are reported in Supplementary Figure 1.

The adult model predictions for both the sensorimotor (rho=0.43, 95% CI=[0.36, 0.52], PFWE<0.001) and language (rho=0.42, 95% CI=[0.35, 0.49], PFWE<0.001) outcomes were significantly correlated with the observed outcomes (Figure 3B), even after adjusting for covariates (sensorimotor: partial rho=0.33, 95% CI=[0.24, 0.41], PFWE<0.001; language: partial rho=0.25, 95% CI=[0.17, 0.33], PFWE<0.001), and they enabled statistically significant classification of impairment status for both the sensorimotor (AUC=0.76, 95% CI=[0.71, 0.81], permutation PFWE=0.004) and language (AUC=0.76, 95% CI=[0.69, 0.81], permutation PFWE=0.004) outcomes (Figure 3C) that remained statistically significant when using the covariate-residualized adult model predictions for both the sensorimotor (AUC=0.65, 95% CI=[0.59, 0.71], permutation PFWE=0.004) and language (AUC=0.63, 95% CI=[0.55, 0.71], permutation PFWE=0.004) outcomes.

The addition of the adult model predictions to the "base" ordinal logistic regression models significantly improved model fits for both the sensorimotor (χ 2 [1]=43.70, PFWE<0.001) and language (χ 2 [1]=14.14, PFWE=0.001) outcomes, with the adult model predictions being identified as statistically significant (PFWE <= 0.001) predictors in each model (Figure 3D, Supplementary Table 1). These effects were unique to the domain-matched adult model predictions (Supplementary Figure 2), consistent with domain-specific structure-function relationships. Each standard deviation increase in impairment severity predicted by the adult model was associated with ~3-3.5x greater odds of worse impairment when holding other predictors constant (Figure 3D; full model details are reported in Supplementary Table 1).

Comparison of adult vs. pediatric lesion-symptom models

We trained lesion-symptom models in the pediatric cohort. The full-sample cross-validation correlations for the pediatric lesion-symptom models of the sensorimotor (rcv=0.42, P<0.001; rhocv=0.40, P<0.001) and language (rcv=0.41, P<0.001; rhocv=0.40, P<0.001) outcomes were statistically significant with moderate effect sizes, indicating that they exhibited good generalization to held-out patients during cross-validation.

The test fold predictions obtained from cross-validation of the pediatric lesion-symptom models significantly improved cross-validation performance over the "base" models for both the sensorimotor (t [24]=3.42, PFWE=0.01, 95% CI=[0.01, 0.03]) and language (t[24]=6.34, PFWE<0.001, 95% CI=[0.02, 0.05]) outcomes, as did the adult model predictions for both the sensorimotor (t [24]=7.81, PFWE<0.001, 95% CI=[0.04, 0.07]) and language (t[24]=6.99, PFWE<0.001, 95% CI=[0.03, 0.05]) outcomes (Figure 4). However, the improvements afforded by the pediatric lesion-symptom model predictions were significantly less than those afforded by the addition of the adult lesion-symptom model predictions for the sensorimotor (t[24]=-6.73, PFWE<0.001, 95% CI=[-0.04, -0.03]) outcome (Figure 4A), and they were also numerically less for the language outcome (Figure 4B) but this difference was not statistically significant (t[24]=-1.09, PFWE=0.29, 95% CI=[-0.006, 0.002]).

Highly similar results were obtained when using the full model weights to generate predictions (Supplementary Figure 1E), when the "base" model for the language outcome included an interaction term between left hemispheric lesion volume and age at stroke onset (Supplementary Figure 4B), and when a regression (rather than classification) approach was used (Supplementary Figure 5).

Comparison of model predictions and weight topographies

We compared predictions generated by applying the adult and pediatric models to the pediatric lesion data. Since it is possible that similar model predictions could reflect common but regionally non-specific relationships captured by the adult and pediatric models, we performed these analyses while adjusting for hemispheric lesion volumes (Figure 5). The test fold predictions generated during cross-validation of the pediatric lesion-symptom models were highly correlated with the out-of-sample predictions obtained by applying the adult lesionsymptom models to the pediatric lesion data for both the sensorimotor (partial r=0.76, P<0.001) and language (partial r=0.83, P<0.001) outcomes. Pediatric model predictions were more strongly correlated with the predictions generated by the domain-matched vs. domainmismatched adult models (sensorimotor: z=3.87, P<0.001; language: z=11.49, P<0.001).

For the language outcome, we were interested in whether the adult and pediatric models made similar predictions regardless of lesioned hemisphere, so we repeated these analyses separately for patients with left vs. right unilateral lesions while adjusting for affected hemisphere lesion volumes. Predictions were highly similar for patients with left unilateral lesions (partial r=0.85, P<0.001) but less similar for patients with right unilateral lesions (partial r=0.46, P<0.001), although both were still more similar for the domain-matched vs. domain-mismatched models (left: z=8.37, P<0.001; right: z=4.64, P<0.001). Notably, spatial correlations computed between model weights for voxels that were common to both language outcome models also indicated that the left hemispheric model weight topographies of the adult and pediatric models were highly similar (spatial rLH=0.72) while the right hemispheric model weight topographies were less similar (spatial rRH=0.22), consistent with the divergence in model predictions for lesions in the right hemisphere.

Discussion

Major progress has been made towards lesion-informed outcome prediction for adult-onset stroke [12] [13] [14] [15] , but much less progress has been made towards leveraging lesion location for predicting outcomes after childhood stroke 1 , likely in part due to the difficulty and cost of collecting large childhood stroke datasets with imaging data suitable for training and testing lesion-symptom models 7 . Our results show that lesion-symptom models trained using adult datasets can be directly translated to predict chronic sensorimotor and language outcomes after childhood stroke.

The relationships captured by adult lesion-symptom models enabled robust outcome predictions in children. When applied to the pediatric lesion data, the adult models generated chronic outcome predictions that correlated with true pediatric impairment ratings (Figure 3B), enabled classification of chronic impairment status (Figure 3C), and afforded unique explanatory power in multivariable outcome models (Figure 3D), with each standard deviation increase in impairment severity predicted by the adult models conferring ~3-3.5x greater odds of worse chronic outcomes in children when accounting for other factors (Figure 3D). Surprisingly, we did not observe a significant advantage for models trained directly in the pediatric cohort (Figure 4, Supplementary Figures 4 5), and when applied to the same lesion input data, the predictions made by the adult and pediatric models were generally similar to each other (Figure 5), suggesting that the adult and pediatric models largely converged on common underlying structure-function relationships despite being trained to predict different outcome measures in developmentally distinct patient samples. These results demonstrate the feasibility of translating adult lesion-symptom models to predict chronic domain-level outcomes after childhood stroke, and suggest that the critical anatomy underlying chronic sensorimotor and language impairments may be generally conserved from early childhood to adulthood.

Lesion location as a predictor of childhood stroke outcomes

Lesion location is an important predictor of chronic outcomes in adult stroke survivors [12] [13] [14] , but findings regarding its value as a predictor of chronic outcomes after childhood stroke have been mixed 9, 10 . Anterior circulation infarcts 6, 18 , infarcts that involve both small and large vascular territories 3 , infarcts of the lenticulostriate artery 6 , and infarcts affecting both cortical and subcortical structures 6, 7 have been associated with increased risk of poor outcomes after childhood stroke, but findings reported in the literature are inconclusive and lack consistency 3, 6, 7, 10, 17, 18, 46 . This may reflect, at least in part, the limited spatial resolution of lesion measures employed by previous studies on this topic, which have typically relied on very coarsegrained lesion characteristics (e.g. arterial territory, cortical vs. subcortical affection, etc.) rather than on the high-resolution measures of lesion location (i.e. voxel-wise lesion segmentations) that are standard for investigating lesion-symptom relationships in adults 10 . Indeed, we recently conducted an inferential voxel-based lesion-symptom mapping analysis of domain-level outcomes in the same pediatric cohort studied here, and we found robust voxel-level associations with different outcome domains 25 , supporting the view that the coarse-grained summary measures used by prior studies may not be sufficient to enable the identification of robust lesionsymptom associations in children 10 . Here, using both true cross-sample prediction approaches and in-sample cross-validation analyses, we consistently found that lesion location information, whether captured by the adult or pediatric models, provided unique predictive value beyond that attributable to other known risk factors such as lesion size and age at stroke onset, establishing lesion location as an important predictor of chronic childhood stroke outcomes.

Adult-to-pediatric lesion-symptom model translation

Our primary goal was to evaluate whether the structure-function relationships captured by adult lesion-symptom models can be successfully translated to predict childhood stroke outcomes, motivated in part by our recent work showing that lesion sites associated with post-stroke epilepsy in adults also confer heightened post-stroke epilepsy risk in children 24 . Here, we show that the potential for adult-to-child translation of structure-function relationships is not restricted to pathological outcomes such as epilepsy, but extends to critical domain-level neurological outcomes such as chronic sensorimotor and language impairments. This is an important finding that highlights adult-to-pediatric model translation as a straightforward and accessible approach with the potential to accelerate the development of lesion-informed outcome prediction approaches for childhood stroke and other pediatric conditions. Determining how to further improve and refine this approach is an important next step.

Ensemble modeling or "model stacking" approaches have shown promise for predicting chronic outcomes after adult-onset stroke 12, 14, 47 . One might therefore envision the development of pediatric ensemble models that combine clinical and demographic variables with lesion-driven predictions obtained from lesion-symptom models trained to predict diverse neurological/neuropsychological outcomes in adults, allowing for comprehensive mapping between adult model-predicted deficit profiles and childhood stroke outcomes. This approach would also allow for the inclusion of pediatric lesion-symptom models as they become available, and could also accommodate models based on lesion-derived connectivity features in addition to lesion location information [13] [14] [15] 47, 48 , providing a flexible and expandable framework for outcome prediction that could be iteratively updated as new datasets, models, and features become available. Alternately, parameters from deep learning models trained using adult datasets could be used to "warm start" analogous pediatric models to improve training efficiency and reduce computational burden, although very large (e.g. N>900) training datasets would be required for such approaches to offer an advantage over traditional machine learning approaches such as those used here 49 . Beyond these examples, our results support the development of novel approaches that leverage adult lesion-symptom relationships to inform pediatric outcome prediction.

Comparison of adult vs. pediatric lesion-symptom models

Surprisingly, lesion-symptom models trained directly in the pediatric cohort did not significantly outperform those trained using adult data (Figure 4, Supplementary Figures 4 5), and when added to the multivariable ordinal logistic regression models, the test fold predictions from the pediatric lesion-symptom models were less strongly associated with chronic impairments (Supplementary Figure 6). This supports the validity and robustness of the adult lesionsymptom models, since any biases related to our model training or comparison approaches should favor the pediatric models 27, 28 . While the adult models performed well in cross-validation (Figure 2), good cross-validation performance does not always confer good generalizability to independent datasets even within the same patient population (e.g. adult-to-adult) [50] [51] [52] [53] [54] . Here, the adult models were predicting out-of-sample, across clinical populations, and across outcome measures, while the pediatric models were predicting out-of-fold but within-sample, withinpopulation, and within-outcome. Nonetheless, we observed comparable performance when comparing the pediatric and adult model predictions via cross-validation in the pediatric cohort, suggesting that they independently converged on common underlying anatomical signals present in both children and adults. The observation that the pediatric and adult lesion-symptom models made highly similar predictions when applied to the same lesion inputs further supports this interpretation (Figure 5).

It is expected that the effects of injury will differ between developing vs. mature brains 1, 2, 9 , so why would the adult and pediatric models capture such similar structure-function relationships? One explanation is that the critical anatomical infrastructure underlying sensorimotor and language functions is effectively "in-place" even by early childhood, such that lesion locations producing chronic deficits in a given domain of function are generally conserved across most of the lifespan. This explanation appears largely consistent with recent developmental neuroscience work indicating that a broadly "adult-like" topographic organization of large-scale functional brain networks, including sensorimotor and language networks, is present in young children and even (to a lesser extent) in neonates [21] [22] [23] . Similarly, while limited in scale and scope, recent work on neonatal stroke has also identified lesion-symptom relationships that appear to be spatially consistent with those identified in adults 55.

Importantly, the spatial conservation of critical structures across development would not preclude the possibility that the form and/or severity of impairment following damage to those structures might vary depending on the developmental stage of relevant brain networks at the time of injury 10 . For example, there is evidence suggesting an enhanced capacity of the right hemisphere to take over language functions prior to the consolidation of "dominant" language systems in the left hemisphere 23, [56] [57] [58] . Along these lines, we recently found that the risk of expressive language deficits conferred by extensive damage to the left hemisphere increases with older age at stroke onset in the same pediatric cohort studied here 25 . However, in the current study, we did not find evidence for a separate moderating effect of age on the relationship between chronic language outcomes and the severity of language impairments predicted by the adult models, suggesting that after accounting for hemisphere-level interactions with onset age, any remaining developmental variation in the effects of lesion location on general language outcomes may be relatively minor, although this should be confirmed by future studies. Finally, we observed that while, in general, the adult and pediatric lesion-symptom models made highly similar predictions when applied to the same lesion data, they diverged with respect to the overall severity of predicted language outcomes for patients with damage to the right hemisphere (Figure 5B). We also observed that when removing shared prediction variance attributable to hemispheric lesion volumes, the correlation between the adult and pediatric model predictions was substantially lower for patients with right unilateral lesions relative to patients with left unilateral lesions, and the deficit-predicting weight topographies of the adult and pediatric models were much more similar in the left hemisphere than in the right hemisphere. While speculative, these observations suggest that the relationship between right hemispheric damage and language outcomes may differ between children vs. adults.

Consistent with this view, right hemispheric lesion volume was a statistically significant predictor in the ordinal logistic regression models of the pediatric language outcome after incorporating the adult lesion-symptom model predictions (Figure 3D, Supplementary Table 1), but not after incorporating predictions from the pediatric lesion-symptom model (Supplementary Figure 6), suggesting that the adult vs. pediatric models differed with respect to how much they captured effects of extensive right hemispheric damage on language outcomes in children. However, the adult model predictions were more strongly associated with pediatric language outcomes (Figure 3D, Supplementary Figure 6), and the pediatric "expanded" model failed to provide a significant advantage over the adult "expanded" model in cross-validation (Figure 4B, Supplementary Figure 4), raising doubts about whether the divergent right hemispheric weight topographies reflect meaningful developmental differences in the effects of right hemispheric damage on chronic language outcomes. It is possible that the observed differences instead reflect other factors such as differences in the sensitivity of the adult vs. pediatric language outcome measures to right hemispheric damage. Thus, while we cannot confidently interpret these observations, they nonetheless provide an example of how model failures or disagreements can reveal potentially interesting insights into potential brain-behavior relationships and support the generation of novel hypotheses that can be refined and tested in subsequent work 59.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, our pediatric sample did not include neonatal/perinatal stroke, and so our conclusions only apply to childhood stroke. Second, our pediatric outcome measures were derived from the sensorimotor and language domain scores of the PSOM/RRQ and therefore lack granularity. Even so, PSOM scores have been shown to exhibit robust associations with quantitative measures of neuropsychological test performance 33 , and we found that adult models trained using more granular neuropsychological outcomes were able to achieve excellent cross-outcome and cross-cohort generalization to outcome measures derived from the PSOM/RRQ. Time intervals between stroke onset and neurological assessment also varied in our pediatric cohort, and so some variability in outcomes could be attributable to variation in the interval between onset and assessment, although we attempted to mitigate this by including it as a covariate in our analyses.

Conclusions

Structure-function relationships captured by lesion-symptom models trained in patients with adult-onset brain lesions can be translated to predict chronic sensorimotor and language outcomes in childhood stroke survivors. Lesion-symptom models trained on data from adults vs. children largely converge on common underlying anatomical signals, suggesting a general conservation of critical structures from childhood to adulthood. Adult-to-pediatric model translation provides a potential means of accelerating lesion-informed outcome prediction in pediatric populations.
