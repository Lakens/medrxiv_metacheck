Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

People who reach 100 have survived risks that many others have not, but which aging-related signals remain informative for their survival? We examined whether epigenetic aging provides information beyond cognition and markers of neurodegeneration in 247 Dutch centenarians recruited for reported cognitive health. Using PacBio long-read sequencing we compared five DNA methylation clocks alongside cognitive, AD-related blood biomarker, hematological measures and neuropathological assessments. GrimAge was the only clock associated with both chronological age and mortality. Higher GrimAge acceleration robustly predicted higher mortality (HR per standard deviation = 1.41, 95% CI: 1.22 -1.63), with associations persisting after adjustment for estimated leukocyte composition and independently of MMSE. Combining GrimAge and MMSE strengthened survival stratification, with a 1.61-year difference in restricted mean survival between the lowest-and highest-risk quintiles, compared with 1.45 years for GrimAge and 1.20 years for MMSE alone. Plasma NfL was also associated with mortality but showed no detectable association with GrimAge acceleration. However, few temporally matched measurements limited assessment of their independent associations with survival. While GrimAge showed limited associations with neurodegeneration-related measures, it strongly correlated with hematological markers of aging, consistent with age-related myeloid shift. However, these did not fully account for its association with survival. Together, these findings suggest that mortality-related epigenetic signals remain informative even after exceptional survival to age 100, with blood epigenetic aging and cognition providing complementary prognostic information at extreme ages.

Introduction

Aging involves declining physiological function and increasing susceptibility to disease.

While chronological aging reflects the passage of time, biological aging reflects changes in the body's functional state and varies among individuals of the same chronological age [1] [2] [3] [4].

Individuals who reach at least 100 years, centenarians, have often escaped or delayed major age-related conditions such as cardiovascular disease, cancer and dementia, which suggests that their exceptional longevity could be supported by slower biological aging 5, 6.

Nevertheless, even among centenarians, remaining survival varies considerably. Previous work in centenarian cohorts has identified clinical markers associated with survival. In particular, those centenarians with higher scores on the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), a standard measure of cognitive function, are likely to survive longer than those with lower scores 7 . In addition, elevated levels of plasma neurofilament light chain (NfL), a biomarker of prevalent neuroaxonal injury, and loss of independence in activities of daily living (ADL) predicted shorter survival in centenarians [8] [9] [10] [11] . These measures retain prognostic value even beyond age 100.

Age related changes in DNA methylation (DNAm) have enabled the development of epigenetic clocks [12] [13] [14] [15] [16] [17] . First-generation clocks were trained primarily to predict chronological age 14, 18 whereas second-generation clocks incorporated aging-related phenotypes, including clinical measures or mortality risk 19, 20 . GrimAge, for example, combines DNAm-based surrogate scores for the abundance of seven plasma proteins including TIMP-1, Cystatin C, GDF-15, B2M, ADM, PAI-1, and Leptin, and a DNAm-based smoking pack-years indicator (PACKYRS) for their association with mortality. These proteins are linked to biological processes central to aging, including inflammation, metabolic adaptation, vascular aging, and 4 immune regulation 20 . A clock estimate that is higher or lower than expected for chronological age is described as epigenetic age acceleration or deceleration, respectively.

PhenoAge-and GrimAge-based age acceleration have been associated with physical capability and the burden of chronic age-related conditions in younger and older adult populations [21] [22] [23] . Whether epigenetic clocks retain prognostic value until extreme ages, and whether clocks with different training targets remain equally informative remains unclear.

Centenarians provide an informative setting to address these questions. Centenarians have undergone exceptional survival selection, yet face annual mortality rates of approximately 40-50% 7, 24 . Each additional year of survival represents substantial further selection, making differences in remaining lifespan particularly informative even within a narrow chronological-age range. Previous studies have reported DNAm age estimates substantially below chronological age in exceptionally long-lived individuals, although age-estimation performance and age-acceleration estimates differ across clocks [25] [26] [27] . These observations suggest that the relevance of the aging processes captured by clocks developed in younger populations may change with increasing age, which should be clearly observable in individuals who reached at least age 100.

Despite their shared exceptional longevity, centenarians vary considerably in DNAm age 27 , cognition and other survival-related measures. Comparing epigenetic clocks and examining their underlying signals alongside established survival markers can help determine which clocks remain informative for predicting survival, what biological processes they may reflect, and whether they provide complementary information to established markers.

To test this, we examined the blood methylation profile in 247 Dutch centenarians from the 100-plus Study, selected for reported cognitive health 28 . Using five epigenetic clocks, the age-based clocks Hannum and Horvath, the mortality-based clocks PhenoAge and GrimAge, and the longevity-focused clock ENCen40+ 14, [18] [19] [20] 27 , we compared the clock estimates with chronological age and assessed their associations with mortality. We then focused on GrimAge, the clock most strongly associated with mortality in this group, and examined which of its components remained associated with mortality at extreme old age. Additionally, drawing on this cohort's extensive phenotyping, including cognitive, plasma biomarker, hematological and neuropathological assessments, we assessed whether GrimAge captures mortality-related information beyond established survival markers in centenarians.

Results

1. GrimAge retains associations with chronological age and mortality in centenarians

We compared five epigenetic clocks, Hannum, Horvath, PhenoAge, GrimAge, and ENCen40+, using blood methylation profiles derived from PacBio long-read sequencing data from 247 Dutch centenarians (median age = 100 years; IQR: 100-102, Table 1), recruited into the 100-plus Study on the basis of self-reported cognitive health confirmed by a proxy 28, 29.

For associations with chronological age, survival and other phenotypes, we used principal component-based versions of Hannum, Horvath, PhenoAge and GrimAge to reduce technical noise and sensitivity to missing methylation measurements 30 , while ENCen40+ was calculated using its original implementation. When analysing the component surrogates, we used the original GrimAge implementation.

6

Baseline blood collection of centenarians preceded death or censoring by a median of 2.1 years (IQR, 1.0-3.2). PacBio-derived methylation estimates showed high concordance with the established EPIC array measurement (Pearson r = 0.96, Supplementary Note 1, Supplementary Figure 1).

To estimate age acceleration across a broader age range, we included 246 younger Alzheimer's Disease (AD) patients from the Amsterdam Dementia Cohort 29 sequenced alongside the centenarians (median age = 69; IQR: 62-73). GrimAge acceleration associates with hematological markers, but these do not fully explain its association with survival Because GrimAge predicted survival independently of established markers such as MMSE, we examined biological processes that might underlie its signal. GrimAge combines DNAmbased surrogates of plasma proteins and smoking exposure selected for their associations with mortality 20 . We therefore correlated GrimAge acceleration with hematological markers in 133 centenarians with both DNAm and blood measurements (Figure 3A, Supplementary Data 2).

Markers in Figure 3A are ordered based on hierarchical clustering of their intercorrelations, such that markers reflecting shared biological processes appear together (Supplementary Figure 4A, Supplementary Data 2).

Across the hematological panel, GrimAge acceleration correlated most strongly with markers consistent with the age-associated "myeloid shift," an increasing proportion of myeloid cells in peripheral blood 37, 38 . It showed a negative association with lymphocyte count (Pearson r = -0.38, adj p = 1.12E-04), together with strong positive associations with myeloid/lymphoid ratios: monocyte-to-lymphocyte (MLR; Pearson r = 0.47, adj p = 4.75E-07), immature granulocyte-to-lymphocyte (IGLR; Pearson r = 0.40, adj p = 3.13E-04), and neutrophil-tolymphocyte (NLR; Pearson r = 0.29, adj p = 3.77E-03). GrimAge acceleration also correlated positively with immature granulocyte count (Pearson r = 0.28, adj p = 1.19E-02), mean corpuscular volume (MCV; Pearson r = 0.32, adj p = 7.77E-04), and mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH; Pearson r = 0.25, adj p = 1.19E-02), and negatively with red blood cell count (RBC; Pearson r = -0.25, adj p = 1.19E-02). DNAmTL, a methylation-based estimator of leukocyte telomere length, correlated modestly and negatively with GrimAge acceleration (Pearson r = -0.24, adj p = 7.9E-04), suggesting partially distinct biological signals 39.

Using the hierarchical clustering structure (Supplementary Figure 4A, Supplementary Data 2), we selected MLR, MCV, platelet count (PLT), and DNAmTL as representatives of the major clusters because they showed the strongest associations with GrimAge acceleration within their respective clusters (see Methods). MLR, MCV, and DNAmTL remained significant in a joint regression model, suggesting partially independent contributions to GrimAge acceleration (Supplementary Figure 4B, Supplementary Data 2).

We next assessed whether these GrimAge-associated hematological markers were associated with survival in the 133 centenarians with hematological markers available. Despite their correlations with GrimAge acceleration, none of the inflammatory or myeloid-associated markers remained significant after multiple-testing correction (Figure 3B), while several methylation-estimated leukocyte fractions were associated with survival in the full cohort (n = 247; Supplementary Figure 7C). Only MLR and RBC showed nominal associations with survival (MLR: HR = 1.25, 95% CI: 1.03-1.53; RBC: HR = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.62-0.93). Given MLR's established links to immune aging and chronic inflammation 37, 38 and the nominal survival associations of MLR and RBC, we tested to what extent these markers accounted for GrimAge's survival association. In a Cox model including GrimAge acceleration and these markers (n = 126; 116 deaths), GrimAge acceleration remained associated with reduced survival (HR = 1.48, 95% CI: 1.15-1.91, p = 2.50E-03), whereas MLR (HR = 1.10, 95% CI: 0.86-1.40, p = 4.39E-01) and RBC (HR = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.65-1.01, p = 6.28E-02) did not (Supplementary Figure 4C). Thus, GrimAge acceleration associated with survival beyond these hematological markers.

Although DNAmTL correlated modestly and inversely with GrimAge acceleration, it was not associated with survival (HR = 1.08, 95% CI: 0.95-1.24, adj p = 3.71E-01; Figure 3B). This is consistent with telomere-related variation contributing only modestly to GrimAge acceleration and not accounting for its association with mortality risk in this centenarian cohort 20.

4. GrimAge acceleration shows limited association with brain aging and other age-related phenotypes

To identify phenotypes that might underlie the association between GrimAge acceleration and mortality, we tested GrimAge against a broad panel of neuropathological measures (available for n = 62 participants), cognitive performance (n = 241), ADrelated plasma biomarkers (n = 50), BMI (n = 188), education (n = 241), smoking history (n = 238) and other medical history records (n = 241) (Supplementary Note 3; Supplementary Tables 2 3 4 5 6).

After correction for multiple testing, only lower cortical Aβ40 load in Brodmann area 38 (BA38) remained significantly associated with higher GrimAge acceleration, whereas most other phenotypes, including cardiovascular conditions, showed no association, and the few nominal associations did not survive multiple-testing correction and had limited effect sizes.

GrimAge acceleration therefore showed limited detectable associations with these phenotypes in this cohort.

Discussion

Among five epigenetic clocks, GrimAge was the only clock associated with both chronological age and mortality in centenarians, and the only clock retaining a significant mortality association after leukocyte adjustment. GrimAge also remained associated with mortality independently of MMSE, demonstrating that it captures mortality-related information beyond cognitive status even after age 100.

Previous work reported epigenetic age estimates below chronological age in exceptionally long-lived individuals including semi-supercentenarians 40 . Younger patients with AD and centenarians selected for reported cognitive health represent strongly contrasting cognitive outcomes for their ages. Yet centenarians did not show downward shift in GrimAge relative to the extrapolated trend in younger AD patient. Exceptional past survival therefore need not translate into lower current values of a mortality-trained clock. We did observe a shallower cross-sectional slope within centenarians when regressing GrimAge to chronological age within centenarians compared to AD patients. This could reflect slower accumulation of methylation changes after reaching extreme old age, selective survival of individuals with lower GrimAge, or differences in clock calibration at these ages. Further studies using cognitively healthy reference cohorts and longitudinal methylation data are needed to confirm these patterns and clarify how they develop as individuals approach and survive beyond age 100.

GrimAge remained associated with mortality independently of MMSE, showing that it provides survival information independent of cognitive status. In addition, GrimAge also shows limited detectable overlap with the available neurodegeneration-related measures in this cohort. Mortality was highest among centenarians with both high GrimAge acceleration and low MMSE scores, and combining them improved survival stratification. Although both markers were associated with mortality during earlier follow-up, MMSE showed its strongest evidence of association more proximal to sample collection, whereas GrimAge acceleration showed its strongest association over longer follow-up. This pattern suggests that cognition may reflect more immediate vulnerability, while GrimAge acceleration also captures processes relevant to longer-term survival. In contrast, the BI did not retain independent predictive value once MMSE and GrimAge were included, suggesting overlapping prognostic information. One possible explanation is that physical independence partly reflects downstream consequences of cognitive impairment and systemic aging, rather than an entirely separate mortality domain in this population.

To explore which GrimAge-related signals remain informative after age 100, we examined the original clock's DNAm protein and smoking surrogates. Compared with the original GrimAge study in younger individuals, fewer DNAm components were significantly associated with mortality in centenarians 20 . The retained associations of DNAmTIMP1, DNAmB2M and DNAmCystatinC suggest links to tissue remodeling and immune-renal physiology [41] [42] [43] . Of these, only DNAmTIMP1 remained significant after leukocyte adjustment and multiple-testing correction. For other protein-surrogate components, related to stress responses, vascular regulation and metabolism, we did not observe significant associations with mortality [44] [45] [46] [47] . This suggests that the physiological vulnerabilities associated with mortality may shift with aging until extreme old age, potentially reflecting selection for resilience to vascular and metabolic dysfunction among individuals reaching age 100 28, 48.

DNAmPACKYRS, the component most significantly associated with mortality in the original study 20 , was also associated with mortality in our cohort, although this association did not survive multiple-testing correction after leukocyte adjustment. Moreover, DNAmPACKYRS showed no detectable association with reported smoking exposure. Its mortality association among never-smokers in the original study suggests that it captures information beyond reported smoking 20 . Survival selection may further influence this relationship: individuals who reach 100 despite smoking may be enriched for protective factors that reduce their susceptibility to smoking-related harm. These analyses assess which individual GrimAge surrogates remain associated with mortality at extreme ages, rather than quantify their contributions to the overall clock's mortality association. These surrogate analyses provide 22 biological context for GrimAge's association with survival, although they may not fully reflect the signals captured by the principal component-based version used in our main analyses.

Unlike findings in some broader aging cohorts 21, [49] [50] [51] , GrimAge acceleration showed no detectable association with cognitive performance. Plasma biomarker and neuropathological associations were also limited, apart from an inverse association with cortical Aβ40. Despite recruitment for self-reported cognitive health confirmed by a proxy 52 , participants varied substantially in cognition at the time of testing and subsequent survival. Smoking history, education and BMI likewise showed no significant associations with GrimAge acceleration, despite reported associations in younger populations 53, 54 . Survival to age 100 may enrich for protective factors that offset risks associated with lifestyle and socioeconomic circumstances, potentially attenuating associations observed in broader aging cohorts.

GrimAge acceleration showed a clearer overlap with hematological aging, correlating with lower lymphocyte counts and higher myeloid-to-lymphocyte ratios, consistent with an agerelated myeloid shift 37, 38 . This shift may partly reflect age-related clonal hematopoiesis (ARCH), which can be extensive in centenarians, with expanded clones contributing disproportionately to myeloid cells 55, 56 . We previously documented pronounced clonal expansion in women aged 111 and 115 years, including preferential contribution to myeloid cells in the former 55, 56 . Common ARCH driver-genes DNMT3A and TET2 regulate DNA methylation, and mutations in these genes have been associated with altered methylation at clock CpGs and epigenetic age acceleration 57 . Expansion of clones with altered methylation profiles could therefore also contribute to the blood-based aging signals captured by GrimAge. Leukocyte adjustment reduced the mortality hazard ratio for GrimAge acceleration from 1.41 to 1.28 per standard deviation, although the association remained significant. The degree of attenuation could reflect a greater contribution of hematological aging at extreme old age, although this requires confirmation through direct comparisons with younger cohorts. The association also persisted after adjustment for measured MLR and RBC.

Together, these findings suggest that GrimAge at least in part overlaps with immune and hematological aging, while also capturing broader systemic aspects of aging relevant to survival at extreme old age.

High concordance between PacBio-derived and EPIC-array methylation measurements supports applying established clocks to long-read sequencing data. Long reads additionally provide sequence variants, structural variants and haplotypes, creating opportunities to connect inherited variation, clonality and allele-specific methylation with epigenetic aging 58-

60 .

A distinctive strength of our centenarian cohort is the depth of phenotyping in individuals who have survived exceptionally long yet face high subsequent mortality. Concurrent cognitive assessment and blood methylation profiling, linked to survival follow-up, hematological and plasma measurements, and post-mortem neuropathology in a subset, allowed us to examine epigenetic aging signals alongside these measures. While such linked data are rare in centenarian research, modest sample sizes limit statistical power to detect associations. For example, while plasma NfL was associated with mortality in 252 centenarians, only 50 had temporally matched plasma NfL and GrimAge measurements, limiting assessment of whether they are independent markers of mortality risk. The similar mortality associations obtained with and without AD-based calibration suggest that this result was not driven by the choice of reference cohort. However, the single-cohort design and relatively homogeneous population warrant replication in centenarian cohorts with different population backgrounds and recruitment criteria. Independent cohorts would also help assess the robustness of slope estimates, given the substantial interindividual variation and fewer observations at the oldest ages. Intercorrelations among leukocyte fractions and among GrimAge components make their independent contributions difficult to disentangle. Although these observational analyses cannot establish causal mechanisms, they generate testable biological hypotheses about the processes that remain relevant to survival after age 100.

In conclusion, GrimAge is a robust predictor of mortality in centenarians recruited for reported cognitive health and retains prognostic value independently of MMSE, with their combination strengthening survival stratification. GrimAge overlapped with hematological aging processes while showing limited associations with most brain aging-related measures, including plasma NfL. These findings indicate that peripheral epigenetic aging captures mortality-related biological changes beyond cognitive status that remain relevant even at extreme old age. The GrimAge surrogates associations suggest that the importance of mortality-related epigenetic signals shift with advancing age and increasing survival selection.

Methods

Study cohort

For DNA methylation profiling we used peripheral blood samples collected from 247 centenarians from the 100-plus Study recruited for reported cognitive health 28 . Detailed information on the 100-plus Study, including cohort design, inclusion and exclusion criteria, baseline and follow-up assessments, general characteristics, and mortality data, is available in Holstege et al. 28 . Of these, 228 (92.3%) died and 19 (7.7%) were censored. Centenarians selected for long-read sequencing were prioritized based on health and sample availability criteria, including European ancestry, unrelatedness, baseline MMSE >25, PBMC availability, and availability of familial DNA and brain donation. As a calibration cohort we used the genomes derived from peripheral blood samples from 246 Alzheimer's Disease (AD) patients, that were sequenced simultaneously with the centenarians. AD patients were selected from the Amsterdam Dementia Cohort 29 . AD samples were selected based on similar criteria, including European ancestry, unrelatedness, PBMC availability, sex balance, and brain availability, with final inclusion constrained by DNA availability. Ten offspring of centenarians, whose peripheral-blood samples were processed using the same PacBio longread sequencing pipeline, were also included.

DNAm components

DNA methylation components of GrimAge, representing estimated plasma protein levels, were computed using the meffonym package (https://github.com/perishky/meffonym). For correlation analyses, DNAm components were adjusted for chronological age by regressing out age effects prior to testing associations.

To examine the internal structure of DNAm GrimAge and its component methylation-based surrogates, we computed pairwise Pearson correlations among DNAm GrimAge, its 8 DNAm components, chronological age, and sex in the centenarian cohort. Correlation significance was assessed using two-sided tests with FDR correction. Hierarchical clustering using complete linkage on a (1 -Pearson r) distance matrix was applied to identify patterns of coregulation among components.

Methylation-based leukocyte fraction estimation

Blood cell-type proportions were estimated from PacBio HiFi-derived CpG methylation profiles. Methylation calling was performed using pb-CpG-tools (v2.3.2), and per-CpG βvalues were computed from the long-read methylation probabilities. Cell-type deconvolution was then carried out using the projectCellType_CP function and the FlowSorted.Blood.EPIC reference dataset (Bioconductor), which performs constrained projection of sample methylation profiles onto reference signatures for major leukocyte populations: CD8 T cells, CD4 T cells, natural killer cells, B cells, monocytes, and neutrophils.

Cognitive function and functional independence

Unless otherwise specified, all phenotypic measures refer to values obtained at baseline.

Global cognitive function was assessed using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE).

Additional cognitive tests were administered as described previously in Holstege et al. 28.

Missing cognitive test scores, including MMSE scores, were imputed as described in Beker et al. 7 . Only cognitive tests with more observed than imputed values in the cohort were included in the present analyses. Functional independence was assessed using the Dutch version of the Barthel Index (BI) questionnaire 62 , which evaluates independence in activities of daily living.

Unless otherwise specified, all phenotypic measures refer to values obtained at baseline.

Global cognitive function was assessed at baseline using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), which was used in the present study as a measure of general cognitive status.

Missing MMSE scores were imputed as described previously 7 . Functional independence was assessed at baseline using the Dutch version of the Barthel Index (BI) questionnaire 49 , which evaluates independence in activities of daily living.

Plasma neurofilament light chain (NfL) was available in 252 centenarians with survival follow-up. Of these, 132 also had DNA methylation, MMSE, Barthel Index, and genetic principal-component data available and were included in the direct survival comparisons involving GrimAge and the median-split NfL analysis.

Hematological markers

Hematological markers were obtained from peripheral blood samples collected at baseline in 133 centenarians and analyzed on a Sysmex XN-9000 hematology analyzer at the Central Research Laboratory of Amsterdam UMC, according to standard laboratory procedures.

Routine hematological parameters generated by the analyzer, including absolute blood cell counts and red blood cell indices, were used in the analyses. Leukocyte ratios were calculated from absolute cell counts. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) was defined as the absolute neutrophil count divided by the absolute lymphocyte count. Monocyte-tolymphocyte, immature granulocyte-to-lymphocyte, eosinophil-to-lymphocyte, and basophilto-lymphocyte ratios (MLR, IGLR, ELR, and BLR, respectively) were calculated analogously.

DNA methylation-based telomere length (DNAmTL) was estimated using the dnaMethyAge package (https://github.com/yiluyucheng/dnaMethyAge).

Neuropathological assessments

Neuropathological analyses were performed in 62 brains from centenarians included in this study. The median interval between the blood draw used for DNA methylation profiling and death was 2.45 years. Procedures for brain donation, tissue processing, neuropathological evaluation, and quantification of Aβ8-17 load have been described previously (Rohde et al.,

2025).

Height and weight were primarily self-reported, supplemented where available by measurements obtained during study visits or from medical records. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated from height and weight. Smoking history was assessed as ever versus never smoking. Passive smoke exposure during adulthood was assessed at baseline by asking whether participants had been exposed to smoke daily at home or in the workplace.

Educational attainment was self-reported using the Verhage scale, which classifies education into seven levels ranging from no formal education to completion of a university degree.

Age acceleration calculation and cohort comparison

Age acceleration was calculated as the residual from a linear regression of each clock's DNAm age estimate on chronological age across the combined centenarian and AD cohorts. Differences in GrimAge acceleration between cohorts were assessed using a Wilcoxon rank-sum test. We additionally calculated centenarians' mean deviation from GrimAge estimates predicted by the AD-only regression extrapolated to their chronological ages. The 95% confidence interval was obtained from 10,000 bootstrap iterations, resampling participants separately within each cohort and refitting the AD regression in each iteration to account for uncertainty in the reference line.

Correlation analysis

Associations between GrimAge acceleration and phenotypic measures were assessed using Pearson correlations for continuous and binary variables and Spearman correlations for ordinal variables. Neuropathological analyses were adjusted for the interval between blood collection and death, with additional adjustment for sex in brain weight analyses. Cognitive test analyses were adjusted for educational attainment, and sociodemographic and lifestyle analyses were adjusted for sex. False discovery rate correction was applied separately within each phenotype domain.

Survival analysis.

Survival time for MMSE and DNA methylation markers was defined from baseline to death or censoring (last confirmed alive).

For plasma NfL, survival time was defined from biomarker sampling to death or censoring. Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess associations with survival. Clock comparison models were adjusted for sex and the first five genetic PCs. As a sensitivity analysis independent of the AD calibration cohort, Cox models were also fitted with each unresidualized DNAm clock and chronological age entered as separate standardized terms, with adjustment for sex and the first five genetic principal components. Separately, the primary clock-comparison Cox models were additionally adjusted for methylation-estimated leukocyte fractions. Models including GrimAge, MMSE, plasma NfL, or combinations thereof were adjusted for age, sex, education, and the first five genetic PCs. Hematological survival models were adjusted for age, sex and the first five genetic PCs. Each GrimAge component was analysed in a separate Cox model adjusted for chronological age and the first five genetic PCs. These models were then additionally adjusted for methylation-estimated leukocyte fractions. Continuous predictors were standardized (z-scores), except DNAmPACKYRS in the component survival analyses, which was analysed on its original scale.

Hazard ratios represent the effect of a one-standarddeviation increase in standardized predictors and a one-unit increase in DNAmPACKYRS.

Kaplan-Meier curves were generated using the survival package in R and stratified at the median or tertiles for visualization.

RMST analysis

Restricted mean survival time (RMST) was estimated using the survRM2 package in R, with τ = 8.4 years, corresponding to the longest observed follow-up. RMST models were adjusted for sex, education, and the first five genetic PCs. Separate models were fitted for MMSE and GrimAge acceleration. Participants were ranked according to the model-derived linear predictor, and RMST was compared between the lowest-and highest-risk quintiles (20% each). A joint model including both MMSE and GrimAge acceleration was then fitted, and RMSTs were computed for the same lowest-and highest-risk quintiles of the combined score.

Clustering and regression analysis of hematological markers

Pairwise correlations among hematological markers were computed using Spearman correlation (pairwise complete observations). To identify groups of related markers independent of correlation sign, hierarchical clustering was performed on a distance matrix defined as 1-|r|, such that strongly positive and strongly negative correlations were treated as similar. Clustering was performed using average linkage, and the dendrogram was cut into four clusters. For downstream multivariable modeling, the marker most strongly correlated with GrimAge acceleration within each cluster was selected as a representative. Selected markers were standardized (z-scores) and entered into a multivariable linear regression model with GrimAge acceleration as the outcome.
