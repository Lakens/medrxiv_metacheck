Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Increased fasting insulin reflects insulin resistance and is as a precursor to type 2 diabetes and a risk factor for kidney, heart and liver diseases and mortality. Cluster-specific fasting insulin polygenic scores (PS) characterize insulin resistance mechanisms based on insulin-related genetic associations with metabolic traits, and they have not been evaluated for liver outcomes.

We calculated seven previously developed FI cluster-specific PS, including non-diabetogenic (Preserved Insulin Secretion, Elevated Insulin Secretion, Proinsulin) and diabetogenic clusters (Adiposity-driven Hyperinsulinemia, Visceral Adiposity, Lipodystrophy, Liver/Lipid) in 12,631 participants of the Hispanic Community Healthy Study/Study of Latinos (HCHS/SOL), and tested their association with type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular, kidney and liver outcomes. We also tested the association of FI clusters with indices of insulin homeostasis in additional 1,316 Hispanic individuals. Among HCHS/SOL participants (mean age 46 years, 59% women, 20% with diabetes), we validated associations of FI cluster-specific PS and the 19 traits used to generate clusters.

The Lipodystrophy cluster was associated with type 2 diabetes (p-value =3.5 x 10 -5 ). In addition, the Adiposity-driven Hyperinsulinemia cluster was associated with the hepatic steatosis index (p-value = 2.2 ×10 -17 ) and the fatty liver index (p-value = 1.7 ×10 -14 ), while the Liver/Lipid cluster with liver fibrosis (p-value = 1.5 ×10 -4 ) and lower kidney function (p-value = 5.2 x 10 -5 ). The non-diabetogenic Elevated Insulin Secretion cluster-specific PS was associated with the disposition index (p = 0.0015). These results support insulin resistance mechanisms implicated in type 2 diabetes, liver and kidney outcomes.

Methods

HCHS/SOL study description.

The HCHS/SOL is a multi-center cohort study of 16,415 Hispanics/Latinos, aged 18 to 74 years, recruited from four US field centers (Chicago, Miami, the Bronx, and San Diego) 16 . The design of the study consisted of a two-stage probability sampling of households in predefined census-block groups This study used the baseline exam (2008 to 2011) and visit 2 exam (2014-2017), which obtained clinical, behavioral, and sociodemographic assessments, and collected fasting blood and spot urine samples. Participants underwent additional survey assessments at follow-up. Serum creatinine was measured using a creatinase enzymatic method traceable to an isotope dilution mass spectrometry reference 17.

Urine albumin (mg/dl) and creatinine (g/dl) were measured using an immunoturbidometric and a creatinase enzymatic method, respectively. HbA1c concentration was measured in EDTA whole blood using a Tosoh G7 Automated HPLC Analyzer. Participants without self-reported diabetes and/or not taking antihyperglycemic medications and/or a fasting plasma glucose ≤ 8.4 mmol/L underwent a standard 75 g two-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). Fasting plasma glucose and 2h post-glucose, serum high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (HDL-C), and serum triglycerides (TG), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were measured at baseline on a Roche Modular P Chemistry Analyzer (Roche Diagnostics). Lowdensity lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) was estimated using the Friedewald's formula when TG < 4.5 mmol/L. High sensitivity C-reactive protein (CRP) was measured using a Roche Modular P Chemistry Analyzer using the immunoturbidimetric method (Roche Diagnostics Corporation, IN). The study was approved by the institutional review boards at each field center, and all subjects provided written informed consent.

HCHS/SOL clinical outcomes. eGFR was estimated from the serum creatinine-based Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation 18 . Prevalent CKD was defined based on an eGFR <60 ml/min/1.73 m 2 at baseline and microalbuminuria was defined by a urine albumin to creatinine ratio [UACR] ≥ 30 mg/g at baseline. Other outcomes were diabetes, defined by a fasting glucose ≥126 mg/dl (7 mmol/L) or use of diabetes medications; prediabetes, defined by a fasting glucose of 100-125 mg/dL (5.6-7.0 mmol/L) and/or a HbA1c of 5.7-6.4% (39-46 mmol/mol); hypertension, defined by a blood pressure ≥130/80 mm Hg; and cardiovascular outcomes, including self-reported prevalent stroke, CAD and myocardial infarction. Incident CKD at visit 2 was defined by an eGFR <60 ml/min/1.73 m 2 and an eGFR decline ≥1 ml/min per year, or UACR ≥30 mg/g at visit 2 among participants with an eGFR≥ 60 ml/min/1.73 m 2 and a UACR <30 mg/g at baseline. For incident diabetes, we included only participants without diabetes at visit 1. No participant was taking SGLT2 inhibitors or incretin drugs.

Genotyping. Participants were genotyped using the Illumina HumanOmni2.5-8v1-1 array as previously described 19 . Standard quality control was applied for variant-and sample-level quality filters. Principal components (PCs) were calculated using PC-AiR 20 . The PCs showed genetic differentiation within and among six self-identified background groups (Cuban, Dominican, Puerto Rican, Mexican, and Central and South American). We removed 56 individuals with missing genetic background group. Imputation was performed using the TOPMed freeze 8 reference panel from the TOPMed imputation server 21 , with Eagle v2.4 for phasing and Minimac4 for imputation 22, 23 . We retained variants that had an imputation R² ≥ 0.3.

Identifying type 2 diabetes vs type 1 diabetes using a GRS.

To exclude type 1 diabetes among HCHS/SOL participants, we calculated the type 1 diabetes genetic risk score 2 (GRS2) developed by Sharp et al., which includes 67 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) 11 . The GRS2 distinguishes type 1 diabetes from type 2 diabetes based on a bimodal distribution, using an approximate cutoff point of 13.5 that includes 87.8% of patients with type 2 diabetes and excludes 82.7% of type 1 diabetes patients 11 . Four SNPs (rs9405117, rs41295121, rs540653847, and rs1281943) were missing in the HCHS/SOL data. To test for unimodality, we ran Hartigan's dip test. A p-value <0.05 indicates a non-unimodal distribution 24.

Calculation of the FI clusters. We used the published weights for 110 SNPs to calculate seven FI cluster-specific PS 15 . Some SNPs contributed to multiple clusters. The alleles in these clusterspecific PS were aligned for increasing insulin effects. PS clusters were defined as Preserved IS, Elevated IS, Proinsulin, Adiposity-driven Hyperinsulinemia, Visceral Adiposity, Lipodystrophy and Liver/Lipid. For each FI cluster, the number of effect alleles for increasing FI at variants were multiplied by their respective multi-ancestry weights and summed across cluster variants to calculate each cluster-specific PS.

Statistical analysis of FI PS clusters.

We first tested each of the cluster-specific PS for associations with 19 traits used to cluster the FI SNPs: (a) among people without diabetes: fasting glucose, 2-hour glucose, HbA1c, HOMA-B and (b) overall sample: height, waist and hip circumference, waist/hip ratio, body mass index (BMI), HDL cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, triglycerides, body fat %, liver enzymes (AST, ALT), heart rate, PR interval, CRP, and urine albumin. We used linear mixed-effects models adjusted for age, sex, genetic group, center and five PCs, and random effects for census tract, household, and genetic relatedness to account for study design. Analyses for waist circumference, hip circumference, and waist/hip ratio were adjusted for BMI, while AST and ALT were natural log transformed. Associations with a p-value <3.8 x 10 -4 were considered significant based on 133 tests (19 traits by seven FI clusters).

We then tested the association of each cluster with prediabetes, type 2 diabetes and insulin treatment use. The analysis for insulin use was only tested among individuals with type 2 diabetes, and individuals with diabetes were excluded from the prediabetes analyses. We also tested additional liver measures that were not used in the original clustering):Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index for liver fibrosis 25 , hepatic steatosis index (HSI) 26 , fatty liver index (FLI) 25 and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease liver fibrosis score (NAFLD-LFS) 27 . FIB-4 and NAFLD-LFS were natural log transformed. We used the same linear mixed-effects models described above for clustering traits.

We then tested associations of each PS cluster with ten outcomes: CKD, CAD, stroke, myocardial infarction, hypertension, prediabetes, type 2 diabetes, insulin use, eGFR, and albuminuria. For the eGFR analysis, we used a linear mixed-effects model. For all categorical outcomes, we used logistic mixed-effects models. Models for CKD, eGFR, albuminuria, CAD, stroke, myocardial infarction, and hypertension were run separately by type 2 diabetes status, as well as jointly adjusting for type 2 diabetes status. The Bonferroni-adjusted significance level for all secondary outcomes was considered to be 5.1 x 10 -4 (98 tests, 21 tests for three dysglycemic outcomes and seven FI clusters, and 49 tests for seven outcomes and seven FI clusters, and 28 for four liver traits by seven FI clusters).

Analysis of measures of insulin homeostasis.

MACAD and HTN-IR study description and genotyping. MACAD and HTN-IR cohorts are two similar cohorts of Mexican American families recruited from the Los Angeles area, as described previously 28, 29 . We analyzed 1,316 individuals who did not have diabetes and who had undergone the euglycemic hyperinsulinemic clamp procedure, yielding direct measures of insulin sensitivity and insulin clearance. These participants were free of major medical illness, and none were taking glucocorticoids or other drugs that could affect glucose homeostasis. MACAD/HTN-IR participants were genotyped using the Illumina HumanOmniExpress Beadchip 30 , with subsequent imputation using 1000 Genomes phase 3. Studies in MACAD/HTN-IR were approved by Human Subjects Protection Institutional Review Boards at UCLA, the University of Southern California, the Lundquist Institute, and Cedars-Sinai Medical Center. All subjects gave written informed consent prior to participation.

Insulin homeostasis traits.

We analyzed insulin clearance, insulin secretion, insulin sensitivity, and disposition index. Insulin sensitivity and insulin clearance were measured in 1,316 non-diabetic individuals from the MACAD and HTN-IR cohorts via the euglycemic hyperinsulinemic clamp 30 . The glucose infusion rate (M value) during the last 30 minutes of steady-state glucose and insulin levels during the clamp reflects glucose uptake by all tissues (mainly insulin-mediated glucose uptake in muscle) and is directly correlated with tissue insulin sensitivity. The metabolic clearance rate of insulin (MCRI) was calculated as the insulin infusion rate divided by the insulin concentration during the steady state of the euglycemic clamp 31.

Multipoint OGTT was available in participants from MACAD (n=512). In those subjects, we calculated acute insulin secretion as the area under the curve (AUC) for insulin from baseline to 30 min over the corresponding AUC for glucose (AUC-Ins30/AUC-Glu30), which is highly correlated with first phase insulin secretion from the intravenous glucose tolerance test (r = 0.7) 32 . This AUC-based insulin secretion measure has been found to have a hyperbolic relationship with insulin sensitivity, consistent with the relationship found when insulin secretion and insulin sensitivity are measured with gold standard physiologic tests 33 . This relationship allows calculation of the disposition index, the product of insulin sensitivity and insulin secretion (M x AUC-Ins30/AUC-Glu30), which represents an index of insulin secretion that accounts for its degree of compensation for insulin resistance.

Statistical analysis.

MACAD and HTN-IR participants were analyzed together as a single dataset. Phenotypes were log-transformed (disposition index) or square root-transformed (M value, MCRI, AUC-Ins30/AUC-Glu30) to normalize their distributions. We constructed clusterspecific PSs using SNPs and weights from Sevilla-Gonzalez et al. 15 . Two SNPs (rs84473 and rs8176749) were not available and rs84473 was replaced with a proxy, rs132941 (r² =0.92).

Association between cluster-specific PS and phenotypes were evaluated using the general estimating equations method 34 assuming an exchangeable correlation structure and using the sandwich estimator of the variance to account for familial correlation. Cluster-specific PS association analyses were adjusted for age, sex, BMI, and four PCs to account for genetic variation in the data. Significance was considered a p-value < 0.002 based on testing seven clusters and four traits.

Results

Descriptive characteristics

HCHS/SOL participants mean age was 46 years, 59% were female, the average BMI was 29.8 kg/m 2 and 20% had diabetes (Table S1). The prevalence of CKD was 3% (n=322), CAD was 5% (n=639), stroke was 1% (n=184), myocardial infarction was 2% (n=279), hypertension was 25% (n=3,158), and albuminuria was 10% (n=1,299). The distribution of clinical and biomarker traits used for clustering is shown in Table S2, and the distribution of liver biomarkers is shown in Table S3.

Results for the type 1 diabetes GRS2

The type 1 diabetes GRS2 applied to the HCHS/SOL cohort had a unimodal distribution that closely matched the location and range of the type 2 diabetes individuals (Figure S1). The Hartigan's dip test for unimodality p-value was 0.99, confirming no indication of a bimodal distribution. Therefore, all participants with diabetes were considered type 2 diabetes.

Validation of FI clusters with clustering traits in HCHS/SOL Hispanics/Latinos

We first assessed whether FI clusters were associated with the cardiometabolic traits used to generate the clusters. For the Liver/lipid, Adiposity-driven Hyperinsulinemia, Lipodystrophy, and Preserved IS clusters, the results were concordant compared to associations in the published study that derived these clusters (Figure 1; Tables S4-5). The Adiposity-driven Hyperinsulinemia cluster was positively associated with increased BMI, waist to hip ratio, body fat %, natural log ALT, and HOMA-B. Conversely, the Lipodystrophy cluster was negatively associated with hip circumference, BMI, body fat % and HDL-cholesterol but positively associated with triglycerides and natural log ALT. Furthermore, the Liver/Lipid cluster was negatively associated with triglycerides, heart rate, CRP, and 2-hour glucose, and positively associated with fasting glucose. The Preserved IS cluster was negatively associated with 2-hour glucose.

Results for FI clusters with type 2 diabetes and prediabetes in HCHS/SOL Consistent with the published findings, we reproduced a negative association of the Preserved IS (p-value = 4.0 x 10 -8 ) with type 2 diabetes (Figure 2; Table S6) and the Lipodystrophy cluster-specific PS association with type 2 diabetes (p-value = 3.5 x 10 -5 ), which were consistent with prior publication (Table S7). The FI cluster-specific PSs were not associated with insulin use among participants with type 2 diabetes. Associations of clusterspecific PS were not significant for prediabetes.

Results for FI clusters with liver fat and fibrosis

We also assessed associations with measurements of liver fat and fibrosis. The Elevated IS cluster was associated with the fatty liver index (p-value = 0.001) but not the hepatic steatosis index (Table S8, Figure 3). The Liver/Lipid cluster was associated with the natural log of NAFLD-LFS (p-value = 1.5 ×10 -4 )(Table S9). Additionally, the Adiposity-driven HI cluster was significantly positively associated with both hepatic steatosis index (p-value = 2.2 ×10 -17 ) and fatty liver index (p-value = 1.7 ×10 -14 ) (Table S9).

Results for cardiovascular and kidney outcomes in HCHS/SOL

We then assessed the association of FI cluster-specific PS with cardiovascular and kidney outcomes. There were no significant associations with cardiovascular disease and hypertension (Figure S2; Table S10). For kidney outcomes, the Liver/Lipid cluster-specific PS was significantly associated with lower eGFR (p-value = 5.2 x 10 -5 )(Figure 4; Table S11).

Associations with albuminuria and CKD were not significant for cluster-specific PS.

The Elevated IS cluster-specific PS was significantly associated with log disposition index (p-value = 0.0015).

Discussion

This study contributes new knowledge on FI genetic cluster associations with prediabetes, liver outcomes, and measures of insulin homeostasis using detailed physiologic phenotyping (euglycemic hyperinsulinemia clamp and OGTT) in Hispanics/Latinos, a population at high risk for type 2 diabetes and cardiometabolic complications. We first confirmed that HCHS/SOL Hispanics/Latinos with diabetes were more likely to have type 2 diabetes versus type 1 diabetes using a type 1 diabetes PS. We then tested FI cluster PSs and were able to reproduce patterns of association of these clusters related to distinct increased insulin mechanisms with cardiometabolic traits. Therefore, we validated for the first time FI clustercardiometabolic trait associations in Hispanics/Latinos, providing support for the relevance of these clusters in this population.

Our findings include positive associations of the Adiposity-driven Hyperinsulinemia and inverse associations of the Lipodystrophy cluster-specific PS with obesity measures of BMI and body fat %. The Lipodystrophy cluster-specific PS was associated with type 2 diabetes in our study. Sevilla-González et al. described an association of the Liver/Lipid cluster with type 2 diabetes, which we could not reproduce. Among the non-diabetogenic clusters, the Preserved IS cluster-specific PS was negatively associated with type 2 diabetes, in agreement with previously reported associations. There were no significant associations with prediabetes, or associations with insulin use among participants with diabetes.

Our main findings were significant associations of with liver measures, which have not been previously reported. The Adiposity-driven HI cluster-specific PS was associated with measures of liver fat, including the fatty liver index and the hepatic steatosis index. In contrast, the Liver/Lipid cluster-specific PS was associated with measures of liver fibrosis. Overall, these results provide insights into distinct liver outcomes for two diabetogenic hyperinsulinemia clusters related to liver fat accumulation and liver fibrosis. Because these associations are from biomarker-derived liver indexes, further studies are needed to assess the long-term clinical liver outcomes related to these FI clusters.

Our study also found a significant association of the Liver/Lipid cluster-specific PS with low eGFR. These results are concordant with findings from the original publication that showed associations of the Liver/Lipid cluster-specific PS with reduced lower eGFR and CKD 15.

However, Sevilla-González et al. described additional associations of non-diabetogenic Preserved IS and Elevated IS clusters with CKD, which were not significant in our study. These kidney outcome findings will need to be further investigated in large longitudinal studies to better characterize CKD patterns and disease progression related to FI clusters, and experimental studies may uncover potential mechanisms and causal relationships, if associations are confirmed.

To gain insight into the physiologic mechanisms underlying the seven FI clusters, we assessed their association with measures of insulin sensitivity, insulin secretion, insulin clearance, and disposition index. We showed that the increased insulin secretion seen with the Elevated IS cluster could represent appropriate compensation or improved beta-cell function, a notion supported by increased disposition index. Disposition index, a measure of the beta-cell's ability to compensate for insulin resistance, is a major determinant of diabetes risk, with higher levels of disposition index indicating lower risk of diabetes. For other clusters, associations were not significant.

Our study is limited to FI clusters previously developed, which used SNPs from GWAS of FI from individuals of European ancestry. Given Hispanics/Latinos have admixture of European, African and Native American ancestries, the contribution of SNPs from African and Amerindian ancestries were not included in the cluster development. Future studies including multi-population GWAS to develop FI clusters may improve prediction of clinical outcomes, especially cardiovascular outcomes in Hispanics/Latinos, which we were not able to identify. Additional limitations are the small number of cardiovascular events in our study, which were self-reported. A limitation to euglycemic hyperinsulinemic clamp measures of insulin sensitivity and insulin clearance is that they measure these traits on a whole-body level, such that organspecific measures (e.g., hepatic insulin clearance vs kidney insulin clearance) are not available.

Conclusion. This large study of Hispanics/Latinos validated cluster-specific PS clusters of FI and associations of the Lipodystrophy cluster-specific PS with type 2 diabetes. It also identified associations of the Liver/Lipid cluster-specific PS with liver fibrosis and low eGFR, and the Adiposity-driven HI with liver fat indexes. These findings support potential hyperinsulinemia mechanisms contributing to liver and kidney diseases.
