Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. Abstract

Background/Objectives: Intensity discretization influences radiomic measurements, but its effects in optical coherence tomography (OCT) of choroidal lesions remain insufficiently characterized. We evaluated fixed bin width (FBW) and fixed bin number (FBN) strategies with different parameters to inform discretization choices for en face enhanced-depth imaging OCT (EDI-OCT) of stable choroidal nevi. Methods: Ninety-three original-domain radiomic features were extracted from 64 eyes across 11 discretization settings (FBW 2-64; FBN 8-64). We assessed feature values, eye rankings, observer reproducibility, slab-depth dependence, redundancy, and feature-set overlap. Primary absolute-agreement intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) used per-eye feature values averaged across six numeric slabs. Histogram entropy and an approximate co-occurrence sampling-density proxy characterized the discretized intensity representation. Results: Seventy-seven features depended on discretization. Median occupied gray levels decreased with increasing bin width from 54 at FBW 2 to five at FBW 25. Eye rankings were similar between neighboring settings (median Spearman 𝜌, 0.97-0.99), but less concordant in selected FBW-FBN comparisons. Median observer ICCs ranged from 0.84 to 0.91, without clear evidence of differences between settings. Feature sets overlapped less after redundancy reduction. FBW 4 and FBN 32 provided intermediate gray-level resolution and sampling-density proxies, retaining at least 16 occupied gray levels in approximately 99% and 100% of observations, respectively. Conclusions: Intensity discretization substantially alters radiomic texture values, eye rankings, and feature selection. Observer reproducibility alone is insufficient to guide discretization choice. Intensity representation, gray-level compression, and slab depth should also be considered. FBW 4 and FBN 32 emerged as exploratory candidate settings within the evaluated pipeline. External validation and assessment of clinical utility remain necessary.

1 Introduction

Choroidal nevus (CN) is the most common intraocular tumor. Histopathologic [1, 2] evidence suggests that melanocytic choroidal tumors comprise a continuous spectrum ranging from benign-appearing melanocytes to atypical-appearing melanocytes (Spindle A) aggregated to form solid lesions, [3] with an overlap between the two, making their differentiation a significant challenge. [4, 5] Although most nevi remain stable, a small subset undergoes malignant transformation into melanoma, therefore early risk stratification is critical [6, 7] and relies on multimodal imaging [8], including optical coherence tomography (OCT) [9, 10]. The imaging risk factors [8, 11, 12] currently used, such as lesion growth, presence of subretinal fluid, or orange pigment, are largely qualitative and observer dependent. Consequently, some pigmented choroidal lesions remain clinically indeterminate [4, 13]. Therefore, objective quantitative biomarkers of the internal structure of CN extracted from routine OCT images are needed to characterize these lesions reproducibly and to support early identification of lesions at risk.

Radiomics is a promising approach [14] [15] [16] [17] [18] for obtaining quantitative imaging biomarkers. It converts medical images into quantitative data based on the assumption that they contain information related to tissue biology that is imperceptible to the human eye [18] [19] [20]. In handcrafted radiomics, predefined features are extracted from a segmented region of interest (ROI) by applying mathematical formulas to the pixel intensities. These handcrafted radiomic features (HRF) include first-order, shape, and texture features, with the latter quantifying the spatial arrangement of pixel intensities and, by extension, tissue heterogeneity [17, [21] [22] [23]. Unlike deep learning approaches, in which the learned parameters vary between model instances and result in a "black box", HRF are transparent and interpretable, do not require model training, and can be applied to relatively small cohorts [24]. Radiomics is well established in oncology and radiology and is increasingly being applied in ophthalmology [25].

The reliability of HRF depends on several factors, including image acquisition, reconstruction, preprocessing and ROI segmentation [14, 26, 27]. Texture analysis is particularly dependent on intensity discretization [28, 29], as texture features are computed on matrices generated after grouping gray levels into bins. This grouping can be performed using two different approaches. Absolute discretization via fixed bin width (FBW) uses bins of a constant size, and the resulting number of bins depends on the intensity range of the ROI. In contrast, relative discretization via fixed bin number (FBN) scales bin widths to divide the total intensity range into a fixed number of intervals. Both the discretization method and its parameters can alter the resulting matrices and, consequently, feature values, redundancy, and reproducibility [27]. Too few bins discard subtle intensity differences, whereas too many generate sparse matrices that amplify noise. In other imaging modalities, such as computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and positron emission tomography (PET), recommendations for binning strategies have been proposed, commonly expressed as a number of gray levels. Practical recommendations in radiomics studies in PET have suggested choosing a bin width that yields 16-128 or 30-130 bins [30, 31]. In OCT however, image intensities are device-dependent and not standardized or calibrated across systems, and therefore recommendations from other imaging modalities cannot be directly transposed to OCT. To the best of our knowledge, the effect of different intensity discretization strategies on radiomic features extracted from OCT images of choroidal lesions has not yet been systematically investigated and has only begun to be explored in dermatological OCT [32].

The choice of intensity discretization strategy is important as it may determine which features can be reliably extracted to characterize choroidal lesions and therefore the feature set available for building subsequent radiomic signatures or predictive models. In this study, we evaluated the effect of intensity discretization on the reproducibility and stability of HRF extracted from en face enhanced-depth imaging OCT (EDI-OCT) images of stable CN, aiming to identify settings that balance information retention, matrix density, and reproducibility for subsequent analyses.

2 Objectives

The primary aim of this study was to systematically evaluate the effect of intensity discretization on the reproducibility and stability of HRF extracted from en face EDI-OCT images of stable CN and to identify settings that balance information retention, matrix density and reproducibility for these lesions.

Specifically, we compared fixed bin width (FBW) and fixed bin number (FBN) strategies to assess: their effect on feature values and on the ranking of eyes by feature value; intra-and inter-observer reproducibility, using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC); the dependence of feature values on choroidal slab depth; feature redundancy and the composition of the resulting non-redundant feature sets; and the trade-off between information retention, texture matrix density and the number of gray levels relative to the commonly advised range of 16 to 128, in order to identify settings that balance them.

3 Materials and Methods

3.1 Study design and participants

A cross-sectional study was conducted at two centers, the Departments of Ophthalmology of ULS of Santa Maria and ULS of São José, between June and October 2024. The study was approved by the institutional Bioethics Committees and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Adults with a known choroidal nevus in the posterior pole, largest basal diameter < 5 mm, thickness < 2 mm, at least 3 years of documented follow-up, and no documented growth were eligible. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy, active intraocular inflammation or infection, retinal or choroidal disease affecting image analysis, refractive error > 6 diopters, prior intraocular surgery other than cataract surgery, and poor OCT image quality (signal strength < 25 dB). All participants underwent dilated fundus examination and macular OCT and OCT angiography. Sixty-four participants fulfilled the eligibility criteria and completed the imaging protocol.

3.2 Image acquisition and ROI segmentation

OCT images were acquired after pupillary dilation using enhanced-depth imaging on a Spectralis OCT system (Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany). A 20° × 20° high-resolution volume centered on the lesion was acquired with 97 B-scans, automatic real-time averaging, and active eye tracking. En face reconstructions and image export were performed using Heidelberg Eye Explorer (HEYEX), version 2.6.4. Six contiguous 20 𝜇m-thick en face slabs were generated, with anterior boundaries positioned 20, 40, 60, 80, 100, and 120 𝜇m posterior to the lower boundary of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE)/Bruch's membrane complex. Slab labels indicate their anterior boundary; thus, the six slabs covered depth intervals of 20-40, 40-60, 60-80, 80-100, 100-120, and 120-140 𝜇m, respectively. An additional TOTAL slab extended from the anterior border of the nevus to the depth at which the lesion signal began to fade. En face images were generated using maximum-intensity projection, and anatomical coverage was assessed by visual inspection. Images were exported as RGB tagged image file format (TIFF) files followed by grayscale conversion. Brightness and contrast settings were left unchanged across images during export.

ROIs were manually segmented on the Total slab using a custom Python application (Ra-diomicsOCT) and propagated to the six depth-specific slabs, with manual adjustment when required. Three independent segmentation rounds were performed: two by the same observer separated by 2 weeks and one by a second observer. Radiomic extraction was repeated for all discretization settings using identical images and masks.

3.3 Radiomic features and discretization

Ninety-three radiomic features were extracted using PyRadiomics [33], including 18 firstorder features and 75 texture features from gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM), gray-level run-length matrix (GLRLM), gray-level size-zone matrix (GLSZM), gray-level dependence matrix (GLDM), and neighboring gray-tone difference matrix (NGTDM) families. Shape and filtered-image features were excluded. Full extraction parameters are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Eleven discretization settings were compared: seven fixed bin width settings (FBW 2, 4, 8, 16, 25, 32, and 64) and four fixed bin number settings (FBN 8, 16, 32, and 64). FBW used fixed-width intensity intervals and FBN divided each ROI intensity range into a predefined number of bins. We assessed the number of occupied gray levels, histogram entropy, and approximate GLCM density, defined as the ROI pixel count divided by the square of the number of occupied gray levels. This density measure was a proxy rather than actual matrix occupancy. Histogram entropy was used as a surrogate for the information content of the discretized intensity representation. Higher entropy indicates greater intensity diversity but does not necessarily imply greater biological or diagnostic information. Detailed quantization definitions and extraction details are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

3.4 Statistical analysis

Discretization effects were assessed using fold-changes relative to FBW 2 and Spearman rank correlations. Intra and inter-observer reproducibility was evaluated using absoluteagreement intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) [34], with the six numeric slabs summarized in the primary analysis and individual slabs and the TOTAL slab assessed separately. Features were considered reproducible when ICC was ≥ 0.80 in both the intraobserver and primary inter-observer comparisons.

Depth-related variation was assessed using the Friedman test and Kendall's W and monotonic trends, with Benjamini-Hochberg correction [35]. Reproducible features were reduced using consensus absolute Spearman correlations |𝜌| ≥ 0.90, retaining one representative per correlation group. Feature grouping and final set overlap were compared using the adjusted Rand and Jaccard indices, respectively.

4 Results

4.1 Study population

A total of 302 patients were initially identified for potential inclusion. Following medical record review, 203 patients were excluded for not meeting the predefined inclusion criteria. An additional 35 patients were excluded after clinical evaluation. Ultimately, 64 patients (mean age 71.9 ± 10.4 years; 56.3% female) were included. All patients were white. None of the patients reported new symptoms at the time of the study.

4.2 Choroidal nevus characteristics

The clinical and multimodal imaging characteristics of the choroidal nevi are summarized in Table 1. The mean largest basal diameter was 2714 ± 814.7 𝜇m, and the mean nevus thickness, measured by OCT, was 316.4 ± 140.4 𝜇m. Subretinal fluid (SRF) was present in 7 (10.9%) nevus-affected eyes, while drusen were observed in 26 (40.6%). Most lesions were flat (𝑛 = 45; 70.3%), 18 (28.1%) were dome-shaped and 1 (1.6%) was almond-shaped. Among dome-shaped lesions, the mean dome height was 173.1 ± 140.1 𝜇m.

4.3 ROI size and segmentation agreement

ROI size varied substantially between eyes with a median of 42,970 pixels (interquartile range [IQR], 27,042-61,216) in observer 1, session 1. ROI-size agreement was high for the intra-observer comparison (ICC: 0.948; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.883-0.978) and the primary inter-observer comparison (ICC: 0.948; 95% CI, 0.867-0.974).

We evaluated 11 discretization settings (Figure 1, Table 2 and Supplementary Figure S1). With FBN, the nominal bin count was fixed by design, while the number of occupied levels could be slightly lower, whereas with FBW it decreased as bin width increased. The median number of gray levels decreased from 54 at FBW 2 to 14 at FBW 8, 5 at FBW 25

4.5 Effect on feature values and rankings

Of the 93 features, 16 first-order features were independent of discretization, whereas the remaining 77 features, mainly texture features, varied across settings. Feature values changed progressively as bin width increased, with substantial changes already evident at FBW 25 (Figure 2). At this setting, median changes ranged from 8-fold for GLCM features to 160-fold for NGTDM features, and most texture features changed by more than two-fold. At FBN 64, typical fold-changes relative to FBW 2 ranged from 1.2 to 1.9 across feature families. Neighboring settings produced similar rankings of eyes. The median Spearman correlation was 0.99 for FBW 2 vs FBW 4 and 0.97 for FBN 32 vs FBN 64, but decreased when more distant settings or different discretization methods were compared (Figure 3). Feature values from FBW settings were also more strongly related to the ROI intensity range than those from FBN settings, whereas neither approach showed a strong association with mean ROI intensity.

4.6 Feature reproducibility

Median ICCs were high across all discretization settings, ranging from 0.87 to 0.91 for intra-observer and from 0.84 to 0.90 for inter-observer reproducibility (Figure 4). Differences between settings were small compared with the variability between individual features, and the paired bootstrap comparisons did not provide clear evidence of differences between settings, though this does not establish equivalence. FBN settings had a slightly higher proportion of features with ICCs ≥ 0.75 than FBW settings, although the confidence intervals overlapped. At FBW 64, where ROIs typically contained only two gray levels, NGTDM features showed poor reproducibility (ICC approximately 0.2-0.5), making this the only setting with poorly reproducible features.

Reproducibility was lower for the TOTAL slab, with median ICCs ranging from 0.76 to 0.88. FBW 4 and FBN 32 showed reproducibility within the range observed across all settings (Table 3). ICCs and concordance correlation coefficients showed similar results.

4.7 Variation with slab depth

Reproducibility varied more across slab depth than across discretization settings (Figure 5A). Median intra-observer ICCs were lowest at 40 𝜇m (0.78-0.83) and highest at 100-120 𝜇m (0.90-0.94). At 40 𝜇m, intensity range and minimum were the least reproducible first-order features (ICC 0.54 and 0.56, respectively). FBN ICCs were generally slightly lower than those for FBW. Although FBW 64 showed the highest ICCs in deeper slabs, it retained very little information.

Image characteristics also changed with depth. Mean ROI intensity decreased by 43% at 120 𝜇m relative to the 20 𝜇m slab. Entropy decreased with depth under FBW, particularly at wider bin widths, but remained relatively stable under FBN 64. Contrast decreased across all settings, by 28%-46% at 120 𝜇m. Almost all features varied significantly with depth (94-100% across settings), although the magnitude of these changes varied. Median Kendall's W ranged from 0.09 to 0.31 for FBW and from 0.24 to 0.28 for FBN. Depthrelated trends were monotonic for most features, particularly under FBW. Depth-profiles and effect-size summaries are shown in Supplementary Figures S4 and S5.

4.8 Redundancy and reproducible feature selection

Among the 77 discretization-dependent features, the number of correlation groups, defined using a consensus |𝜌| ≥ 0.90 threshold, ranged from 28 to 41 across the 11 settings (Supplementary Figure S6). The number of groups was negatively correlated with the number of occupied gray levels, Spearman 𝜌 = -0.92. FBW 4, FBW 8 and FBN 32 yielded 32, 32 and 33 groups, respectively. Feature grouping was more similar between settings with comparable numbers of gray levels (adjusted Rand index 0.67-0.83) than between settings with different numbers (0.22-0.37).

The number of features with 𝐼𝐶𝐶 ≥ 0.80 for both intra-and inter-observer comparisons ranged from 56 to 65 for FBW and from 72 to 78 for FBN. After redundancy reduction (Figure 6), the final sets contained 24-31 features for FBW and 31-34 for FBN. Overlap between settings and feature-selection frequencies are illustrated in Supplementary Figure S7. When restricted to discretization-dependent features, the median Jaccard index was 0.74 before redundancy reduction and 0.40 after reduction. For neighboring discretization settings, the index ranged from 0.89 to 0.91 before reduction and from 0.54 to 0.74 after reduction.

The final feature sets contained 5-7 first-order features, which were independent of discretization, together with texture features, most frequently from the GLSZM family.

4.9 Information, matrix density and choice of setting

Across the 11 discretization settings, histogram entropy increased as approximate matrix density decreased (Figure 7). FBN 64 retained 5.12 bits (95% CI, 5.08-5.16), compared with 4.89 bits (95% CI, 4.83-4.97) for FBW 2, with median matrix densities of 10.6 and 14.4 pixels per cell, respectively.

The setting yielding the highest histogram entropy varied according to the minimum matrix density threshold. FBN 64 was selected at a threshold of 5 pixels per cell, FBW 4 at 50 pixels per cell, FBN 16 at 100, FBN 8 at 500, and FBW 25 at 1,000 pixels per cell. At a threshold of 10 pixels per cell, FBN 64 was selected in 65% of bootstrap resamples and FBW 2 the remainder. At a threshold of 20 pixels per cell, FBN 32 was selected in all resamples.

FBW 4, FBW 8, and FBN 32 had median entropy values of 3.90, 2.91, and 4.16 bits, respectively, and approximate matrix densities of 56, 215, and 42 pixels per cell. Their median ICCs were within the range observed across all settings (Table 3). FBW 2 and FBW 4 retained at least 16 occupied gray levels in nearly all ROIs, whereas no observations had fewer than 16 occupied levels under FBN 16, FBN 32, or FBN 64.

5 Discussion

In this study of stable CN imaged with EDI-OCT, the choice of intensity discretization settings substantially affected texture feature values and the composition of the final feature sets. In contrast, observer reproducibility was similar across settings, while slab depth had a greater influence on reproducibility than discretization. These findings indicate that discretization is an integral component of the radiomic feature definition, consistent with Image Biomarker Standardization Initiative (IBSI) [27] recommendations, rather than a neutral processing step.

Of the 93 evaluated features, 16 first-order features were independent of discretization, whereas the remaining 77 features varied across settings. This is expected because texture features depend on the distribution and spatial relationship of discretized gray levels. Although neighboring settings generally preserved the relative ranking of eyes, agreement was lower between more divergent settings and between FBW and FBN. Changes in absolute feature values do not necessarily imply equivalent changes in the ability to distinguish between lesions, but the choice of discretization can affect the information available for subsequent modeling. Discretization-induced changes in feature values and patient rankings have been reported in PET [29], while MRI studies have demonstrated effects of discretization and preprocessing on feature reproducibility [36, 37].

The choice between FBW and FBN depends on how image intensity is meant to be represented. FBW applies fixed intensity intervals, thereby retaining part of the absolute intensity information, while resulting in a variable number of gray levels depending on each ROI's intensity range. Conversely, FBN divides each ROI's intensity range into a predefined number of levels, effectively normalizing local intensity and yielding relative rather than absolute intensity representations. Consistent with these definitions, FBW features were more strongly associated with ROI intensity range, whereas entropy decreased with depth under FBW but remained relatively stable under FBN 64. While FBW preserves a fixed intensity scale, FBN reduces the dependence of gray-level representations on the absolute intensity range of each ROI. The weaker associations with intensity range observed under FBN are consistent with this distinction. This distinction may be relevant in OCT, where intensities are not standardized across devices. However, our study did not assess inter-device generalizability or establish whether absolute intensity differences carry clinically relevant information. Ultimately, these differences reflect the distinct intensity representations produced by absolute and relative discretization [27, 29].

Discretization strategy should be selected considering both measurement reliability and the representation of image intensities. Observer ICCs assess agreement under segmentation variation, but do not establish whether a setting preserves clinically relevant texture. This distinction was evident in our study, in which high observer ICCs coexisted with substantial intensity compression under coarse discretization. High observer agreement therefore does not, by itself, establish that a setting preserves a sufficiently detailed graylevel representation. At FBW 25, the median number of occupied levels was five, and 98% of ROI-slab observations contained fewer than eight levels. These findings indicate substantial intensity compression and argue against adopting the default bin width without examining its effects on the images under study. Discretization recommendations derived from CT or MRI cannot be transferred directly to OCT without validation. In derma-tological OCT, Widaatalla et al. identified a BW 25 as a candidate setting using test-retest data [32]. Differences in tissue, acquisition, and image representation preclude directly transferring this setting choice to en face choroidal OCT.

Our results further demonstrate a trade-off between information retention, assessed using histogram entropy as a surrogate, and approximate GLCM sampling density. FBN 64 yielded slightly higher histogram entropy than FBW 2 but had a lower sampling-density proxy. This relationship is partly inherent in the definitions: finer discretization generally increases the number of occupied gray levels and histogram entropy, whereas the density proxy divides the ROI pixel count by the square of the occupied-level count. The entropy-density analysis therefore describes competing properties of the discretized representation rather than independently validating a particular setting. FBW 4 and FBN 32 occupied intermediate positions along this curve, whereas FBW 8 yielded a higher density proxy but lower histogram entropy and fewer occupied gray levels. FBW 4 and FBN 32 emerged as exploratory candidates for further study from this post hoc comparison, although no validated sampling-density threshold or evidence of clinical superiority is available to establish a universally optimal approach.

Median ICCs were high across all settings, and differences in reproducibility were small relative to variability between features. Although FBN yielded a higher proportion of features above the predefined reproducibility threshold, uncertainty in the paired comparisons did not establish superiority, and many features were close to the cutoff. Moreover, FBW 64 achieved high ICCs in deeper slabs despite typically yielding only two occupied gray levels, further illustrating that high observer agreement can coexist with a highly compressed intensity representation. Reproducibility alone is therefore insufficient to guide discretization choice. Depth had more pronounced effects: almost all features varied across slabs, and reproducibility was generally lower at 40 𝜇m than at 100-120 𝜇m. Depth should consequently be standardized and reported in retinal and choroidal studies. Technical reliability must be considered alongside the ability to characterize texture, as illustrated by phantom studies evaluating both reproducibility and texture discrimination [38].

Discretization also influenced feature redundancy and selection. The number of correlation groups decreased as the number of occupied gray levels increased, and feature sets retained after redundancy reduction overlapped less than reproducibility-eligible sets before pruning. Lower Jaccard overlap after pruning may partly reflect the selection of different representatives from similar correlation groups, rather than an equivalent change in the underlying feature information. Jaccard overlap should therefore be interpreted alongside the adjusted Rand index, which assesses agreement between the correlation-group partitions. Studies in other modalities have shown that texture indices can be redundant or strongly dependent on lesion volume, and that apparent radiomic performance can persist after spatial texture has been disrupted [39, 40].

However, these criteria do not establish a definitive optimal discretization setting. Histogram entropy does not distinguish biological signal from noise or directly quantify spatial texture, while the pixels-per-cell ratio does not measure actual GLCM occupancy. Moreover, no single setting simultaneously dominated both entropy and approximate density, leaving candidate selection dependent on exploratory density thresholds. Consequently, FBW 2 and FBN 64 remain viable options, though FBW 4 and FBN 32 offer reasonable starting points for future investigations. FBW 8 may be considered when a denser matrix is preferred, with the caveat that most ROIs contained fewer than 16 gray levels. These study-specific candidates require external validation across OCT devices, acquisition protocols, and lesion populations. Future studies should report the discretization method, its parameters, and the resulting number of gray levels.

Beyond its methodological contribution, this study provides a starting point for exploring choroidal lesions through a quantitative dimension of OCT image texture that remains largely unexplored. Characterizing radiomic patterns in stable choroidal nevi establishes a reference against which patterns associated with lesion growth or malignancy can subsequently be investigated. By showing how discretization and slab depth influence these measures, our findings support the development of standardized analytical approaches for distinguishing biological variation from variation introduced by image processing. This foundation may contribute to more reproducible and generalizable predictive models that complement conventional clinical and imaging assessment. Ultimately, the potential value of radiomic phenotyping must be evaluated against established clinical and imaging risk stratification frameworks, rather than inferred from technical stability alone [8, 22].

This study has several limitations. The cohort included only 64 eyes with CN acquired using a single OCT platform and a standardized imaging protocol; thus, findings may not generalize directly to other OCT devices, acquisition protocols, or broader populations and lesion types. The analysis was limited to two-dimensional en face EDI-OCT images and the specific depth slabs evaluated. Alternative approaches, such as threedimensional radiomic analysis, different slab thicknesses, or custom ROI definitions, may yield different results. Observer reproducibility was assessed through repeated segmentation of fixed images, rather than repeated image acquisition, and therefore does not quantify variability arising from reacquisition, repositioning, or changes in image quality. In addition, the primary ICC analysis used feature values averaged across six slabs.

Depth averaging may smooth observer differences; these ICCs therefore characterize the six-slab average and should not be assumed to represent the reproducibility of individual slabs or the TOTAL projection.

As a purely methodological investigation, this study did not evaluate clinical outcomes or diagnostic performance, such as the ability of radiomic features to discriminate CN from choroidal melanoma or predict malignant transformation. Consequently, while these results clarify feature stability, reproducibility, and redundancy across differing settings, they do not identify which discretization strategy yields the most clinically informative biomarkers. Finally, feature extraction was based on the standard PyRadiomics framework; alternative software implementations, preprocessing pipelines, or filter classes may produce different results.

6 Conclusions

In en face EDI-OCT radiomics of choroidal nevi, intensity discretization substantially alters feature values, relative patient rankings, feature redundancy, and downstream feature selection. Observer reproducibility and technical stability alone are insufficient to guide parameter selection. Discretization choices should also consider information retention, assessed through histogram entropy as a surrogate, gray-level representation, and approximate co-occurrence sampling, while accounting for slab depth. The choice between fixed bin width (FBW) and fixed bin number (FBN) should reflect whether fixed intensity intervals or ROI-relative discretization better suits the intended analysis. Within the evaluated acquisition and extraction pipeline, FBW 4 and FBN 32 emerged as exploratory candidate settings, with FBW 2 and FBN 64 warranting further comparison. These findings require external validation across devices, acquisition protocols, and lesion types, and the clinical utility of the extracted features remains to be established.
