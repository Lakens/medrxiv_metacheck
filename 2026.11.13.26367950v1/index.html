How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Heterogeneity in infectious disease spread reshapes the risk and severity of outbreaks. This heterogeneity can arise from intrinsic individuallevel differences in susceptibility, contact patterns, and infectious durations. Heterogeneity can also emerge from disease dynamics in a canonically homogeneous population, due to the fact that individuals randomly become infected at different times, recover after different intervals, and have access to different numbers of susceptible individuals. Here, we explore the mechanisms generating emergent heterogeneity by analyzing secondary case distributions within the SIR model. We show numerically and prove analytically that the variation in secondary cases, calculated over the course of the epidemic, does not change with R0. This is in contrast to the increasing variation in secondary cases in the linearized SIR model, often used to study heterogeneity at the beginning of an outbreak. When we consider "forward cohorts" of individuals infected at the same time, between-cohort variation increases as R0 increases, while within-cohort variation decreases. We show that these effects balance exactly, leading to the invariance of the variation in secondary cases with respect to R0. These results demonstrate the importance of considering emergent, as well as intrinsic, variation between individuals and of considering heterogeneity of transmission spread over the whole epidemic cycle, including substantive dynamical effects that appear even in the initial outbreak phase.

.

1 Introduction

Real outbreaks have significant heterogeneity in the number of secondary cases -including superspreading, marked by a small fraction of individuals causing a disproportionately large fraction of realized transmission [12, 19]. Superspreading can help explain why epidemics take off in some situations and not in others, despite apparently similar contexts [4, 11]. Leveraging knowledge of superspreading can make control efforts more effective [5, 14, 18], especially at the beginning of outbreaks [6]. Correspondingly, theoretical treatment of superspreading has emphasized individual-level transmission patterns while assuming a fully susceptible population [9, 12]. Models of superspreading often focus on linking observed secondary case counts to a continuous random variable, the individual reproductive number, via a Poisson sampling process. Canonically, the individual reproductive number is modeled as a gamma distribution, giving rise to negative binomial secondary case distributions [12]. In this way, variation arising from the stochastic transmission process is conceptually separated from intrinsic heterogeneity in expected infectiousness that may emerge from potential differences in biology or behaviors between individuals [1, 3, 7].

Conventional models accounting for heterogeneity typically assume a constant pool of susceptible individuals, implicitly or explicitly focusing on the very beginning of an outbreak. Over longer timescales, susceptible depletion and replenishment drive population-scale disease dynamics. Changes in the size of the susceptible population change the proportion of contacts that lead to successful disease transmission and drive differences in expected cases between different cohorts of infectors. Many deterministic epidemiological models also include explicit heterogeneity, for example by adding compartments for different demographic or geographic groups [17], which can have different average rates of mixing, transmission, or recovery. When implemented in terms of individual events, the inclusion of explicit heterogeneity reproduces superspreading in linear compartmental models [13]. Despite shared theory motivating models that highlight individual-level transmission, and those that emphasize populationlevel transmission dynamics, a gap persists in how each model framework treats individual-level transmission heterogeneity.

The canonical SIR (Susceptible-Infected-Removed) model uses ordinary differential equations (ODEs) to describe disease dynamics. The SIR model assumes constant, uniform infectivity and recovery times across all individuals during the infectious stage [8, 10]. Although it appears homogeneous, even the canonical SIR model has two sources of emergent heterogeneity. First, the changing effective reproductive number arising from susceptible dynamics drives variation between fractions of the population infected at different times. Second, the exponentially distributed recovery times assumed implicity by the ODE framework drive variation in the number of infections attributable to fractions of the population infected at a given time. In other words, the deterministic SIR model implies the presence of variation in the expected number of secondary cases.

Here we delineate sources of individual variation in transmission inscribed in simple dynamical models. We trace how these sources compose into observed or observable heterogeneity in different settings, for example contrasting how heterogeneity emerges in dynamical models versus linearized, or branching-process models. We begin by revisiting early findings that emergent heterogeneity can drive variation in the secondary case distribution, and discuss how individuallevel heterogeneity has a stronger effect when the basic reproductive number R 0 is smaller. We then show, first via simulations and then analytically, that emergent heterogeneity in secondary cases is not affected by R 0 across a full SIR outbreak. Throughout, we highlight the understudied, but critically important, role of disease dynamics per se in generating individual-level heterogeneity. We aim to spur the development of theory and methods that better integrate heterogeneity and dynamics, ultimately facilitating feedback between data on incidence and individual-level transmission chains, and mechanistic model specification, identification, validation, and intervention design.

2 Results

2.1 Heterogeneity in the secondary case distribution in the linearized SIR model

The early outbreak phase of an epidemic is modeled using a linearized SIR model, which assumes that the pool of susceptible individuals does not change appreciably. Early dynamics can thus be characterized by a transmission rate and an infectious period. Demographic stochasticity generates heterogeneity in this model despite the lack of explicit accounting of individual-level variation in transmission rates and infectious periods. Since recovery is Markovian, the model implicitly assumes an exponentially distributed infectious period, resulting in an exponentially distributed expected infectiousness (or individual reproductive number) with mean µ = R 0 and generalized dispersion κ = 1. The resulting distribution of secondary cases is geometric and has a mean equal to R 0 (see Box 1). Thus, the expected and realized secondary-case distributions are characterized by R 0 (Figure 1a).

Box 1. Linking variance across discrete and continuous time models

During the early stages of an outbreak, realized secondary-cases counts are typically modeled using a negative-binomial distribution, with mean µ and shape, or "dispersion," parameter k. This distribution corresponds mechanistically to a gamma-distributed "expected number of (secondary) cases", with the same mean and shape parameters. The "realized number of (secondary) cases" is then given by the convolution of this expected number with a Poisson distribution (reflecting necessary, individual-level stochasticity arising from a Poisson contact process) [12]. This is illustrated in Figure 1 for the case where differences in infectiousness arise from random recovery times under simple SIR assumptions, and is thus exponentially distributed. Expected infectiousness scales exactly with R 0 , so that the smooth curves in Figure 1b, are all the same, but the relative variance in realized cases increases when R 0 is small, because the proportional amount of randomness added by the Poisson step is larger.

We note a couple of points. First, the squared coefficient of variation (SCV) of the underlying Gamma distribution is κ = 1/k, so its variance is σ 2 act = κµ 2 . The resulting variance in secondary-case counts is σ2 = κµ 2 +µ, with the last term arising from the Poisson step of the negative-binomial construction. In the case of Figure 1, exponentially distributed infectiousness (corresponding to a geometric distribution of secondary cases), κ = k = 1. Second, the relationship between σ 2 act and σ 2 holds regardless of the form of the expected infectiousness distribution, due to the Law of Total Variation [16].

We therefore suggest κ as a generalized dispersion parameter. This has the advantages of (a) less dependence on a specific assumption about distribution (and parameterization), and (b), the intuitive idea that variation increases when "dispersion" increases.

The heterogeneity in the secondary case distribution means that a large fraction of the initial secondary cases are generated by only a few infectors, even when starting from the canonical homogeneous assumption. The proportion of expected cases caused by the individuals who remain infectious the longest is the same for any value of R 0 , but the proportion or realized cases caused by the most effective infectors is larger for smaller values of R 0 , since the relative importance of the Poisson infection process increases (Figure 1b). As R 0 becomes smaller, an increasing fraction of infected individuals generate no secondary infections, so the individuals who do transmit make up a smaller fraction of all infectors, such that realized heterogeneity is expected to increase as R 0 decreases.

2.2 Heterogeneity in secondary cases over an epidemic appears independent of R 0

Next we consider the impact of the depletion of the susceptible population during outbreaks on secondary case distributions. We simulated stochastic SIR epidemics with different R 0 values in a population of 10 4 individuals. For each infected individual, we recorded the number of secondary cases they generated during the outbreak. Figure 2 shows realized distributions of secondary cases caused by individual infectors over the entire outbreaks. Unlike in the linearized scenario shown in Figure 1, where the susceptible pool remains fully populated, the secondary case distributions, computed across the entire outbreak, appear indistinguishable across a broad range of the key parameter R 0 (compare panel (a) of Figure 1 with the entire set of panels in Figure 2).

Two key features of the secondary case distributions across an entire outbreak are salient. First, the mean of the secondary case distribution is approximately 1, as expected. Since we are considering a single outbreak seeded by a single initial case, the number of infectors must almost equal the number of infectees. Second, the variance of the realized secondary case distribution is approximately 2, corresponding to a variance of 1 in the expected case distribution, independent of R 0 . This result is unexpected. In the absence of susceptible depletion, heterogeneity in expected secondary cases is constant across values of R 0 (Figure 1b). During the full epidemic, we expect large-R 0 simulations to cause large dynamical changes, leading to larger variation in expected (and thus realized) secondary cases. At first glance, it is not clear what could balance the dynamical variation in susceptibles for different values of R 0 to leave the variance constant.

2.4 Offsetting contributions of within-and between-cohort variance drive R 0 -invariance of heterogeneity in secondary cases

To understand why the heterogeneity in secondary cases over the course of the epidemic is independent of R 0 , we divide the population into "forward cohorts" of individuals infected at the same time, and use variance partitioning to split the heterogeneity into two components: a within-cohort component and a between-cohort component. Individuals within the same forward cohort have access to the same susceptible pool initially but have differences in their secondary case counts as a result of their exponentially distributed infectious periods. Individuals in different forward cohorts also differ in the starting size of the susceptible pools that they have access to. It is important to note that both early and late cohorts have the same distributions of infectious periods and expected infectiousness but due to outbreak dynamics ((Figure 3a) that deplete the susceptible pool over time (Figure 3b), later cohorts, on average, have much lower secondary cases than earlier cohorts. Thus, the between-cohort variance can be attributed to susceptible depletion. Numerical calculation of the within-and between-cohort components of the variance in secondary cases using the ODE-based SIR model (see methods for details of the calculation) shows that as R 0 increases, the between-cohort component of the variance increases and the within-cohort component of the variances decreases (Figure 3c). However, the sum of the two components, remains constant across R 0 . As R 0 increases, the variance in the size of the forward cohorts (Figure 4a) and well as the speed and severity of susceptible depletion also increases (Figure 3b). As a result of the susceptible depletion, the mean number of secondary cases also decreases rapidly across forward cohorts (Figure 4b), with earlier forward cohorts having a much higher mean of secondary cases compared to later forward cohorts. Further, the decline in the mean number of secondary cases across forward cohorts becomes sharper as R 0 increases. As a result, the between-cohort variance in the number of secondary cases increases with R 0 . The within-cohort variance, on the other hand, decreases as R 0 increases (Figure 3c). Since the mean number of secondary cases varies widely between early and late cohorts, we look at the within-cohort dispersion in secondary cases to better understand how within-cohort heterogeneity changes between early vs late cohorts for an epidemic with a given R 0 . For a given R 0 , early cohorts have high dispersion in secondary cases, driven by access to a large susceptible pool (Figure 4c). As the epidemic progresses, the susceptible pool depletes (Figure 3b) and the within-cohort dispersion in secondary cases decreases (Figure 4c). The within-cohort dispersion increases for the later cohorts, driven by the shrinking within-cohort mean in the number of secondary cases (Figure 4c). However, the within-cohort contribution to the total variance depends not just on the within-cohort dispersion, but also on the cohort weight. Once the contributions of each forward cohort to the variance are appropriately weighted by the cohort mean and cohort size, we find that the weighted within-cohort dispersion is low for both early and late cohorts, and is high for cohorts that arise in the middle of the epidemic (Figure 4a) for a given R 0 . Indeed, across different values of R 0 , we find that the weighted within-cohort dispersion decreases as R 0 increases (Figure 3c). Together, the decrease in the within-cohort variances balances the increase in the between-cohort variance, leading to the total variances in secondary cases being independent of R 0.

3 Discussion

Quantifying heterogeneity in secondary-case distributions helps explain how epidemics unfold and how they can be contained. Although there have been efforts to quantify individual-level heterogeneity in contact rates [19] and to parameterize biologically realistic secondary case distributions, abstracted from the mechanism [12], these efforts have been largely separate from the construction and evaluation of the compartmental models that enable epidemic forecasting and intervention design (but see [13]). Here, we expand the bridge between individual-and population perspectives of heterogeneity by studying epidemiologically-relevant cohorts of infectors that become infectious at the same time.

We first showed how demographic stochasticity and a Poisson contact process result in heterogeneity in secondary cases, even in the linearized SIR model with its canonical assumption of homogeneity between individuals, and lack of susceptible depletion. Next, we showed how heterogeneity emerges in the SIR model of disease dynamics. Depletion of susceptibles over the course of an epidemic leads to differences in realized secondary cases between early and late infectors, despite sharing identical underlying infectiousness distributions. We then found, via simulations, and proved a surprising result -that the total variance in secondary cases over an epidemic is constant across all values of R 0 . This invariance is unexpected: in the linearized model, relative variance within cohorts is the same across values of R 0 , so we expected the larger betweencohort differences when R 0 is large to drive larger total variance in that case. Instead, we found the susceptible depletion also drives a corresponding decrease in within-cohort variance.

Our cohort-level analyses reveal the importance of susceptible depletion, even in early stages of the epidemic. We find that even very early cohorts of infectors have mean secondary case counts much below the theoretical R 0 (Figure 4b). This discrepancy becomes larger as R 0 increases. In practice, this means that mean secondary case counts systematically underestimate R 0 , especially when it is appreciably greater than 1 [15]. We also showed that the dispersion parameter κ can unify estimates of heterogeneity, without reliance on a particular distributional assumption, across individual-based and compartmental models, particularly beyond the initial outbreak phase, facilitating comparisons with data measured at the individual level.

The independence of the variance in secondary cases hinges on the SIR model structure. It requires that the infectious period be exponentially distributed. Further, it requires a well-mixed population. However, our results with the SIR model provide a baseline for comparing heterogeneity in secondary cases across different compartmental models. More broadly, by making the implicit explicit, the framework developed here provides a pathway to estimate and incorporate sources of emergent heterogeneity that arise from the dynamics of disease spread, and eventually to combine them with intrinsic individual-level heterogeneity.

4 Materials and Methods

4.1 Individual-based SIR model

We simulated an individual-based SIR model for a population of N individuals. Each individual was assigned a numerical index and an infectious duration, drawn from an exponential distribution with rate γ. Next, we built a matrix containing all possible pairs of individuals, and generated a pool of contact events by randomly sampling pairs from this matrix with replacement. The contact rate for each pair of individuals is β/(N -1), where β is the transmission rate. The total rate of contact events in the population was obtained by multiplying this rate by the total number of possible pairs, N (N -1)/2, giving βN/2. The time interval between successive contact events was then drawn from an exponential distribution with a rate βN/2.

Throughout the simulation, we tracked the infection status and number of infections caused by each individual. We initialized the simulation by assigning a single random individual to be infectious. We then ran the simulation forward in time, with successive time steps corresponding to successive contact events. At each time step, we checked the infection statuses of the individuals that came in contact based on when they became infectious and their assigned infectious periods. If both individuals were susceptible or infectious or recovered/removed, we moved to the next time step. If one of the individuals was recovered/removed, we moved to the next time step. However, if one of the individuals was infectious and the other was susceptible, we changed the infection status of the susceptible individual to infectious, noting the time when they became infectious. We also increased the tally of secondary cases caused by the infectious individual by 1. Then, we moved to the next time step. We stopped this process when either no infectious or no susceptible individuals remained.
