Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Introduction

Calcific aortic stenosis (AS) is one of the most frequent and lethal valvular heart diseases in contemporary practice, with a substantial global disease burden that is rising with ageing populations [1, 2]. Despite clear guideline frameworks for diagnosis and timing of intervention, outcomes for untreated AS remain poor, underscoring the need for earlier recognition and more precise risk stratification [3, 4]. The 2025 ESC/EACTS valvular heart disease guidelines emphasize echocardiography as the cornerstone for AS grading and recommend intervention primarily for severe AS, whereas patients with non-severe AS are generally managed with surveillance [4]. However, a major unresolved clinical challenge is that risk within non-severe AS is highly heterogeneous, and it remains unclear which patients are at increased risk of rapid progression, early myocardial damage, or adverse outcomes and may therefore benefit from closer monitoring or earlier treatment strategies.

A key unmet need is the management of moderate AS, which is increasingly recognized as a heterogeneous condition with clinically relevant risk, particularly when accompanied by LV dysfunction, heart failure (HF), or broader cardiac damage [5]. In a systematic review and metaanalysis, moderate AS was associated with higher mortality compared with no/mild AS, with risk amplification in subgroups such as symptomatic patients and those with impaired LVEF, supporting the concept that "moderate" haemodynamic grading does not equate to "moderate" biological risk [5]. Consistent with this, contemporary outcome data highlight a substantial mortality burden among patients with AS who do not undergo valve replacement, pointing to both clinical and health-system gaps in timely identification and treatment [3].

Progression of AS is highly variable across individuals, and conventional echo metrics (e.g., mean gradient, peak velocity, AVA) incompletely capture the underlying biology that drives valve calcification, myocardial remodeling, and clinical decompensation [4, 6]. Recent work has focused on estimating individual progression rates and identifying predictors of rapid progression, reinforcing the need for longitudinal cohorts with repeated standardized assessments to enable personalized risk prediction rather than population-average surveillance intervals [6, 7].

Computed tomography (CT) aortic valve calcium scoring (AVCS) has become a key adjunct when echocardiographic grading is discordant, particularly in low-flow or low-gradient states, and guideline-endorsed, sex-specific AVCS thresholds enable standardized, flow-independent adjudication of AS severity with established prognostic value [4, 8, 9]. Beyond valve anatomy, myocardial injury and fibrosis are increasingly recognized as relevant components of disease severity, and cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) provides complementary information on myocardial remodeling for improved phenotyping and risk stratification [10, 11].

Aortic stenosis is increasingly understood as an active fibro-calcific and inflammatory disease rather than a purely degenerative process, with inflammation and myocardial remodeling contributing importantly to disease initiation and progression [12]. This evolving concept supports the investigation of immunological parameters in humans as potential markers and mediators of disease activity. Among these, platelet-driven immune responses may be of particular relevance, as experimental data indicate that platelets can modulate monocyte activation and cytokine production through direct cellular interactions and soluble mediators [13]. In addition, genetic and epigenetic mechanisms may contribute to interindividual differences in inflammatory activity and disease trajectory. In this context, clonal haematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) has emerged as a clinically relevant modifier of cardiovascular inflammation and has also been associated with AS progression, supporting the integration of genetic and immunological profiling in the present study [4, 12].

Within this context, the MODAS study (Mechanisms Of Disease progression in Aortic Stenosis) project was established to investigate biomarkers and mechanisms of AS progression and outcome in a longitudinal human cohort, integrating morphological, functional, genetic, and immunological parameters and explicitly aiming to develop predictive models using advanced analytics including deep learning. MODAS constitutes the clinical cohort of subproject C06 of the Collaborative Research Center/Transregio TRR259 "Aortic Diseases" (Bonn-Cologne-Düsseldorf), funded by the German Research Foundation (DFG). The study is embedded within the GUARD (genes underlying aortic valve disease) network, a collaborative research platform of the participating centers that pools clinical, imaging, biobank, and genetic data from patients with bicuspid and tricuspid calcific AS to investigate the genetic and molecular determinants of disease; MODAS contributes its longitudinal moderate-AS cohort and biobank to this platform and uses its harmonized sequencing and genotyping pipelines [12, 14]. Here, we describe the rationale and design of this prospective longitudinal study (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT05851209); the detailed study procedures, phenotyping strategy, and endpoints are provided in the Materials

Cardiac CT is performed at baseline to enable detailed phenotyping of valve pathology (Figure 1) [8, 17]. The same acquisition protocol is used at all participating sites on ECG-gated dualsource or ≥64-slice multidetector systems (SOMATOM Definition Flash, Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany, at the coordinating center), following the acquisition standards of the Society of Cardiovascular Computed Tomography [18]. After a topogram, a non-contrast, prospectively ECG-triggered acquisition (120 kV, 3 mm slice thickness) is obtained for quantification of aortic valve and coronary calcification by the Agatston method; aortic valve calcium burden is graded against sex-specific thresholds [4, 15]. This is followed by a prospectively ECG-triggered, diastolic coronary CT angiography after intravenous injection of 100 mL of iodinated contrast (350 mg iodine/mL), covering the aortic valve, aortic root, and coronary arteries for anatomical characterization. Image quality is documented for every examination, and quantification is performed on dedicated post-processing software. Calcium quantification is performed with identical acquisition and reconstruction settings at all sites to ensure cross-site comparability.

The baseline CT constitutes the single study-related radiation exposure per participant approved by the German Federal Office for Radiation Protection (see Statement of Ethics). CT is therefore not repeated for study purposes. CT examinations performed during follow-up for clinical indications -in particular pre-procedural CT before TAVI or surgical AVR, which is standard of care and includes calcium quantification, and CT calcium scoring for discordant echocardiographic grading -are captured in the study database together with the documented indication, enabling paired analyses of calcification progression in the subset of participants with serial CT, which are prespecified as exploratory and hypothesis-generating analyses.

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging

CMR is performed at baseline for standardized assessment of ventricular volumes, mass, systolic function, aortic flow, and myocardial tissue characterization (Figures 1 and 2), following the standardized protocols of the Society for Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance [19]. The same dedicated study protocol is used at all participating sites; at the coordinating center, imaging is performed on a 1.5 T system (Achieva, Philips Healthcare, Best, The Netherlands). After survey and anatomical reference scans, ECG-triggered breath-hold cine steady-state free precession imaging is acquired in the two-, three-, and four-chamber views, the left ventricular outflow tract and aortic valve planes, and as a short-axis stack with full ventricular coverage from base to apex CHIP is assessed by targeted next-generation sequencing of DNA from peripheral blood using a panel of 48 recurrently mutated clonal haematopoiesis driver genes (Illumina NovaSeq 6000; mean sequencing depth approximately 20,000×), with CHIP defined as the presence of a driver mutation at a variant allele frequency ≥2% (≥4% for X-linked genes in men), using the pipeline established within the GUARD cohort [12]. In the analyses, CHIP is modelled primarily as a binary variable (presence of any driver mutation at VAF ≥2%); clone size (largest VAF, continuous and ≥10%) and gene-specific analyses (DNMT3A, TET2, other genes) are secondary and exploratory. Genome-wide genotyping is performed on germline DNA within the GUARD consortium using a genome-wide array with subsequent imputation. MODAS is not powered for stand-alone discovery GWAS; genetic data will be contributed to consortium-level metaanalyses and used for polygenic risk score analyses in the MODAS cohort. Multiple-testing correction is described in the statistical analysis section.

Study endpoints Primary endpoint

The primary endpoint is the time from enrolment to the first occurrence of either (a) progression from moderate to severe AS or (b) aortic valve replacement (AVR; TAVI or surgical AVR) performed for severe AS. Severe AS is defined according to the 2025 ESC/EACTS guidelines [4]: Vmax ≥4.0 m/s or mean gradient ≥40 mmHg; or AVA <1.0 cm² (indexed AVA <0.6 cm²/m²) in low-flow, low-gradient states when severity is confirmed by the guidelinerecommended integrated assessment, including sex-specific CT calcium score thresholds and, in patients with reduced LVEF, low-dose dobutamine stress echocardiography where applicable.

Endpoint adjudication follows a predefined process. Whenever progression to severe AS is suspected -at a scheduled study TTE or on clinical grounds between visits -the treating study investigator presents the patient with all available imaging findings to the local multidisciplinary Heart Team (cardiology, cardiac surgery, and anaesthesiology). The minimum dataset for Heart Team review comprises current symptom status and NYHA class, the 12-lead ECG, the index TTE with the complete standardized AS dataset (Vmax, mean gradient, AVA, stroke volume index, DVI, and LVEF), the baseline CT calcium score and any clinically indicated repeat CT, the natriuretic peptide level, and the comorbidity and frailty assessment. The Heart Team adjudicates on the basis of the clinical record and is therefore blinded by design to all research biomarker, immunological, and genetic data, which do not enter the clinical record and are not analysed before database lock; it is not blinded to the clinical baseline imaging, which is part of routine care and required for the severity assessment. After review of the findings and the clinical history, the Heart Team confirms or rejects the diagnosis of severe AS and issues a treatment recommendation according to the ESC/EACTS guidelines [4]. The Heart Team decision, the date of the index imaging study on which it is based, and -if applicable -the indication and date of AVR are documented in the study database. A confirmed diagnosis of severe AS counts as a primary endpoint event with the date of the index imaging study as event date; AVR counts as a primary endpoint event only if it is preceded by a Heart Team-confirmed diagnosis of severe AS. AVR performed without confirmed severe AS (for example, replacement of a still-moderate valve during coronary bypass or other cardiac surgery, or aortic valve surgery for infective endocarditis) is not counted as a primary endpoint event; such participants are censored at the date of surgery and analysed separately in sensitivity analyses. The two components of the composite endpoint are additionally analysed separately as secondary endpoints. Because the decision to intervene is not standardized by the protocol, the proportion of participants with confirmed severe AS who undergo AVR, and the time from confirmation to AVR, are monitored and reported annually by site as quality indicators; at the current data cutoff this proportion was 76% at the coordinating center and 62% at each of the two other sites. Center is a stratification factor in all time-to-event analyses, and the sensitivity analysis treating AVR as a censoring event is independent of site-level intervention thresholds.

Secondary endpoints

Secondary endpoints include major clinical outcomes such as all-cause mortality, and additional clinical events and trajectories (including symptom progression, heart-failure-related outcomes, and procedure-related pathways) as predefined in Table 3 and captured systematically at each follow-up visit through structured events ascertainment (hospitalisations, interventions, vital status), symptom and frailty assessment, the six-minute walk test, and the standardized echocardiography protocol. Vital status and date of death are verified through hospital records and, where necessary, contact with the treating physician or the population registry. Rapid haemodynamic progression is prespecified as an annual increase in Vmax of ≥0.3 m/s, the threshold derived from the natural-history study of Rosenhek et al. and adopted by the ESC/EACTS guidelines to define rapid progression [4, 20]. Sample size calculation

The planned sample size is 938 participants. This estimate was based on the expected precision of the incidence of progression to haemodynamically significant severe aortic stenosis according to current ESC guideline criteria over the 5-year follow-up period. In longitudinal cohorts of moderate AS with serial echocardiography, progression to severe AS has been reported in 39% of patients over a mean follow-up of 4.8 years [7] and in 47% of patients with mild or moderate AS, with 56% progressing within 5 years [21]; our own pilot data showed progression of calcific aortic valve disease in 37% of patients with aortic valve sclerosis within 4 years [22]. Because these rates were derived from populations that differ from the MODAS cohort in baseline severity, a conservative 5-year event rate of 40% was assumed. MODAS is a descriptive and model-development cohort without a comparator group; the sample size was therefore based on the precision of the primary estimate rather than on power for a hypothesis test. The required number of participants was derived from the precision of the Kaplan-Meier estimate of the 5-year cumulative incidence of the primary endpoint (Greenwood variance), allowing for 20% censoring before 5 years through death -approximately 18% at 5 years in comparable moderate-AS populations [7] -or loss to follow-up. With p = 0.40 and a two-sided α of 0.05, 750 participants with complete follow-up yield a half-width of the 95% confidence interval of 3.5 percentage points under the binomial approximation, n = z²•p•(1 -p)/d² (z = 1.96, d = 0.035, n ≈ 752), and 938 enrolled participants yield a half-width of approximately 3.3 percentage points for the Kaplan-Meier estimate under the censoring assumption; the planned enrolment is therefore 750/0.8 = 938 participants. In a sensitivity analysis across plausible 5-year event rates of 30%, 40%, and 50%, the expected half-width of the 95% confidence interval of the Kaplan-Meier estimate is 3.1, 3.3, and 3.4 percentage points, with approximately 255, 340, and 425 expected events, respectively, so that the planned sample size provides adequate precision over the entire plausible range. Based on the 750 completers, approximately 300 participants with progression to severe AS and 450 participants with stable moderate AS are expected to be available for analysis. For the analysis of predictors, approximately 300 events provide 80% power (two-sided α of 0.05) to detect a cause-specific hazard ratio of 1.38 for a binary predictor with 50% prevalence and of 1.45 for a predictor with 25% prevalence.

For the development of multivariable prediction models, the sample size is driven by the number of events relative to the number of candidate predictor parameters. With approximately 300 events, a conservative ratio of at least 10 events per candidate parameter supports stable estimation of models with up to about 30 candidate predictor parameters in the prespecified (Tier 1) clinical-imaging model, which is sufficient for a clinically oriented model integrating demographic, echocardiographic, CT, CMR, and selected biomarker variables. Formal samplesize criteria for prediction models (minimizing overfitting, achieving a small absolute difference between apparent and optimism-adjusted performance, and precise estimation of the overall risk) will be verified according to Riley et al. [23] before model development, and high-dimensional immunological and genetic data will be entered only through dimension-reducing or penalized approaches as described below.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables will be summarized using appropriate measures of central tendency and dispersion, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. The primary analysis population (full analysis set) comprises all enrolled participants who fulfil the eligibility criteria and have a baseline TTE; given the observational design, no intention-to-treat or per-protocol distinction applies.

The primary endpoint will be analysed as a time-to-event outcome. The primary estimand is the 5-year cumulative incidence of the primary endpoint in the presence of the competing risk of death, estimated with the Aalen-Johansen estimator; Kaplan-Meier estimates are reported for descriptive comparison only. For the association of baseline characteristics with progression, the primary (confirmatory) analysis uses cause-specific hazard ratios from Cox proportional-hazards models stratified by study center, because the aetiological question concerns the rate of progression among participants at risk. Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard models are supportive analyses and are used where the estimand is absolute risk, namely for the prediction models.

Prespecified sensitivity analyses comprise (i) progression to severe AS by imaging criteria only, with AVR treated as a censoring event; (ii) a composite including all AVR irrespective of indication; (iii) exclusion of participants with AVR for non-AS indications; and (iv) restriction to participants with concordant grading at baseline. To avoid immortal time bias, exposures that arise during follow-up will be modelled as time-dependent covariates or using landmark analyses. Longitudinal echocardiographic and biomarker trajectories will be analysed with linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts and slopes for participants and a random effect for center; center will otherwise be handled by stratification or as a random effect in all multivariable models. Missing data will be addressed according to a prespecified strategy. Missingness will be quantified and its pattern described for every variable. For baseline predictors, multiple imputation by chained equations (20 imputed datasets, including the outcome and auxiliary variables in the imputation model) will be used under the missing-at-random assumption, with complete-case analyses as sensitivity analyses. In the primary time-to-event analysis, outcome status and event dates are not imputed; for the prediction models the outcome is likewise taken as observed, and imputation applies to predictors only. Missing serial imaging data will not be imputed; analyses of imaging progression will be restricted to participants with paired examinations, with sensitivity analyses comparing baseline characteristics of participants with and without repeat imaging to assess selection. Differential follow-up intensity will be addressed by site-level monitoring of visit adherence and by sensitivity analyses restricted to participants with complete scheduled follow-up.

Prediction models for progression will be developed in a prespecified two-tier hierarchy. Tier 1 (prespecified, confirmatory) is a parsimonious clinical-imaging model with at most 30 candidate predictor parameters, prespecified on the basis of clinical knowledge and the literature (demographic, clinical, laboratory, echocardiographic, CT, and CMR variables, and ECGderived features including, where available, artificial-intelligence-based ECG markers of AS [24]); univariable screening will not be used for variable selection. The Tier 1 model will be fitted with penalized Cox regression (LASSO or elastic net) and validated internally by bootstrap optimism correction; its discrimination (C-index), calibration (calibration plots and slope), and clinical utility (decision-curve analysis) constitute the prespecified primary prediction result. Tier 2 (exploratory, hypothesis-generating) adds immunological, CHIP, and genetic variables and complementary machine-learning algorithms (e.g., random forests and gradient boosting) for multimodal risk prediction. Overfitting in Tier 2 is controlled by (i) dimension reduction or penalization of high-dimensional variable blocks before entry, (ii) nested cross-validation with center-stratified resampling, in which imputation, feature selection, and hyperparameter tuning are repeated within each outer fold, (iii) comparison of every Tier 2 model with the Tier 1 model on identical resamples, so that incremental value is tested against the clinical model, and (iv) explicit labelling as exploratory in all reports. Tier 2 models will not be presented as validated risk tools without external validation, which is planned in independent cohorts within the GUARD network. For exploratory analyses of immunological and CHIP variables, the false discovery rate will be controlled with the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure; for genome-wide analyses, the conventional genome-wide significance threshold (p < 5 × 10 ⁸) applies. Model ⁻ development and reporting will follow the TRIPOD+AI statement for all prediction models, whether based on regression or machine learning [25]. A detailed statistical analysis plan will be finalized before database lock and will be made publicly available via the trial registry (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05851209) before the primary analysis is performed. Analyses will be performed with R and Python.

Discussion

Moderate aortic stenosis (AS) is not a benign 'watch-and-wait' condition; multiple datasets show a meaningful burden of death and heart-failure events across the AS spectrum, and outcomes worsen as cardiac damage accrues [3] [4] [5]. Current guidelines still recommend intervention primarily once AS becomes severe or when other surgical indications coexist, leaving a large group of patients in whom risk is real but the timing of action remains uncertain [4]. This uncertainty is reinforced by the marked heterogeneity of disease progression and the limited ability of routine follow-up intervals to capture individual risk trajectories [6].

Against this background, the MODAS Study has been established as a large prospective multicenter cohort of patients with moderate AS, designed to provide deep longitudinal phenotyping through harmonized clinical assessment, multimodality imaging, immunological profiling, and genetic analyses. By integrating these complementary data domains, this study aims to identify mechanisms and predictors of rapid progression and adverse outcomes and to enable more individualized risk stratification in moderate AS [14].

The emphasis on standardized echocardiography plus advanced imaging is clinically motivated:

CT-derived aortic valve calcium metrics provide diagnostic and prognostic information and can refine disease staging when echocardiographic parameters are discordant or when remodeling appears disproportionate [4, 8]. Furthermore, myocardial substrate matters: in the EVOLVED trial, 224 patients with asymptomatic severe AS and CMR evidence of myocardial fibrosis (late gadolinium enhancement) were randomized to early AVR or guideline-directed conservative management; early intervention did not significantly reduce the primary endpoint of all-cause death or unplanned AS-related hospitalization, but it reduced symptom burden and unplanned hospitalizations, and the trial established CMR-based fibrosis phenotyping as a feasible tool to identify a biologically higher-risk stratum [11]. This supports the inclusion of CMR tissue characterization in MODAS as a mechanistically anchored marker of myocardial vulnerability. A key differentiator of MODAS is the explicit linkage of imaging phenotypes to systems biology, reflecting growing evidence that inflammation and immune dysregulation are not just epiphenomena in calcific AS.

In particular, CHIP has emerged as a plausible driver of inflammatory amplification and has been associated with AS progression and adverse outcomes in contemporary cohorts, including the GUARD cohort of the participating centers [12, 26, 27]; embedding CHIP and immune profiling into a prospective longitudinal design creates the opportunity to move from association toward testable mechanistic pathways and actionable risk stratification. improvement, but not superior for its hierarchical primary endpoint, with substantial crossover to TAVI because of AS progression [28]. The "earlier is better" hypothesis therefore depends on better staging, enrichment, and patient selection [29] -precisely the "who progresses fast?" question that prospective phenotyping cohorts such as MODAS are designed to answer.

Methodologically, recent work shows that AS progression can be predicted with meaningful accuracy from clinical and echocardiographic data, machine-learning models can reach high discrimination, and artificial-intelligence analysis of the 12-lead ECG can detect AS and track its trajectory years before intervention [24]; yet clinical translation hinges on transparent reporting and validation [6, 21, 25]. Prediction modeling in MODAS therefore follows a prespecified twotier plan that separates a confirmatory clinical-imaging model from exploratory multimodal and . machine-learning models, with penalized regression, internal validation, planned external validation, and reporting under TRIPOD+AI [25].

Several limitations are inherent to the present study design and must be acknowledged upfront.

Observational cohorts cannot establish causality, and residual confounding remains a structural concern even with careful adjustment, particularly when clinical decisions (e.g., timing of AVR/TAVI) influence endpoints [30, 31]. Although all sites are Heart Team-based tertiary centers applying the same ESC/EACTS guidelines, the decision to perform AVR is not standardized by the study protocol and may vary across sites and over time; the Heart Teambased adjudication and the prespecified sensitivity analyses treating AVR as a censoring event are intended to limit, but cannot eliminate, the resulting ascertainment bias. A related concern is immortal time bias, since participants must survive and remain under surveillance to undergo AVR; this is addressed by time-to-event analysis with time-dependent modelling of exposures arising during follow-up and by competing-risk analyses with death as a competing event.

In addition, multimodal, real-world longitudinal studies are exposed to missing data and differential follow-up intensity; recent methodological work highlights that handling missingness for prediction is non-trivial and must be prespecified to avoid biased performance estimates [32, 33]. The restriction of CT and CMR to baseline, which follows from the radiation protection approval and the resources available for an observational cohort of this size, limits the assessment of serial changes in valve calcification and myocardial fibrosis to the subset of participants with clinically indicated repeat imaging; because clinically indicated imaging is more likely in patients with rapid progression, serial imaging is not a study objective, and analyses of serial imaging are exposed to reverse causation and are prespecified as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, with the indication for each repeat examination documented and modelled. The prespecified missing-data strategy and the sensitivity analyses restricted to participants with complete scheduled follow-up address differential follow-up intensity at the analysis level.

Finally, recruitment at tertiary valve centers may enrich for comorbidity and referral bias, potentially limiting generalizability to lower-acuity community populations [30]. This spectrum bias will be assessed by comparing the baseline characteristics of the MODAS cohort with those of published population-based and registry cohorts of moderate AS, and the applicability of any prediction model to lower-risk populations will require external validation.

In summary, the MODAS Study (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT05851209) is positioned to deliver a clinically relevant framework for individualized risk stratification in moderate AS by integrating longitudinal imaging, biomarkers, and mechanistic immune/genetic profiling. If successful, its outputs should directly inform (i) follow-up interval personalization, (ii) trial enrichment strategies for early-intervention studies, and (iii) biological target discovery for diseasemodifying therapies in calcific AS [4, 25].

Eligible participants are adults (≥18 years) with moderate calcific AS of a tricuspid aortic valve confirmed by transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) who provide written informed consent. In accordance with the 2025 ESC/EACTS guidelines [4], moderate AS is defined by a peak aortic jet velocity (Vmax) of 3.0-4.0 m/s and/or a mean transvalvular gradient of 20-40 mmHg, with an aortic valve area (AVA) of 1.0-1.5 cm² by the continuity equation. A haemodynamic criterion (Vmax or mean gradient within the moderate range) is mandatory for inclusion; AVA serves as a confirmatory parameter. Participants who fulfil the haemodynamic criterion but have an AVA >1.5 cm² (e.g., because of large body size or high transvalvular flow) are enrolled, with AVA additionally assessed indexed to body surface area and in relation to flow, and are prospectively flagged in the database as "discordant AVA". Calcific AS is defined by echocardiographic evidence of leaflet calcification with restricted cusp motion; the calcification burden is quantified at baseline by CT calcium scoring. Patients with reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and patients with low-flow states (stroke volume index ≤35 mL/m²) are eligible, since these subgroups carry the highest risk and are of particular interest for progression research. Flow status, the dimensionless velocity index (DVI), and the CT aortic valve calcium score are recorded in all participants to characterize discordant flow-gradient patterns at baseline. Discordant grading is handled as follows: (i) patients with AVA <1.0 cm² and mean gradient <40 mmHg (possible low-flow, low-gradient severe AS) are not eligible, as they require the guideline-recommended diagnostic work-up for severe AS; (ii) patients with AVA 1.0-1.5 cm² but Vmax <3.0 m/s and mean gradient <20 mmHg in the presence of normal flow are classified as mild AS and are not eligible; (iii) in lowflow states with gradients at the lower moderate boundary, severity is classified using the integrated approach recommended by the guidelines, combining AVA, stroke volume index, DVI, and sex-specific CT calcium score thresholds [4, 8, 15]. Discordant categories -AVA >1.5 cm² with a moderate-range gradient, and low-flow states with gradients at the lower moderate boundary -are prespecified analysis strata, and the primary analysis is repeated in the concordant subgroup as a sensitivity analysis.

All sequences are part of the standard study protocol at all sites; examinations in which individual sequences could not be acquired (e.g., because of arrhythmia or limited breath-hold capacity) are documented, and the missing data are handled as described in the statistical analysis section. CMR is not repeated for study purposes; clinically indicated repeat CMR during follow-up is captured with its documented indication and analysed on an exploratory basis only.Biospecimen collection and biobankingPeripheral venous blood is collected at baseline and follow-up using predefined collection tubes and standardized processing workflows harmonized across sites. Biospecimens are used for routine laboratory and biomarker analyses, including haematologic, biochemical, coagulation, inflammatory, lipid, endocrine, immunologic, and urine-related phenotyping, as well as for dedicated molecular analyses such as genetic profiling, genome-wide association studies (GWAS), and clonal haematopoiesis (CHIP) assessment. In addition, selected samples are collected for downstream transcriptomic and proteomic analyses where applicable. Samples are processed, aliquoted, and stored under standardized biobank conditions for subsequent analyses. At baseline, blood is drawn into EDTA, citrate, and lithium-heparin tubes for plasma, into a PAXgene RNA tube for transcriptomic analyses, and into a cell preparation tube (CPT) for mononuclear cell isolation. Plasma is separated by centrifugation (EDTA: 3000 g for 10 min; citrate and heparin: 500 g for 10 min) and stored in 500 µL aliquots; additional EDTA wholeblood aliquots are frozen unprocessed as back-up and as a source of germline DNA. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) are isolated from pooled EDTA blood by Ficoll densitygradient centrifugation (700 g for 20 min without brake), washed twice, and cryopreserved in 90% fetal calf serum/10% DMSO; mononuclear cells from the CPT tube are pelleted after centrifugation (1700 g for 20 min) and cryopreserved in the same medium. PAXgene tubes are frozen unprocessed. All aliquots are stored at -80 °C in the local biobank; cryopreserved cells are transferred to -150 °C for long-term storage. At follow-up visits, plasma and PBMC samples are collected; genetic sampling is not repeated. Immunological profiling is performed centrally at the Institute of Innate Immunity, University of Bonn, on cryopreserved samples processed with the harmonised biobanking standard operating procedure at all sites, so that no cross-site assay variability arises. Following the methods established by the group [13], it comprises multiparameter flow-cytometric immunophenotyping of PBMCs with a focus on monocyte subsets and platelet-monocyte aggregates, ex vivo assessment of monocyte cytokine responses with and without platelets, and plasma cytokine profiling. Standardised gating strategies and inter-run reference controls are applied, and the analytical batch is recorded and included as a covariate in all analyses. The primary immunological readouts for the MODAS analyses will be prespecified in the statistical analysis plan before database lock, and immunological variables enter only the exploratory tier of the prediction models (see Statistical analysis).
