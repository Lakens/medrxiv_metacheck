About the personal-information flags. These are pattern matches, not confirmed disclosures, and they are tuned to over-report rather than miss something. Expect false positives:

  • Column names are matched against identifying words in English and other European languages (email, telefoonnummer, geboortedatum, …). A column called phone_number flags whether or not it holds one, and an empty or already-anonymised column flags on its name alone.
  • Values are matched against the shape of an identifier. An IP address pattern also matches a version string or a dotted numeric code; a card-number pattern requires both a real issuer prefix and a checksum, but a long numeric ID can still coincide.
  • Free text is flagged when a column holds long, mostly-distinct prose — the shape open-ended answers take. Most such columns contain no names at all.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Introduction

Marburg virus disease is a severe filovirus infection with no licensed vaccine or treatment. Its natural reservoir is the Egyptian fruit bat, and human outbreaks have been rare. They are now becoming more frequent and are reaching new places. Equatorial Guinea and Tanzania reported their first outbreaks in 2023, Rwanda in 2024 and Ethiopia in 2025, and Tanzania had a second outbreak in 2025 [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]. Each of these countries had produced little Marburg research before.

The response to these outbreaks also differed from the past. In Rwanda, the national health authorities started a phase 2 vaccine trial ten days after the outbreak was declared and published the main clinical report of the outbreak [3, 6]. In Ethiopia, a national research institute provided scientific leadership for the vaccine trial [7]. Filoviruses remain on the WHO list of priority pathogens [8], and in January 2025 the WHO-coordinated Filovirus Collaborative Open Research Consortium published ten Marburg research priorities. These priorities call for equitable participation of researchers from places where the virus circulates and state that leadership of studies by local scientists should be a continuous goal [9].

Several bibliometric studies of Marburg research appeared between 2023 and 2026 [10] [11] [12] [13] [14]. They map publication output, journals, countries, keywords and collaboration networks, and they agree that the United States dominates the field. The most recent notes that endemic African countries are underrepresented in collaboration networks [14]. None of them asks who leads the work: whether women hold senior author positions, whether researchers from affected countries lead studies about their own countries, or who pays for the research. These questions have been studied in other fields. Women are under-represented as last authors in biomedical and COVID-19 research [15] [16] [17], and the gap is wide in health research from sub-Saharan Africa [18]. In infectious-disease research done in Africa, about half of first authors and four in ten last authors are African [19], and local researchers are often placed in middle author positions or left out altogether [20, 21]. During the 2013-2016 Ebola epidemic, only 13% of authors writing about the epidemic were based in Africa [22]. No comparable analysis exists for Marburg virus.

Methods

Study design and data source

This is a cross-sectional bibliometric study. I searched the Web of Science Core Collection (WoS) on 23

February 2023 with the query Marburg* AND (virus* OR disease* OR hemorrhagic OR haemorrhagic OR viral* OR MARV OR fever) and kept English-language articles, reviews and letters. The search returned 932 records published between 1968 and 2023 (Supplementary Figure S1).

The analysis was restricted to the 684 papers published from 2008 onward, for two reasons. First, WoS has indexed funding acknowledgements systematically only since 2008 [23, 24]: in these data, 1.7% of papers published before 2005 had a funding record, compared with 80-86% from 2011 (Supplementary Figure S2). Second, author first names, which gender inference requires, are rarely available in WoS before 2007. Five early-access records without a publication year were assigned their early-access year (2022). The year 2023 is partial and contains 3 papers; it is grouped with the period 2018-2023.

Authors, gender and author position

Each author was linked to their affiliations through the bracketed author lists in the WoS address field.

Gender was inferred from first names with Gender-API, which had the lowest error rate in an independent benchmark of name-to-gender services [25]. Of 5,700 authorships, 96.4% were classified as woman or man; the remainder were left unclassified and excluded from gender denominators. The first author was the first listed author. The last author was the final author on papers with two or more authors and was taken as a proxy for the senior or supervising author. Shares are reported per authorship and by position.

Geography and local leadership

Each author's country was taken from the last segment of their address and grouped by World Bank region and FY2023 income group [26]. A paper was classed as being about an African country when that country was named in its title, abstract or author keywords. Virus and strain names that contain place names (for example Sudan virus, Zaire ebolavirus or MARV-Angola) were removed before matching, and ambiguous names required a place context (for example "in Angola" or the province name "Uíge").

A first or last author was counted as local when affiliated in a country the paper was about, and as African when affiliated in any African country. Results were compared with a systematic review of 1,182 papers on infectious-disease research done in Africa [19].

Funders

Funder names from the WoS "Funding Name Preferred" field were grouped with 54 ordered pattern rules into funder groups, such as the US National Institutes of Health (NIH), the US Department of Defense or the German Research Foundation. Each group was assigned a country and a type: government, government (defence), philanthropy, multilateral, industry or academic. The rules classified 86% of paper-funder links; the remaining 14% were mostly one-off names and were kept as unclassified. The rules are published with the code.

Research themes and WHO priorities

Research topics were identified with latent Dirichlet allocation fitted to titles, abstracts and author keywords. Missing abstracts were left empty rather than filled with placeholder text. The number of topics (k = 10) was chosen as the value with the highest c_v coherence between 6 and 14 [27], and topics were labelled from their top words. Separately, each paper was tagged against the ten WHO 2025 Marburg research priorities [9] and two themes that are not WHO priorities (basic molecular virology, and biosecurity and biodefence), using a published keyword codebook. A paper could match several themes.

Citations and open access

Citation counts are WoS Core Collection counts at the date of export. To compare papers of different ages, year-normalised citations were calculated as each paper's citations divided by the mean citations of papers published in the same year [28]. Open-access status was taken from WoS.

Statistical analysis

Proportions are reported with Wilson 95% CIs and compared with chi-square tests, or Fisher's exact test when expected cell counts were small. Logistic regression modelled the odds that the first or the last author was a woman, adjusted for publication year, the region of that author, team size (log number of authors), document type and topic. Negative binomial regression modelled citation counts, with publication-year fixed effects and adjustment for funding, open access, team size, international collaboration, document type and topic. Logistic regression modelled open access by funding status with the same covariates. Subgroup comparisons by topic, region and theme are descriptive; they were not corrected for multiple testing and should be read as exploratory. Analyses used Python (pandas, statsmodels, scikit-learn and gensim). All code, reference tables and a de-identified paper-level dataset are public (see Data and code availability).

Sensitivity analyses

The gender analysis was repeated in four ways: keeping only Gender-API assignments with an accuracy of at least 80% (89.8% of classified authorships); excluding authors based in East Asia and the Pacific, where name-based inference is least accurate [25]; and counting all unclassified authors first as women and then as men. The local-leadership analysis was repeated for the stricter subset of papers that name the study country in the title.

Results

Characteristics of the corpus

The 684 papers had 5,700 authorships from 81 countries. Papers had a mean of 8.3 authors, 1.7 countries and 3.4 unique organisations, and 36.3% involved authors from more than one country. A US-based author appeared on 70.5% of papers. Reviews made up 18.0% of papers. Overall, 81.1% of papers had a funding acknowledgement and 81.3% were open access.

Gender

Women held a third of all authorships but a fifth of last-author positions (Figure 1; Supplementary Table S1). They held 35.1% (95% CI 33.9-36.4) of the 5,497 classified authorships, 41.6% (37.9-45.4) of 658 first-author positions and 19.6% (16.7-22.9) of 642 last-author positions.

The share of women last authors rose from 15.1% in 2008-2012 to 18.4% in 2013-2017 and 23.2% in 2018-2023. After adjustment, the odds that the last author was a woman rose by about 6% per year (odds ratio [OR] 1.06, 95% CI 1.01-1.12). The share of women first authors rose from 36.8% to 44.5% over the same periods, but the adjusted trend was not statistically significant (OR 1.04, 1.00-1.09).

Of 628 multi-author papers with both positions classified, 48.1% had a man as first and last author, 32.2% a woman first and a man last, 10.2% a woman in both positions and 9.6% a man first and a woman last.

When the last author was a woman, the first author was also a woman in 51.6% of papers, compared with 40.1% when the last author was a man (chi-square p = 0.026).

The gender gap differed by region. Women were 36.9% of authors based in North America and 40.5% in Europe and Central Asia, but 28.0% in East Asia and the Pacific and 20.1% in sub-Saharan Africa. Only 2 of 25 last authors based in sub-Saharan Africa were women (8.0%). Women were 12.1% of authors based in lower-middle-income countries and 17.1% in low-income countries. In adjusted models, first authors based in East Asia and the Pacific were less likely to be women than those in North America (OR 0.40, 0.21-0.79), and reviews were more likely than other papers to have a woman as last author (OR 3.24, 1.78-5.90).

The results were robust to the four sensitivity analyses of gender inference (Supplementary Table S5).

The share of women last authors ranged from 17.7% when only high-accuracy assignments were kept to 21.8% when all unclassified authors were counted as women.

Local and African leadership

Researchers based in Africa were rare across the corpus. Only 14.6% of papers had any author based in Africa, 6.4% an African first author and 6.6% an African last author.

Of the 102 papers about an African country, about one in four was led from the country studied (Table 1;

Figure 2). Half had at least one author from the country studied, but only 25.5% had a first author and 23.5% a last author from that country. African authorship of these papers was well below the benchmarks for infectious-disease research done in Africa: 29.4% had an African first author (benchmark 49.8%) and 28.4% an African last author (benchmark 41.3%).

Funders

Of the 555 funded papers, 69.0% acknowledged a US funder. The US NIH appeared on 47.2% of funded papers and the US Department of Defense on 23.1% (24.0% with other US defence agencies). Funders from Germany (7.7%), Japan (6.5%), Canada (5.6%), China (5.6%), the United Kingdom (5.0%) and the European Union (4.1%) followed. African funders appeared on 3.2% of funded papers (18 papers), and industry on 5.6%.

Funders mostly supported last authors in their own region (Figure 3). Of the 383 papers with a US funder, 84% had a last author based in North America and 3% a last author in sub-Saharan Africa. Of the 18 papers with an African funder, half had a last author based in sub-Saharan Africa. Of the 57 papers with an African first or last author, 77.2% were funded: 26.3% by an African funder and 36.8% by a US funder.

Research themes and WHO priorities

The ten topics ranged in size from vaccines and animal challenge models (123 papers) to innate immunity and interferon antagonism (23 papers). Authors based in Africa appeared on 32.9% of papers on outbreaks and public health, 31.2% of diagnostics papers and 27.6% of papers on bat reservoirs, but on 1.4% of papers on viral assembly and on none of the papers on innate immunity. Women were first authors on 53.4% of viral assembly papers but on 21.0% of overview papers.

Research concentrated on medical countermeasures (60.1% of papers) and basic molecular virology (59.6%) (Figure 4). Four WHO priorities were each covered by fewer than 8% of papers: supportive clinical care and infection prevention (5.3%), research during outbreaks, including trials (3.9%), social sciences and community engagement (7.2%), and collaboration and capacity strengthening (6.0%).

The thematic profile differed by funder and by leadership. Papers funded by US defence agencies were mostly about medical countermeasures (76.7%) and animal models (48.9%), and rarely about social science (3.0%) or capacity strengthening (0.8%). Papers with an African first or last author more often covered epidemiology and surveillance (70.2%), reservoirs (56.1%), social science (21.1%), capacity strengthening (19.3%) and clinical care (10.5%).

Funding, citations and open access

Across all 932 records from 1968 onward, papers with a funding acknowledgement had fewer mean citations than papers without one (38.7 vs 55.8; Mann-Whitney p < 0.001), a comparison often reported in studies of research funding. This comparison is confounded by publication age: most "unfunded" papers were published before WoS indexed funding data, so they had more time to collect citations (Supplementary Figure S2). Within 2008-2023, funded papers had higher year-normalised citations than unfunded papers (1.03 vs 0.85).

Regression models within 2008-2023 showed how the apparent effect of funding depends on adjustment (Supplementary Figure S3). Without adjustment, funding was not associated with citations (rate ratio 1.16, 95% CI 0.90-1.48). After adjustment for publication year, funded papers were cited more (1.28, 1.04-1.57). After full adjustment, the association disappeared (1.07, 0.87-1.33). The year-adjusted difference was therefore explained by other characteristics of funded papers, mainly open access: openaccess papers were cited more (adjusted rate ratio 1.87), and funded papers were more often open access (adjusted OR 2.34, 1.39-3.96).

Citations also differed by the gender of the first and last authors. Compared with papers with a man in both positions, papers with a woman in both positions received fewer citations (adjusted rate ratio 0.76, 0.59-0.99), and papers with a man first and a woman last received more (1.31, 1.01-1.71). Papers with a woman first and a man last did not differ (0.90, 0.76-1.06).

Discussion

Principal findings

In the 15 years of Marburg virus research before the 2023-2025 outbreaks, women held one in five senior author positions, and researchers from the countries studied led about one in four papers about those countries. US government agencies, including defence agencies, funded most of the work. The research centred on countermeasures and basic virology, while the WHO priorities on clinical care, outbreak research, social science and capacity were rarely addressed. Together, these findings describe a field that was largely led from outside the places where the virus circulates, at the moment those places began to face it.

Gender

The 19.6% share of women last authors is low compared with other fields. It is below the 35% reported for health research from sub-Saharan Africa [18], and it is consistent with the under-representation of women among authors of COVID-19 research [16, 17]. The gap between first authorship (42%) and last authorship (20%) follows the pattern seen across science, in which women's share falls with seniority [15]. The rise in women last authors to 23% in 2018-2023 is encouraging, but it is slow.

Women first authors were more common when the last author was a woman. This is consistent with evidence that women faculty in the life sciences train more women than men faculty do [29]. If this holds in Marburg research, the shortage of senior women may also limit the next generation of women researchers. The citation differences by gender pairing point in different directions and come from small groups, so they should be interpreted with caution. The near-absence of African women in senior positions (2 last-author papers in 15 years) is where the gender gap and the geographic gap meet most clearly.

Local leadership

African first and last authorship of papers about African countries (29% and 28%) was well below the 50% and 41% found for infectious-disease research done in Africa [19]. By this measure, Marburg virus research was less locally led than infectious-disease research in Africa as a whole. Part of this gap reflects the nature of the field. Laboratory work with live Marburg virus requires biosafety level 4 containment, and of 59 such laboratories worldwide, only 3 are in Africa [30]. Papers based on samples or viral sequences from Africa may therefore be analysed and led elsewhere.

The stricter title-based analysis found higher, but still minority, local leadership, so the main result is not an artefact of loose country tagging. The rise in African first authorship over time, and the locally led responses in Rwanda and Ethiopia [3, 6, 7], suggest that the field may be changing. This baseline makes it possible to measure that change. Journals and funders can support it: PLOS Medicine, for example, now expects local researchers to be included as authors of research done in their countries [31].

Funding and the research agenda

The dominance of US government funding agrees with funding-flow data. The G-FINDER survey found that Marburg received about 1.3% of tracked emerging-infectious-disease R&D funding between 2015 and 2022, that the NIH was its largest funder, and that industry provided no funding at all [32]. This study adds two findings. First, a large share of funded papers acknowledged defence agencies, and these papers focused on countermeasures and animal models rather than clinical care, social science or capacity. This pattern fits a biodefence rationale for Marburg research, although the data cannot show whether the funder's priorities shaped the topics or whether defence agencies funded groups already working on these topics. Second, funders mainly supported last authors in their own region, and only a quarter of Africanled papers acknowledged an African funder. Building local leadership will therefore probably need funding that flows directly to institutions in affected countries, and not only to collaborations led from abroad.

A methodological note on funding acknowledgements

A naive comparison suggests that funded papers are cited less. This result arises from comparing papers across years in which WoS did not yet index funding, so that "unfunded" largely meant "old". When the analysis was limited to the indexed period and adjusted for publication year and paper characteristics, the effect disappeared. Studies that use WoS funding acknowledgements should restrict their analyses to 2008 onward and normalise citations by year [23, 24, 28].

Strengths and limitations

To my knowledge, this is the first study of gender, local leadership and funding in Marburg virus research, and it compares the research directly with the WHO 2025 priorities. The pipeline is reproducible, and the code and de-identified data are public, together with an interactive explorer of the results.

The study has several limitations. WoS misses some regional journals and non-English papers, which may under-count African authors. Gender inference from names is binary, less accurate for some name origins [25] and silent on how people identify; 3.6% of authorships could not be classified, although the sensitivity analyses suggest that this does not change the conclusions. Last authorship is an imperfect proxy for seniority, and shared or alphabetical authorship was not detected. Affiliation is not nationality, so researchers from affected countries who work abroad were counted as non-local. Tagging the study country from text is approximate, even with strain names removed and the stricter title-based check.

WHO priority tags are keyword-based and show broad emphasis rather than precise counts. Funding acknowledgements record what authors declare, not amounts. Finally, the data end in February 2023, before the recent outbreaks, so the results describe a baseline.

Implications and future research

Three actions follow from these findings. Funders can route support to institutions in affected countries and to the neglected WHO priorities on clinical care, outbreak research, social science and capacity. Journals can expect local first or last authorship for research done in affected countries. Researchers can repeat this analysis with 2023-2026 data to measure whether the locally led responses in Rwanda and Ethiopia changed who leads the field.
