How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

High dietary fructose consumption is a recognized risk factor for metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), but the clinical relevance of fasting fructose levels remains unexplored. This study investigated gut microbiota and hepatic transcriptional alterations associated with fasting fructose levels and their potential link to MASLD. We analyzed data from a subset of 49 morbidly obese individuals enrolled in the prospective BARIA cohort. Fasting plasma fructose levels were significantly higher in individuals with MASLD and were associated with polyol pathway downregulation and lipogenic gene upregulation. Fasting fructose also positively correlated with postprandial plasma ethanol increase, suggesting enhanced microbial endogenous production and/or altered ethanol catabolism, the latter supported by suppressed hepatic alcohol dehydrogenase gene expression. In addition, fasting fructose was associated with fecal gut microbiota alterations, including significant shifts in beta diversity and correlations with specific bacterial species. At the transcriptomic level, elevated fasting fructose was linked to impaired mitochondrial energy metabolism and antioxidant defense, signatures associated with MASLDrelated metabolic disruption. In conclusion, our data indicate that fasting plasma fructose reflects dietary intake rather than endogenous production and increased levels are associated with metabolic and microbiota changes linked to MASLD. Although not intended to replace established glycemic markers, our findings suggest that fasting fructose may provide complementary insights into the metabolic state of the liver and MASLD-related dysregulation, supporting its potential as a MASLD risk biomarker.

Introduction

Fructose consumption has increased in recent decades, driven by the growing intake of processed foods and beverages containing high amounts of added sugars such as high fructose corn syrup. 1, 2 This increased fructose consumption has been identified as an important risk factor for the development of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), [3] [4] [5] [6] the most prevalent chronic liver disease worldwide. 7 MASLD not only increases liver-related complications, but also the risk of cardiovascular-and chronic kidney diseases. 8 An estimated rise of the MASLD prevalence to more than 55% by 2040 9 highlights the urgent need for novel preventative and therapeutic strategies.

Excessive dietary fructose intake is recognized as an important driver of MASLD due to its unique metabolic effects. In contrast to glycolysis, hepatic fructose catabolism is an unregulated process as it bypasses the rate-limiting steps of glycolysis. The unregulated catabolism of fructose promotes de novo lipogenesis (DNL), resulting in excessive production and accumulation of fatty acids (FA) in the liver. 3, 10, 11 Moreover, the rapid phosphorylation of fructose in the liver can cause ATP depletion and subsequent uric acid production, [12] [13] [14] which induces oxidative stress and further promotes DNL and insulin resistance. [15] [16] [17] [18] [19] In addition to these direct hepatic metabolic effects, the small intestine has also been suggested to play a crucial direct role (next to dietary uptake) in fructose catabolism. 20 In murine models, it has been demonstrated that the small intestine can convert physiological doses of dietary fructose into glucose and organic acids. 20 However, this small intestinal gluconeogenesis was saturated at high fructose doses, leading to fructose overflow to both the liver and the distal parts of the intestine, where it becomes available for gut microbial fermentation. In the colon for example, gut microbiota-derived acetate can be produced from dietary fructose, which can stimulate DNL. 21 Furthermore, various intestinal microbiota species have been shown to produce ethanol, another driver of DNL, from the fermentation of sugars, including fructose. 22, 23 This gut microbiota derived endogenous ethanol production has been associated with the development of MASLD, 24 highlighting the potential of the gut-liver axis to contribute to fructoseinduced liver disease.

While associations between dietary fructose intake and MASLD pathogenesis are well-established, the significance of fasting plasma fructose concentrations remains underexplored. We used plasma metabolites, liver histology, fecal shotgun metagenomics and liver RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) data to investigate the associations between fasting plasma fructose levels, the gut microbiota and hepatic gene expression, as well as their potential association with MASLD histology in a morbidly obese population. Using this integrative analysis, we aimed to provide novel insights into the metabolic and microbial signatures associated with fasting plasma fructose levels and their potential role in MASLD pathogenesis. By this means, we investigate whether fasting plasma fructose concentrations may serve as a potential metabolic marker in the context of MASLD.

Materials and methods

Study design

For this study a subset of 49 individuals was included from the ongoing BARIA study, a longitudinal cohort study of morbidly obese subjects scheduled for bariatric surgery. 25 The inclusion criteria for this study were adults, aged <65 years, scheduled for bariatric surgery, with a BMI ≥40 kg/m 2 or a BMI ≥35 kg/m 2 with obesity-related comorbidity. Baseline measurements, collected within 3 months before surgery, included demographics, biometrics, fasting blood samples, urine and faecal samples and a two-hour oral mixed meal test (MMT). On the day of the bariatric surgery, preoperative fecal samples and fasting blood samples were collected. Furthermore, tissue biopsies from liver, jejunum and adipose tissues were obtained during surgery. All samples were stored at -80°C until further use. All subjects provided written informed consent and the study is executed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was registered at the Dutch trial registry at 29-03-2016, registration number: NL-OMON50702, and approved by the Academic Medical Center Ethics Committee of the Amsterdam UMC (approval code: NL55755.018.15).

Mixed meal test

During the preoperative MMT, all participants orally ingested a standardized mixed meal (Nutridrink) after a fasting period of at least 9 hours. Blood sample collection, performed as described in the published study design, 25 comprised seven time points.

Fructose plasma measurements

On the day of the bariatric surgery, fasting peripheral blood samples were obtained prior to the procedure. Fasting plasma fructose concentrations were measured by Gas Chromatography -Triple Quadrupole Mass Spectrometry. Fructose was analyzed as the per-acetate derivative, prepared as previously described for glucose. 26 D2-fructose (Campro, the Netherlands) was added as internal standard and both the M (Fructose) and the M +2 (D2-Frcutose) signal were monitored for accurate quantification by a 7890A GC, equipped with a 7693 auto sampler coupled to a 7000 Triple Quadrupole Mass Spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, U.S.A.).

Ethanol plasma measurements

Endogenous ethanol concentrations were measured in plasma samples obtained during the MMT in the fasting state (t=0 min) and 120 minutes postprandially, as previously described. 24 Ethanol measurements were performed in a subgroup of 34 individuals. Briefly, plasma samples were deproteinized and subsequently neutralized following centrifugation. Ethanol was initially assayed using an enzyme-based kit (DiaSys, Germany) and validated using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) 4000 series (Jasco, Benelux). Ethanol was separated and next detected via refractive index detection. ChromNav chromatography software version 2.0 (Jasco, Benelux) was used for calculation of absolute ethanol concentrations. The difference between the fasting and postprandial ethanol concentration was used as a measure for postprandial ethanol increase.

Tissue biopsies and histology

Intraoperative tissue biopsies were obtained from the liver and jejunum, as previously outlined. 25 All tissue biopsies were either snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80°C until further processing, or formalinfixed, paraffin-embedded for further histological analysis. Hematoxylin-eosin and Sirius red staining was performed on the paraffin-embedded biopsy sections. These histological sections were scored independently by two pathologists in accordance with the Steatosis, Activity, and Fibrosis score. 27 Based on this scoring system, MASLD was characterized by isolated steatosis in more than 5% of hepatocytes or steatosis with mild inflammation, but without ballooning. MASH was characterized by steatosis in more than 5% of hepatocytes accompanied by both lobular inflammation and ballooning.

RNA isolation and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from the intraoperatively obtained liver and jejunum biopsies, homogenized, and purified. RNA quality was assessed on a BioAnalyzer instrument (Agilent Technologies, U.S.A.), and quantification was performed on Nanodrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific, U.S.A.). Due to RNA degradation, rRNA depletion was used for RNA-seq library preparation. Sequencing was performed at Novogene with 150-bp paired-end reads per sample. The average number of reads per sample was 42 ± 15 million. Obtained reads were quality-trimmed and pseudo-aligned to the human transcriptome. Full details are provided in a formerly published protocol. 24

Bulk RNA-seq analysis

Raw transcriptomic data from liver tissue was aggregated to gene-level counts using appropriate gene annotations. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 package (v 1.50.2) in R, with fasting plasma fructose concentration included as a Z-score normalized continuous predictor. Batch effects were accounted for by including the batch variable as a covariate in the model design. No additional biological variables were included as covariates, to avoid obscuring associations with the primary predictor. Genes with fewer than 10 counts in at least two samples were excluded to improve data quality. Multiple testing correction was applied using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. Genes with FDR adjusted p-values <0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Functional enrichment of these genes was assessed using Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis via the enrichGO() function and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis via gseKEGG(), both conducted using the clusterProfiler package in R.

Following differential gene expression analysis, an expanded dataset comprising 289 BARIA individuals was used to evaluate hepatic expression of selected differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in relation to MASLD status. Raw liver gene counts were normalized using DESeq2 and transformed using a variance stabilizing transformation (VST) to reduce variability, particularly in low-count genes. VST-normalized expression levels were compared between MASLD and non-MASLD groups. For genes exhibiting significantly different expression between groups, binomial logistic regression models were applied to assess associations with MASLD (binary outcome), adjusting for age and sex as potential confounders. No additional variables were included to avoid overcorrection and loss of meaningful associations in this exploratory analysis.

To assess tissue-specific expression patterns of fructolysis genes within the study population, gene-level counts from both liver and jejunum were normalized using DESeq2, followed by VST. Genes of interest were extracted from the VST-normalized dataset for subsequent analysis and visualization.

Single-nuclei RNA-seq analysis

To confirm our bulk RNA-seq findings in an independent cohort, we used publicly available single-nuclei RNA-seq data from Kim et al. (2025), containing 18 livers from healthy (normal), MASLD and MASH participants. 28 We have downloaded the processed data (gene counts, gene and cell identifiers and metadata) from the original publication, and processed the data in Seurat, following the paper description, with minor modifications. Cell type allocation was performed using the same genes as the original paper. After subsetting only the normal and MASLD samples, we have conducted differential expression analysis with Seurat differential analysis functions, for the DEGs identified in the bulk RNA-seq that showed an association with MASLD. The findings were visualized as a volcano plot. Packages Seurat (v 5.3.0) and ggplot2 (v 4.0.0) were used for the analysis and visualization.

Isolation of fecal total genomic DNA and microbiota profiling

Extraction of total fecal genomic DNA was performed using a modified IHMS extraction protocol 29 involving homogenization and purification steps. Shotgun metagenomic sequencing libraries were prepared using a PCR-free method and sequenced with 150-bp paired-end reads per sample. Detailed description of the fecal total genomic DNA isolation is previously described. 24 Adapter removal and quality trimming of reads were performed using fastp (v 0.23.4). 30 Read quality was assessed using FastQC (v 0.12.1). Human reads were removed by mapping to the GRCh38.p14 human reference genome using Bowtie2 (v 2.2.5). 31 The remaining non-human reads were profiled for microbial composition using the HUMAnN(v 3.9), 32 MetaPhlan (v 4.1.1) 33 pipelines.

Fecal microbiota analysis

The phyloseq package (v 1.54.0) in R was used for preprocessing the microbiota data, including taxonomic agglomeration to the genus and species level. Fecal microbiota alpha diversity was quantified at the species level using the Shannon diversity index and its association with continuous fasting plasma fructose levels was assessed using Spearman rank correlation. Fecal microbiome beta-diversity at the species level was assessed using Bray-Curtis dissimilarity and associations with continuous fasting plasma fructose levels were tested with PERMANOVA (1000 permutations) using the vegan package (v 2.7-2). Subsequently, gut microbial species were filtered based on ≥20% prevalence across samples to minimize the risk of spurious associations in downstream analyses. Associations between fasting plasma fructose concentrations and the relative abundances of these microbial species were assessed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficients with FDR correction. Statistical significance was defined as an FDR-adjusted p value < 0.05.

In vitro bacterial culture experiments

Streptococcus parasanguinis DSM6778 and Gordonibacter pamelaeae DSM19378 were obtained from the Leibniz Institute DSMZ (DSMZ GmbH, Germany). Both strains were cultivated at 37 o C under anaerobic conditions (5% H2, 10% CO2, 85% N2) in YCFA media at pH 6.5 (10g/l casein hydrolysate, 10g/l yeast extract, 4g/l NaHCO3, 2.7g/l sodium acetate, 4.5g/l K2HPO4, 0.7g/l KH2PO4, 0.9g/l (NH4)2SO4, 0.9g/l NaCl, 0.09g/l MgSO4, 0.09g/l CaCl2, 0.5g/l L-cysteine, 1µg/l biotin, 1µg/l cobalamin, 3µg/l para-aminobenzoic acid, 5µg/l folic acid, 15µg/l pyridoxamine) with the addition of 10mM fructose as a carbon source for S. parasanguinis DSM6778 or the addition of 1% of L-arginine for G. pamelaeae DSM19378. The strains were grown in triplicate on single culture or mixed culture at a 1:1 ratio for 48h in a 96-well plate setting using YCFA media with the addition of 1% L-arginine for all conditions and varying fructose concentrations (no addition, 10mM or 20mM). Inoculation ratio was 5% for both tested strains from an active culture. Growth was monitored by measuring optical density at 600nm (OD600) during incubation at 37 o C for 48h using a Biotek Epoch 2 microplate reader (BioTek, USA).

Samples were collected at 0 and 48 hours of culture across all tested conditions and were centrifuged at 18.000 rpm for 5 minutes to precipitate microbial cells. The supernatant was analyzed for metabolites using HPLC using a SH1821 (SUGAR) column (Shodex, Japan). The mobile phase was sulfuric acid 0.05 N with a flow rate of 1mL/min. The column was kept at a temperature of 50 o C for RI and UV (210nm) measurements. Appropriate standards were used for the identification and quantification of metabolites.

Statistical analysis

Although this was an exploratory study in an ongoing cohort study, a power calculation was performed. Sample size calculation was based on a previous publication reporting a mean plasma fructose level of 46±25.22 μM in samples from subjects being evaluated for diabetes control. 34 Using a two-sample t-test for two independent groups (with a two-sided significance level of 0.05 and 80% power), we estimated that a sample size of approximately 38 subjects would be required to detect a 50% difference in plasma fructose concentrations between non-MASLD and MASLD subjects. As unequal group sizes are expected, a total of approximately 48 subjects would therefore be required to maintain adequate statistical power. Descriptive statistics for continuous variables following a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± Standerd Deviation (SD) and non-normally distributed variables as median Inter Quartile Range [IQR]. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Group-comparisons for normally distributed variables were performed using unpaired t-tests, and non-normally distributed variables were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The table 1 was created using the tableone package (v 0.13.2). Correlation analyses were conducted using Spearman's rank correlation. Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed p-value <0.05. All statistical analysis were performed using R (version 4.5.2). The underlying code for this study is not publicly available but may be made available to qualified researchers on reasonable request from the corresponding author.

Results

Patient baseline characteristics

This study included a subset of 49 morbidly obese subjects enrolled in the prospective BARIA cohort, with data collected prior to and during bariatric surgery. Morbid obesity was defined as a body mass index (BMI) ≥40 kg/m2 or a BMI ≥35 kg/m2 with obesity-related comorbidity. The baseline characteristics (Table 1) were stratified by MASLD status. Overall, the majority of this study population, 81.6%, was female and 57.1% of the subjects had MASLD. The median [IQR] age was 45.0 [37.0-53.0] years, and the mean BMI±SD before surgery was 38.8±2.9 kg/m 2 . Subjects with MASLD were significantly older than those without MASLD.

MASLD is associated with higher fasting plasma fructose levels

In contrast to the well-established link between high dietary fructose intake and MASLD, the association between fasting plasma fructose levels and MASLD remains poorly characterized. To investigate this, we compared fasting peripheral plasma fructose concentrations between subjects with and without MASLD. Subjects with MASLD showed significantly higher fasting fructose levels with a median [IQR] of 20.32 [17.45-23.82] µM, compared to 16.57 [15.77-20.33] µM in non-MASLD subjects (Figure 1a). Differential gene expression analysis of liver bulk RNA-seq data did not demonstrate upregulation of polyol pathway genes in association with increased fasting plasma fructose levels, with SORD expression even showing a significant downregulation (Figure 1b). Among lipogenic genes, MLXIPL -encoding transcription factor ChREBP -was marginally upregulated and FASN, the main regulator of hepatic FA synthesis, was significantly upregulated (Figure 1c). This is consistent with the well-established increase in DNL induced by high-fructose intake. [35] [36] [37] [38] [39] [40] [41] Furthermore, there was no significant correlation between fasting fructose and glucose concentrations. Collectively, these findings suggest that fasting plasma fructose levels do not reflect endogenous production by glucose conversion through the polyol pathway, but are rather indicative of (prolonged) high dietary intake.

Fasting plasma fructose concentrations positively correlate with postprandial ethanol excursions

Recent literature proposes that fructose toxicity arises in part from its conversion into ethanol by gut microbial fermentation. 23 Hence, we investigated the relationship between fasting plasma fructose levels and the postprandial increase in plasma ethanol concentrations. Plasma ethanol levels were measured in MMT samples obtained in a fasting state and at 120 minutes postprandially. A significant positive linear correlation between fasting plasma fructose levels and postprandial plasma ethanol excursions was identified (Figure 2a). Importantly, this correlation was independent of reported exogenous alcohol consumption (Supplementary Figure 1). This indicates that increased fasting plasma fructose concentrations, as a potential proxy of high dietary fructose exposure, may reflect enhanced microbial ethanol production capacity due to intestinal microbiota alterations and/or altered ethanol catabolism. The postprandial increase in plasma ethanol was significantly higher in MASLD subjects (median [IQR]: 35.83 [29.11-56.30] µM), than in non-MASLD subjects, (14.73 [-1.26-20.78] µM) (Figure 2b).

Correlations with fecal microbiota composition and ethanol catabolism at the transcriptomic level

To elucidate the mechanism underlying the link between fasting plasma fructose levels and postprandial plasma ethanol increase, we first assessed the overall gut microbiota composition in relation to fasting fructose. Fecal microbiota alpha-diversity did not show a significant association with fasting plasma fructose concentrations (Figure 2c). In contrast, fecal microbial beta-diversity was significantly associated with fasting fructose levels (Figure 2d), explaining 3.1% of variance, indicating distinct fecal microbiota compositions between subjects with high and low fasting fructose concentrations. Next, we assessed correlations between fasting fructose levels and the relative abundance of overall fecal microbiota species. Among all species analyzed, fasting fructose positively correlated with Streptococcus parasanguinis (Figure 2e), and negatively correlated with Gordonibacter pamelaeae (Figure 2f). This negative correlation was primarily influenced by higher fasting fructose concentrations among subjects lacking detectable G. pamelaeae abundance (Figure 2g). Correlations with other microbial species, as well as functional pathways were also assessed but did not reach statistical significance.

In addition to these correlations, bulk RNA-seq data showed a downregulation of multiple alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) encoding genes in relation to increased fasting plasma fructose levels (Figure 2h). This indicates that, besides potential enhanced endogenous ethanol production, alterations in ethanol catabolism coincide with the greater postprandial ethanol excursions observed in subjects with higher fasting plasma fructose levels.

In vitro growth and metabolite profiles of S. parasanguinis and G. pamelaeae in response to fructose

To investigate the correlation between fasting fructose and the two associated species, we performed in vitro experiments with S. parasanguinis and G. pamelaeae grown individually and in co-culture across two fructose concentrations. S. parasanguinis exhibited dose-dependent growth stimulation by fructose (Figure 3a), supporting its role as a fructose fermenter. G. pamelaeae, by contrast, showed no growth response to fructose (Figure 3b), consistent with its negative correlation with fasting fructose in our cohort. In co-culture, a dose-dependent fructose response was retained, though with an accelerated decline phase compared to both monocultures (Supplementary Figure 2). Metabolite profiling revealed distinct, species-dependent fermentation profiles (Figure 3c,d). In monoculture, S. parasanguinis produced lactate as its primary fermentation end-product in a fructose dose-dependent manner (Figure 3c), with acetate and formate as the most abundant secondary products, consistent with its classification as a lactic acid bacterium. In contrast, G. pamelaeae generated only low metabolite levels overall (Figure 3d), reflecting its limited fructose-fermenting capacity. Ethanol was not detected under any condition.

Hepatic and jejunal expression of fructolysis genes in a morbidly obese population

Both the liver and jejunum are tissues involved in fructose catabolism and exhibit high expression levels of fructolysis-related genes. Nevertheless, consensus expression data are usually derived from non-diseased tissues, which might not accurately reflect the tissue-specific expression profiles in a morbidly obese population. To obtain further insights into the involvement of the liver and jejunum in fructose catabolism among morbidly obese subjects, the expression of the genes encoding the main enzymes involved in fructolysis was compared across these tissues. VST-normalized expression levels of KHK, ALDOB, and TKFC were analyzed (Figure 4). In this population, KHK expression was highest in liver, whereas TKFC expression was highest in jejunum, consistent with consensus expression patterns reported by the Human Protein Atlas. 42, 43 ALDOB showed higher expression in jejunum than in liver contrasting Human Protein Atlas data showing greater hepatic expression. 44 Induction of Aldob expression in jejunum following fructose feeding has been observed in mice, 45 suggesting that jejunal ALDOB expression could be upregulated in a morbidly obese population as a metabolic adaptation to increased fructose exposure.

Increased fasting plasma fructose levels are associated with extensive hepatic transcriptional changes

To gain a deeper understanding of the pathways and specific genes underlying the association between fasting plasma fructose levels and MASLD, transcriptional changes related to increased fasting fructose concentrations were analyzed using DESeq2. As the liver is the primary target of fructose toxicity, the analysis focused on hepatic transcriptomics. 3969 significant DEGs were identified in liver tissue, of which 1522 were found to be upregulated and 2447 downregulated (Figure 5a). When applying a minimum absolute Log2FoldChange ≥1, this resulted in one upregulated and 19 downregulated DEGs. No distinct clustering of samples or gene expression patterns were observed among these DEGs in relation to fasting fructose levels (Supplementary Figure 3). Importantly, genes exhibiting smaller but statistically significant changes may still be crucial in driving meaningful transcriptional effects.

Functional enrichment analysis associates increased fasting plasma fructose levels with impaired mitochondrial energy metabolism and antioxidant defense GO Biological Process enrichment analysis on all DEGs demonstrated that upregulated genes were significantly enriched in processes related to DNA repair, transcriptional regulation and epigenetic modification (Figure 5b). Downregulated genes were predominantly enriched in processes related to mitochondrial function and energy metabolism (Figure 5c). Among the top 15 enriched KEGG pathways (Figure 5d), suppression of the oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) pathway was identified (Figure 5e), consistent with GO enrichment results showing downregulation of processes involved in mitochondrial energy metabolism. Examination of the KEGG OXPHOS pathway map (hsa00190) showed widespread downregulation of genes across all major electron transport chain (ETC) complexes (Supplementary Figure 4). All OXPHOS pathway DEGs were extracted and their DESeq2 results summarized, confirming broad impairment of mitochondrial energy metabolism (Supplementary Table 1).

Given the effects on mitochondrial energy metabolism, the opposing yet interconnected roles of βoxidation and lipogenesis, and the role of fructose as a hepatic lipogenic substrate, downstream βoxidation genes were also examined (Supplementary Table 2). The majority of mitochondrial β-oxidation genes were significantly downregulated, further highlighting the previously observed impaired mitochondrial energy metabolism. Notably, ACADM expression was significantly reduced, ACADVL was upregulated, suggesting a shift suggesting a shift towards the oxidation of long-chain fatty acids (LCFA).

Moreover, a suppression of the chemical carcinogenesis -reactive oxygen species (ROS) KEGG pathway was found (Figure 5d,f), suggesting alterations in the cellular oxidative stress response. The KEGG pathway map (hsa05208) revealed downregulation of key genes involved in antioxidant defense and cellular detoxification (Supplementary Figure 5). Detailed analysis of liver-expressed antioxidant genes showed significantly reduced expression of SOD1, CAT and nearly all components of the glutathione pathway, thioredoxin system, peroxiredoxin system and AKR family (Supplementary Table 3). Downregulation of the thermogenesis pathway was also identified (Figure 5d,g). Although uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1) -the mitochondrial protein central to thermogenesis -is not expressed in liver, downregulation of this pathway could reflect a broader suppression of mitochondrial activity and energy metabolism. Pathway map analysis (hsa04714) illustrated the previously observed downregulation of OXPHOS genes, alongside reduced expression of genes encoding protein kinase A (PKA) and its downstream target CREB1 (Supplementary Figure 6). CREB1, in turn, can induce expression of PPARGC1A, encoding the master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis, whose expression was similarly downregulated. Targeted analysis confirmed significant downregulation of PPARGC1A, CREB1 and nearly all genes encoding PKA subunits (Supplementary Table 4), indicating coordinated suppression of this metabolic signaling pathway that affects mitochondrial function. Together, these functional enrichment analyses results demonstrate that higher fasting plasma fructose levels are associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, encompassing impaired mitochondrial energy metabolism and antioxidant defense, in a morbidly obese population.

Gene expression changes in mitochondrial energy metabolism and antioxidant defense link elevated fasting fructose to increased MASLD risk

Expression of genes involved in mitochondrial energy metabolism and antioxidant defense were associated with fasting plasma fructose levels, which in turn were linked to MASLD histology scores. Therefore, we investigated whether these DEGs were differentially expressed by MASLD status. Gene expression was assessed in an expanded subset of the population (Supplementary Table 5), stratified by histology-based MASLD status. The analysis included all identified DEGs related to OXPHOS, β-oxidation, antioxidant defense and the regulatory genes encoding PKA, CREB1, and PPARGC1A. Among downregulated OXPHOS pathway genes, expression levels of NDUFA5 and NDUFB5, UQCRH and COX7A2 were significantly lower in MASLD subjects compared to non-MASLD subjects (Figure 6a-d). In logistic regression analysis, lower expression of each of these genes was independently associated with increased MASLD risk, after adjusting for the confounders age and sex (Supplementary Figure 7). Expression of β-oxidation genes ACADM and ACADVL also differed significantly between groups (Figure 6e,f). ACADM expression was lower in MASLD subjects, whereas ACADVL was higher, consistent with their expression changes related to elevated fasting fructose. Reduced ACADM expression was marginally associated with increased MASLD risk, while increased ACADVL expression was significantly associated with a higher MASLD risk (Supplementary Figure 7). Of the differentially expressed antioxidant defense genes, SOD1, PRDX6 and AKR1C4 expression levels were all significantly lower in MASLD subjects (Figure 6g-i). Lower expression of SOD1 and PRDX6 were each significantly associated with increased odds of MASLD, whereas AKR1C4 expression was not associated with MASLD risk (Supplementary Figure 7). Among the identified regulatory DEGs, the expression of PRKAR1A, which encodes the most highly expressed PKA regulatory subunit in liver, was significantly lower in MASLD subjects (Figure 7a), as were CREB1 and PPARGC1A expression (Figure 7b,c). Lower expression of PRKAR1A and PPARGC1A were each strongly associated with increased MASLD risk (Supplementary Figure 7), whereas CREB1 expression was not.

Collectively, these results suggest that downregulation of multiple OXPHOS and antioxidant defense genes, as well as dysregulation of β-oxidation genes, contribute to increased MASLD risk. The directionality of these associations was consistent with fasting fructose-associated gene expression changes. This indicates that impaired mitochondrial energy metabolism and antioxidant defense, potentially influenced by downregulation of PKA and PPARGC1A signaling, may represent a mechanistic link between increased fasting plasma fructose levels and MASLD pathogenesis.

Single-nuclei RNA-seq analysis highlights cell-specific downregulation of PPARGC1A in MASLD

To investigate the gene expression patterns of the DEGs associated with MASLD across distinct cell types, we re-analyzed single-nuclei RNA-seq data from an independent cohort. 28 This analysis included the subset of DEGs that were associated with MASLD in our bulk RNA-seq findings, in addition to differentially expressed lipogenic genes, comparing MASLD subjects to controls. We found significant downregulation of PPARGC1A in hepatocytes, endothelial cells, and myeloid cells (Figure 8), whereas FASN was markedly upregulated in these same cell types, as well as in B cells, T cells, and hepatic stellate cells (HSCs). Apart from the well-established role of FASN as a lipogenic gene in MASLD, these results further highlight the potential contribution of PPARGC1A downregulation -associated with elevated fasting plasma fructose -to MASLD pathogenesis. Based on the affected cell types, suppression of PPARGC1A may be associated not only with disruptions in metabolic homeostasis, but also with disturbances in vascular homeostasis and immune responses.

Discussion

High dietary fructose drives MASLD development, but the significance of fasting plasma fructose levels in relation to MASLD remains to be elucidated. Using plasma metabolites, fecal shotgun metagenomic data, and hepatic bulk-and single-nuclei RNA-seq data, we explored associations with fasting plasma fructose and their link to MASLD. We found significantly higher fasting plasma fructose concentrations in individuals with MASLD compared to those without. Fasting plasma fructose levels positively correlated with postprandial plasma ethanol excursions, which may result from microbial fructose fermentation, suggesting shifts in gut microbial composition and/or changes in host ethanol catabolism. This was further supported by associations between fasting fructose and gut microbiota composition, including a significant effect on beta-diversity and correlations with the abundances of S. parasanguinis and G. pamelaeae, as well as downregulation of hepatic ADH-encoding genes with increased fasting fructose concentrations. Finally, transcriptomic analysis revealed that heightened fasting plasma fructose levels are associated with impaired hepatic mitochondrial energy metabolism and antioxidant defense.

One previous cohort study linked elevated fasting plasma fructose to increased MASLD risk, hypothesizing it reflects endogenous production. 46 However, downregulation of SORD in our study suggests reduced conversion of sorbitol to fructose, challenging this hypothesis. Moreover, there was no correlation between fasting fructose and glucose in our study. This lack of correlation is not unexpected, given that fructose catabolism follows a distinct metabolic pathway and is largely independent of systemic glucose homeostasis. 47 Taken together with the upregulation of lipogenic genes, our findings are more consistent with a hepatic effect associated with increased dietary fructose exposure.

The gut microbiota can endogenously produce clinically relevant amounts of ethanol through sugar fermentation, which is implicated in MASLD pathogenesis. 24, 48, 49 The positive correlation between fasting plasma fructose and postprandial ethanol excursions implies that higher fasting fructose reflects a gut microbial composition more prone to ethanol production. Beta-diversity analysis confirmed distinct fecal microbiota compositions between subjects with low and high fasting fructose levels. Previous studies have shown that high-fructose intake alters microbial profiles and promotes aberrant gut microbiota composition 50 and in vitro data confirm fructose as a suitable substrate for ethanol production. 51 Together, this suggests that dietary fructose-driven modulation of the gut microbiota may explain the correlation between fasting plasma fructose and postprandial ethanol production, supporting a role for fructose as a substrate for endogenous ethanol production in vivo. Furthermore, high circulating (endogenously produced) plasma ethanol has previously been ascribed to TNFα-dependent impairment of ADH activity, 52 consistent with our observed downregulation of ADH encoding genes in relation to high fasting plasma fructose, This may contribute to the positive correlation between fasting fructose and postprandial ethanol excursions. S. parasanguinis was positively correlated with fasting plasma fructose levels, while G. pamelaeae was inversely correlated, yet neither species has been identified as an ethanol producer. 22 Despite limited literature on their abundance in relation to fructose exposure, both species have been described in the context of MASLD. S. parasanguinis is a typical oral commensal and enrichment of oral microbiota has been observed in females with MASLD. 53 Correspondingly, S. parasanguinis was among the top discriminatory species differentiating MASLD-cirrhosis from non-MASLD controls, 54 and positively correlated with polyamines, 53 whose excessive catabolism can drive oxidative stress, which is implicated in the development of MASLD. [55] [56] [57] G. pamelaeae converts ellagic acid into urolithin C, 58 a metabolite that protects against MASLD by maintaining intestinal barrier integrity and mitigating gut dysbiosis in a murine model. 59 Accordingly, this species was negatively associated with MASLD and predicted healthy status in overweight individuals, 60 further supporting a protective role. Our in vitro experiments confirm that S. parasanguinis ferments fructose, consistent with its positive correlation with fasting fructose, producing lactate and acetate as major fermentation end-products in a fructose dose-dependent manner. As expected, no ethanol was detected. Nevertheless, these fermentation products may potentiate the hepatotoxic effects of fructose by serving as substrates for hepatic de novo lipogenesis, 21 or indirectly as cross-feeding substrates for bacteria capable of producing toxic metabolites such as ethanol. Collectively, increased S. parasanguinis and decreased G. pamelaeae abundance are associated with pathogenic effects linked to MASLD and with gut dysbiosis, which may reflect unrecognized enrichment of ethanol-producing strains within the broader microbial community.

Dietary fructose exerts metabolic effects beyond promoting hepatic lipogenesis. In rodent models, highfructose diets primarily impaired mitochondrial function including β-oxidation, 61, 62 and OXPHOS was the most significantly downregulated pathway in mice fed a high-fructose, high-fat diet, compared to a highfat diet alone. 63 Consistently, our study revealed downregulation of many hepatic genes involved in βoxidation and OXPHOS. Moreover, key antioxidant genes including CAT, SOD1, glutathione peroxidases (GPx), thioredoxins and periredoxins were downregulated. Animal studies similarly showed impaired hepatic antioxidant defense in high-fructose fed rats, demonstrated by reduced mRNA and protein expression of Cat, Sod1 and GPx, [64] [65] [66] along with decreased mitochondrial Sod1 and Cat enzyme activities. 67, 68 In our study, the expression of multiple genes involved in these processes was linked to MASLD risk. This aligns with previous studies that identified mitochondrial dysfunction as a driver of MASLD pathogenesis and progression, [69] [70] [71] [72] [73] with impaired OXPHOS, β-oxidation, and antioxidant defense playing key roles particularly in advanced disease stages. [69] [70] [71] [72] [73] Mitochondrial dysfunction, reflected by reduced βoxidation and carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 (CPT1) activity, preceded MASLD development in a rat model 74 , also emphasizing the role of early mitochondrial impairment in disease onset. ACADVL upregulation and its association with MASLD in the present study, despite overall β-oxidation gene downregulation, could reflect a metabolic adaptation to a Western-style diet high in fructose and fat. Approximately 95% of dietary lipids are triacylglycerols, primarily composed of LCFA, 75 and ACADVL encodes the main enzyme catalyzing the initial step of LCFA β-oxidation. 76 Additionally, CPT1A expression -which facilitates mitochondrial import of LCFAs 77 -was not decreased in this study potentially reflecting a coordinated response to increased LCFA load.

In addition, higher fasting fructose was associated with reduced PPARGC1A expression in our study, which is consistent with the demonstrated mitochondrial dysfunction. Correspondingly, high-fructose feeding in rats impaired mitochondrial biogenesis, indicated by reduced mitochondrial DNA copy number, downregulation of Ppargc1a, 14 and increased hepatic acetylation of Pgc-1α, 78 an inactivating modification. Furthermore, reduced mitochondrial biogenesis contributes to mitochondrial dysfunction in MASLD, indicated by lower hepatic expression of PPARGC1A and reduced hepatic ETC activity in MASLD patients. 79 Collectively, these results highlight that the disruption of mitochondrial regulation and function in association with elevated fasting plasma fructose levels can be linked to MASLD, with fasting fructose likely reflecting dietary fructose exposure that contributes to these metabolic alterations. Although fasting fructose was also associated with gut microbial composition, we hypothesize that the association with mitochondrial function primarily reflects a direct hepatic effect of fructose rather than microbiomemediated mechanisms, given that peripheral fructose concentrations are considered indicative of hepatic fructose exposure.

A significant strength of this study is its focus on associations with fasting fructose levels in humans rather than fructose exposure, providing a clinically relevant perspective and addressing a gap left by predominantly animal-based studies. Furthermore, the integrated approach combining both transcriptional and microbial profiling facilitated examination of host-microbe interactions in the context of fasting plasma fructose and MASLD. Limitations include the small study population consisting exclusively of individuals with morbid obesity, limiting the generalizability of our findings to non-obese individuals or those with BMI <35 kg/m 2, and constraining the establishment of clinically and biologically relevant cut-off values for fasting fructose concentrations. Additionally, the absence of small intestinal microbiota data as well as functional microbial analyses, restricts the characterization of gut microbial dynamics in this key site of fructose fermentation and ethanol production. Finally, the cross-sectional design of this study limits the ability to establish causality.

While fasting glucose is a well-established metabolic (glycemic) marker, fasting fructose may provide complementary information due to its distinct hepatic metabolism and effects. Our data challenge the notion that fasting plasma fructose levels merely reflect endogenous production, instead suggesting it primarily indicates dietary exposure based on similarities in associated transcriptional changes to those previously observed in fructose-feeding models. We demonstrated that fasting plasma fructose levels positively correlate with postprandial ethanol excursions in plasma, likely mediated by gut microbial reshaping and impaired host ethanol clearance, as indicated by downregulation of ADH genes. Furthermore, fructose and its catabolic products have been linked to hepatic mitochondrial dysfunction, which we similarly observed with increased fasting plasma fructose. This highlights a key mechanism proposed to contribute to MASLD-associated metabolic disruption. Together, these findings suggest that fasting fructose could be an indicator of hepatic metabolic stress, contributing to the characterization of the metabolic state of the liver. Future longitudinal studies are needed to evaluate whether fasting plasma fructose is a reliable proxy for dietary fructose exposure and can serve as a novel (additional) risk marker for MASLD, while also elucidating the potential causal and mediating mechanisms, including the role of the gut microbiome.
