Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Abstract

Background: Children who have not received the first dose of a diphtheria-tetanus-pertussiscontaining vaccine are classified as zero-dose. Understanding how many children remain without this dose beyond infancy, the correlates, and their geographic distribution can inform routine and catch-up vaccination services in Ethiopia. This study aimed to estimate the burden of zero-dose, identify its correlates, and map its spatial distribution among children aged 12-35 months in Ethiopia.

Methods: We analyzed data from 2,097 children aged 12-35 months in the Pathways for Ethiopia survey, a recent national population-based cross-sectional household survey conducted from August to November 2025. The burden estimates and regression analyses accounted for sampling weights, stratification, and clustering. Adjusted prevalence ratios (aPRs) were obtained through marginal standardization of survey-weighted logistic regression models. Spatial autocorrelation, hotspot, and spatial-scan analyses assessed geographic clustering. Ordinary kriging predicted prevalence at unsampled locations.

The weighted prevalence of zero-dose status was 20.6% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 15.9-26.3), with regional point estimates ranging from 0.8% in Addis Ababa to 35.9% in Somali.

Its burden was 20.4% among children aged 12-23 months and 20.8% among those aged 24-35 months. Higher burden was associated with no antenatal care compared with four or more visits (aPR: 2.39; 95% CI: 1.61-3.53), unimproved compared with improved drinking-water sources (aPR: 1.52; 95% CI: 1.18-1.97), and caregivers rating vaccination as less than very important compared with very important (aPR: 2.68; 95% CI: 2.03-3.54). Zero-dose prevalence showed significant spatial clustering (Moran's I = 0.229; p = 0.0001). Hotspots occurred among sampled communities in Somali, Oromia, Central Ethiopia, Afar, and South Ethiopia. Predicted prevalence was highest across parts of eastern and south-central Ethiopia.

Background

Childhood immunization prevents serious illness and death, yet millions of children continue to miss essential vaccine doses. Children who have not received the first dose of a diphtheriatetanus-pertussis-containing vaccine (DTP1) are classified as zero-dose [1]. Globally, an estimated 13.5 million children were zero-dose in 2025 [2]. Missing this first dose is an important indicator of gaps in the reach of routine immunization services. Reaching these children is central to the Immunization Agenda 2030 commitment to equitable access to vaccination [1].

Ethiopia has a substantial burden of zero-dose children, with considerable geographic variation.

Analyses of the 2019 Mini Demographic and Health Survey and the 2023 immunization survey estimated zero-dose prevalence at 23.7% and 24.8%, respectively, among children aged 12-23 months, which indicated that its prevalence is high in Somali and much lower prevalence in Addis Ababa [3, 4]. Local concentrations of children who missed the first dose have also been identified in Somali, Afar, and parts of Amhara and Oromia [5]. These patterns show that the national burden is unevenly distributed and that regional averages may not show local concentrations of high zero-dose prevalence. Research among children aged 12-35 months in selected underserved settings similarly documented considerable local differences [5].

Fewer antenatal care visits, delivery outside health institutions, and rural residence were identified as factors associated with zero-dose from the finding generated in the 2019 survey [3].

Consistently, finding generated from 2023 survey showed that antenatal care, perceived vaccination benefits, and distance to vaccination services, with differences in these relationships across locations were factors associated with zero-dose [4]. These findings identify maternal healthcare contact and vaccination perceptions as relevant factors for further investigation [3, 4].

Ethiopia introduced a catch-up vaccination policy in 2022 and renewed implementation of the Big Catch-Up initiative in 2025 [6]. To minimize the zero dose status among children, routine immunization services were strengthened, underserved and hard to reach populations were reached, community engagement and health education, strengthening health extension programs and integration of immunization with other essential health services were implemented as strategies in Ethiopia [6].

The 2023 national spatial analysis also excluded Tigray because of security constraints [4].

These studies establish the importance of geographic and population differences, while leaving a need for updated national evidence that includes older children who remain without a first dose.

As catch-up services expand, understanding the size and geographic distribution of this unmet vaccination need can help determine whom to reach and where to focus vaccination activities.

Using a national population-based household survey conducted in 2025, this study aimed to estimate national and regional zero-dose prevalence among children aged 12-35 months in Ethiopia, identify correlates among child, maternal, household, and service characteristics, and examine spatial clustering and geographic patterns of zero-dose status.

Methods

Study design, setting, and participants

This study used data from the Pathways for Ethiopia national population-based cross-sectional household survey, conducted from August to November 2025 across all 12 regional states and the two chartered cities. Information on children was collected in the survey of women of reproductive age. The survey used a stratified two-stage cluster sampling design. In the first stage, census enumeration areas (EAs) were stratified by region and urban-rural residence and selected for the survey. In the second stage, eligible women were listed and sampled within the selected EAs. Fieldwork was completed in 247 of the 251 planned EAs. The analysis included children aged 12-35 months whose first pentavalent dose status could be determined from the survey. Records with incomplete or invalid sampling or geographic information were excluded.

Variables and measurements

Zero-dose status was defined as nonreceipt of the first pentavalent dose, a diphtheria-tetanuspertussis-containing vaccine. Vaccination status was assessed from vaccination records and caregiver reports, with records taking precedence when available. The independent variables were child age, region, residence, maternal age, maternal education, household financial situation, water and sanitation, antenatal care (ANC), place of delivery, walking time to the nearest health facility, self-reported local armed conflict, participation in decisions about child healthcare, and perceived vaccination importance. Caregivers rated the importance of vaccines for their child's health on a four-point scale: "not at all important," "a little important," "moderately important," or "very important" [7]. For analysis, the first three responses were grouped as "less than very important," with "very important" as the reference category.

Survey-weighted analysis

Data were analyzed using R version 4.3.3 [8]. The national and regional zero dose prevalence was estimated using the final normalized survey weights, accounting for clustering within 247 EAs and stratification by region and urban-rural residence (27 strata). Design-based 95% confidence intervals were calculated on the logit scale and back-transformed. Regional estimates with coefficients of variation of 0.30 or greater were considered imprecise. Survey-weighted logistic regression was used to examine associations with zero-dose status. The adjusted analysis included 2,015 children with complete data for all model variables. Adjusted prevalence ratios (aPRs) and 95% confidence intervals were obtained by marginal standardization of predicted probabilities, adjusting for region, residence and the other covariates. Overall design-based Wald tests assessed factors with more than two categories.

Spatial analysis

Spatial autocorrelation in zero-dose prevalence was assessed using global Moran's I. Local Moran's I was used to identify local clusters and spatial outliers, while Getis-Ord Gi* identified hotspots and cold spots [9, 10]. Local p values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate procedure. A Bernoulli spatial scan was used to identify geographic clusters of high zero-dose prevalence, with zero-dose children classified as cases and other eligible children as controls [11]. The primary cluster and nonoverlapping secondary clusters were mapped.

Ordinary kriging was used to predict zero-dose prevalence at unsampled locations using surveyweighted estimates from sampled EAs [4]. Linear, spherical and exponential semivariograms were compared using leave-one-EA-out cross-validation. Predictions were generated on a 10-km grid using the 16 nearest EAs. The exponential model was further evaluated using five geographically separated validation groups. Its prediction error was compared with that of a prediction based on the pooled prevalence in the training sample.

Geographic variation in associations with zero-dose status was examined using binomial models with a spatial intercept and a spatially varying coefficient. Ten contrasts involving child, maternal, household and service characteristics were assessed among children with complete model data. Tests of geographic variation were adjusted for multiple comparisons, and spatial cross-validation compared each model with a corresponding constant-coefficient model. Local odds ratios were mapped for contrasts showing geographic variation and improved prediction, at locations with sufficient observations in both comparison groups. Each odds ratio compared the specified category with its reference group.

Discussion

Approximately one in five children aged 12-35 months had not received the first pentavalent dose in 2025. Zero-dose status was associated with maternal healthcare use, household living conditions and perceptions of vaccination. High zero-dose prevalence clustered in sampled communities in eastern and south-central Ethiopia. These findings show that missed first doses remain a substantial concern beyond infancy and identify communities where vaccination services should be strengthened.

The 20.6% prevalence (95% CI: 15.9-26.3) was broadly comparable to the 23.7% reported by Muchie and colleagues from the 2019 Mini Demographic and Health Survey of children aged 12-23 months [3] and the 24.8% reported by Endehabtu and colleagues from a 2023 programme evaluation of the same younger age group in ten regions and two cities [4]. Tamir and Agimas also reported geographic variation using other spatial analyses of the 2019 survey [12, 13].

Selected underserved Ethiopian communities had approximately one-third of children classified as zero-dose [5, 14]. Differences in age groups, sampled populations and zero-dose definitions may contribute to the variation between estimates and limit conclusions about changes over time [15].

Somali and Oromia had the highest regional point estimates in this survey, although regional confidence intervals were often wide. Endehabtu and colleagues likewise reported elevated prevalence in Somali and Afar, while their 2023 survey could not include Tigray; Shiferie and colleagues identified concentrations in Somali and Afar in underserved settings [4, 5]. Similar geographic differences have been reported for broader childhood vaccination outcomes in Ethiopia [16] [17] [18] [19] [20]. Spatial studies of BCG and measles vaccination have also documented geographic differences within Ethiopia [21] [22] [23]. The repeated identification of high-prevalence areas supports prioritizing these communities when planning outreach and identifying children who have missed vaccination.

ANC was found to be a factor associated with zero-dose among children. Children whose mothers reported no ANC visits had higher zero-dose prevalence as compared with those mothers reported four or more visits. This finding is consistent with the study conducted in Kenya [24] and Ethiopia in 2019 [3]. Interviews in underserved Ethiopian communities documented irregular immunization sessions and inadequate information from health workers [25]. The observed association between ANC attendance and childhood vaccination may also reflect broader engagement with the healthcare system, as mothers who attend ANC may be more likely to use immunization services for their children. ANC visits may provide opportunities for mothers to receive information about childhood vaccination schedules and develop trust in vaccination services [4].

Lower perceived vaccination importance was also strongly associated with zero-dose status.

Endehabtu and colleagues similarly reported an association involving caregivers' perceptions of vaccination benefits [4]. Reviews of childhood immunization have identified parental attitudes, limited information and communication barriers among the reasons for non-vaccination [26, 27].

These findings support clear explanations of vaccination benefits and opportunities for caregivers to discuss their concerns. Because perceptions and vaccination status were measured at the same time, present views may precede missed vaccination or reflect previous service encounters.

An unimproved drinking-water source was noted as a factor associated with zero-dose status.

Children living in households with unimproved drinking water sources had 52% higher prevalence of zero-dose as compared with those living in households with improved drinking water sources. Water source may reflect household living conditions and access to basic services.

Wendt and colleagues, using surveys from 80 low-and middle-income countries, found that children who had received no DTP-containing vaccine were more likely to experience other forms of deprivation, with particularly consistent associations involving water, sanitation and maternal education [28]. An analysis across 33 sub-Saharan African countries also linked receipt of no vaccine with household wealth and maternal healthcare use [29]. Limited access to improved water and vaccination may therefore reflect overlapping household disadvantages. The finding supports considering broader living conditions when identifying children missed by immunization services.

In the descriptive analysis, zero-dose prevalence was higher among rural children and those whose mothers reported non-institutional delivery. However, the adjusted prevalence ratios did not show statistically significant associations with either residence or place of delivery. In the 2019 Mini Demographic and Health Survey, Muchie and colleagues did report adjusted associations with rural residence and noninstitutional delivery [3]. Maternal education, household financial situation and sanitation also showed no clear overall adjusted association in the present study. Earlier Ethiopian research on full vaccination found inequalities by wealth and maternal education [16]. Differences in vaccination outcomes, populations and covariate adjustment may contribute to these findings. Full vaccination measures completion of several doses, so those findings do not directly contradict results for first-dose nonreceipt. The absence of a clear adjusted association here does not establish that these characteristics are unrelated to vaccination access.

The overall association with walking time to the nearest health facility was not statistically significant (p = 0.052), although category-specific estimates were higher for longer walking times. Okwaraji and colleagues found lower third-pentavalent-dose coverage among children living at least an hour from a health post in rural northwestern Ethiopia [30]. Their study included children aged 12-59 months and used geographically estimated travel time, whereas the present survey used reported walking time and examined first-dose nonreceipt. The present estimates suggest a possible access barrier, although the overall association was not statistically conclusive.

Self-reported local armed conflict was associated with lower zero-dose prevalence (aPR: 0.70, 95% CI: 0.50-0.99), and children whose mothers decided alone on child healthcare had lower prevalence than those with joint decisions (aPR: 0.64, 95% CI: 0.44-0.94). The timing and intensity of reported conflict in relation to vaccination were not established. Household decisionmaking responses did not identify who arranged or provided vaccination. Qualitative research in underserved Ethiopian communities identified gender norms and limited support for caregivers as barriers to childhood vaccination [25]. The decision-making association may therefore reflect household circumstances not fully captured by the response categories. Both inverse associations may be affected by residual confounding and should not be interpreted as protective effects.

The associations of ANC attendance and participation in decisions about child healthcare with zero-dose status varied across sampled communities with adequate local data. Endehabtu and colleagues also reported geographic variation in associations involving ANC and vaccination perceptions [4]. Together, these findings suggest that national average associations may not describe every locality. The local maps can help guide assessment of maternal healthcare use and household decision-making in the identified communities.

The local analyses identified concentrations of high zero-dose prevalence within the broader regional pattern. Four communities were classified as high-high clusters and nine as hotspots in Somali; the primary scan cluster extended across parts of Somali, Oromia and Sidama. Nigerian surveys have also mapped zero-dose concentrations while examining first-dose status separately from other vaccination outcomes [31, 32]. In Zambia, geospatial analysis found differences in zero-dose prevalence within and between health-facility catchment areas [33]. In Kikwit, Democratic Republic of the Congo, Mutwadi and colleagues found scan clusters spanning several health areas and associations with distance and absence of community-health-worker visits [34]. Although their city sample differs from a national survey, the findings illustrate how local concentrations can cross administrative borders. Coordination between neighbouring service areas may therefore help reach children in the identified clusters.

The predicted surface showed higher prevalence in parts of eastern and south-central Ethiopia, consistent with the clusters identified in sampled communities. Spatial cross-validation showed a modest reduction in prediction error compared with predictions based on the pooled prevalence in the training sample. Other spatial vaccination studies have demonstrated the value of detailed maps for identifying gaps while emphasizing the importance of survey design, geographic scale and prediction uncertainty [35] [36] [37] [38] [39] [40]. The 10-km predictions can guide local assessment, although their accuracy is limited where sampled communities are sparse.

The 2024-25 Ethiopia Demographic and Health Survey reported first-pentavalent-dose receipt in 70.6% of children aged 12-23 months, equivalent to 29.4% without the dose, based on vaccination cards or maternal reports [41]. The present study found first-dose nonreceipt of 20.6% among children aged 12-35 months. Differences in age eligibility and survey methods limit direct comparison. Nevertheless, both surveys indicate a substantial unmet need for firstdose vaccination beyond infancy.

The mapped concentrations support prioritizing first-dose vaccination services in the identified communities. The primary scan zone crossed Somali, Oromia and Sidama, suggesting that reaching missed children may require coordination across neighbouring service areas. Reliable outreach and clear information for caregivers are relevant priorities, consistent with barriers reported in underserved Ethiopian settings [25]. Later-dose dropout, documented separately in these settings [42], also requires continued contact after children enter the schedule. Research on the immunization cascade across low-and middle-income countries also documents dropout after the first dose [43]. Identifying zero-dose children should therefore be accompanied by follow-up to complete the recommended doses.

Strengths and limitations

This study drew on a national survey covering Ethiopia's regional states and chartered cities, accounted for complex sampling in prevalence estimates and adjusted association analyses, and examined geographic clustering using several spatial methods.

This study finding might present with limitations such as a reporting bias as zero-dose status was assessed from vaccination records and caregiver reports; incomplete records and recall errors may affect classification. Several regional estimates were imprecise and some EAs included few eligible children. Maternal health-service information referred to the woman's most recent eligible pregnancy and may not relate to the pregnancy of the child included in this analysis. The predicted values depend on the distribution of sampled EAs, which limits local interpretation, as discussed in other spatial vaccination studies. The cross-sectional design and correlated household and service factors preclude causal attribution.

Conclusions

Approximately one in five children aged 12-35 months had not received the first pentavalent dose. ANC utilization, drinking-water sources, and caregivers' perceived importance of vaccination were associated with higher zero-dose prevalence.

High prevalence clustered among sampled communities in eastern and south-central Ethiopia. Immunization programmes should prioritize identifying and vaccinating missed children in these communities, including those aged 24-35 months, through age-appropriate catch-up services. Strengthening links between maternal healthcare and immunization, addressing caregiver concerns, and reviewing the regularity and reach of outreach services are relevant programme priorities. Local planning should combine the geographic findings with current service and household information.
