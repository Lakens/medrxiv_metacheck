To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 9 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. Abstract

Methamphetamine use is common among people with HIV (PWH) and is associated with worse neurocognitive and clinical outcomes, but how it reshapes HIV-associated immune dysregulation across cell types and compartments is unknown. In this cross-sectional and observational study, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from controls without HIV, PWH without methamphetamine use and PWH who use methamphetamine, then scored pathway enrichment, cytokine response and transcription factor activity in each cell population. We then profiled plasma from the same three groups in a larger set of participants by targeted proteomics, with PWH who use cocaine added as a stimulant comparison. In the single-cell data, methamphetamine use acted in opposite directions across lineages. HIVassociated changes were reversed most often in monocytes and CD8 + T cells, with interferon, antigen presentation and JAK-STAT programs shifting in monocytes and IFN-γ, IL-2 and MYC programs shifting in CD8 + T cells, whereas HIV-associated proliferative and TNF signaling programs persisted in CD4 + and γδ T cells of Meth+HIV participants. Central memory CD8 + T cells of Meth+HIV participants showed reduced type 1 effector programs. In plasma, HIV was associated with a cytotoxic lymphocyte activation signature (KLRK1, IFN-γ, TNF, granzyme B and CXCL9) that was further elevated with methamphetamine use, together with higher IL-6, CXCL13 and neurofilament heavy chain. IFN-γ was the most connected protein in the plasma interaction network, linking a cytotoxic module to a lymphoid chemokine module. These data define lineageand compartment-specific immune consequences of methamphetamine use in PWH and nominate IFN-γ signaling, acting through monocyte antigen presentation and CD8 + T cell effector programs, together with TNF, IL-6 and CXCL13, as candidate mediators of neuroimmune dysfunction in PWH who use methamphetamine.

Introduction

Methamphetamine use has resurged sharply in the United States. Approximately 2.4 million Americans reported past-year methamphetamine use in 2024, and stimulant-involved overdose deaths have tripled over the past decade [1] [2] [3] . Methamphetamine use is especially common among people with HIV (PWH), with estimated rates roughly seven times those of the general population 1, 4 , and among sexual and gender minorities, persistent methamphetamine use accounts for a large share of new HIV infections 5 . For PWH, the consequences extend well beyond transmission risk, as methamphetamine use is associated with accelerated disease progression, poorer adherence to antiretroviral therapy (ART) and worse neurocognitive outcomes [6] [7] [8] . Between 53% and 58% of PWH who use methamphetamine show neurocognitive impairment, compared with approximately 40% of those with either condition alone, with additive deficits in attention, executive function and decision-making 7, [9] [10] [11] . The biological mechanisms underlying these compounding effects remain poorly understood, and no targeted interventions are approved.

Beyond its effects on plasma viral load, HIV infection produces broad and persistent immunological consequences, even under sustained viral suppression. ART controls viremia, but chronic immune activation persists, characterized by dysregulated inflammatory signaling and altered myeloid and lymphoid cell function 12, 13 . Among the most durable features of this state is the expansion of CD8 + T lymphocytes, which does not normalize over time, even with viral suppression [14] [15] [16] . Activated CD8 + T cells accumulate in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and brain during acute and chronic infection [17] [18] [19] , and ongoing T cell activation in CSF is associated with neurocognitive decline 20 . CSF CD8 + T cells that express interferon-γ (IFN-γ) correlate with the severity of HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders, and IFN-γ-producing, non-cytolytic CD8 + T cells appear to drive pathology through chronic inflammatory signaling, whereas cytolytic CD8 + T cells help control viral replication 21 . How the balance between these CD8 + T cell states is determined in circulating cells, and how substance use alters this balance, has not been defined in PWH.

Methamphetamine alters this immune landscape through several mechanisms. In mice with chronic viral infection, methamphetamine increases PD-1 expression and chemokine receptors associated with brain trafficking on CD8 + T cells and impairs T cell subset progression and polyfunctionality 22, 23 . In PWH on suppressive ART, methamphetamine use alters T cell function and promotes CD8 + T cell proliferation across memory subsets 24 , and cumulative stimulant exposure is associated with adverse immune function outcomes 25 . In SIV-infected macaques, methamphetamine increases inflammatory and Toll-like receptor signaling programs and reprograms gene expression in brain microglia toward cellular profiles linked to inflammation, susceptibility to viral infection, altered metabolism, and cell death [26] [27] [28] . Methamphetamine also disrupts the blood-brain barrier through oxidative stress and tight junction degradation, increasing peripheral leukocyte entry into the central nervous system (CNS) 29, 30 . Mechanistic attention in neuroHIV has largely focused on myeloid cells, particularly microglia and monocyte-derived macrophages [31] [32] [33] [34] [35] [36] [37] , whereas substance use neuroscience has rarely considered adaptive immunity. We recently showed that CD8 + T cells recruited to mesolimbic structures by the chemokine CXCL13 are required for opioid self-administration 38 , adding to evidence that CXCL13 and CD8 + T cells can act directly on neural function [39] [40] [41] [42] [43].

Despite these advances, how methamphetamine use reshapes the immune landscape of PWH across cell types and compartments has not been systematically defined. Most human studies have only measured a limited set of circulating markers or a single immune population. This leaves many questions unanswered such as which effects of methamphetamine compound those of HIV, which oppose them, and whether peripheral changes are accompanied by circulating markers of CNS injury. Here, we combined single-cell RNA sequencing of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from controls without HIV, PWH without methamphetamine use, and PWH who use methamphetamine with pathway, cytokine response, transcription factor and protein interaction analyses. We paired these data with targeted plasma proteomics, including a panel of neurological and CNS-related proteins, in a cohort that also included PWH who use cocaine. We find that methamphetamine use leads to lineage-specific alterations. HIV-associated activation programs persist in CD4 + and γδ T cells, whereas methamphetamine use shifts interferon and antigen presentation programs in monocytes, and IFN-γ, IL-2 and MYC programs in CD8 + T cells, in the opposite direction of HIV infection. The monocyte changes we identify span the antigen presentation machinery and parallel interferon signatures in CD8 + T cells. Within the CD8 + T cell compartment, central memory cells of Meth+HIV participants show reduced type 1 effector programs. In plasma, HIV is associated with a rise in neurofilament heavy chain and a cytotoxic lymphocyte activation signature that is further elevated with methamphetamine use.

Materials & Methods

Study participants and design

Peripheral blood samples were obtained from adults participating in research examining the effects of HIV and stimulant use on neuroimmune and neurobehavioral functioning. The study sample consisted of PWH who used methamphetamine in the past month (Meth+HIV), along with comparison groups of PWH without methamphetamine use (HIV) and people without HIV (Control). Participant characteristics are summarized in Table 1. HIV diagnosis was confirmed through electronic health records. In addition, participants with HIV had to be on stable ART and have plasma HIV RNA levels <200 copies/mL quantified via PCR testing on the day of the blood draw. For participants without HIV, a rapid HIV-1/2 antibody test (OraQuick ADVANCE®) was conducted using a droplet of whole blood collected by pinprick. Participants in the Meth+HIV group reported at least weekly use of methamphetamine in the past month and had a positive urine drug screen for methamphetamine. Participants in the HIV and Control groups reported 0 days of methamphetamine use in the past 12 months, no history of regular methamphetamine use, and no lifetime stimulant use disorder. For plasma proteomics, PWH who used cocaine (Coc+HIV) were also included, with eligibility criteria that mirrored those for the Meth+HIV group, with cocaine substituted as the primary drug of use. Nicotine, alcohol, and marijuana use was permissible in all groups. Exclusion criteria included limited English proficiency or inability to read, severe neurological disease or injury (other than HIV-related infections), acute psychiatric symptoms, autoimmune or hematological disorders affecting immune function, organ or bone marrow transplantation, dialysis, and receipt of immunomodulatory therapies (e.g., chemotherapy, radiation, blood transfusions). Study procedures were approved by the Wake Forest Health Sciences IRB, and all participants provided written informed consent prior to completing any study activities.

Blood collection and PBMC isolation

Non-fasting blood samples were collected by peripheral venipuncture. Plasma for proteomic analyses was obtained from EDTA-treated blood samples using standard processing procedures, aliquoted, and stored at -80°C until assay. For transcriptomic analyses, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from Cell Preparation Tube (CPT) vacutainers processed within 2 hours of collection. CPT tubes were centrifuged at 1,800 × g for 30 minutes at 20°C, and the PBMC fraction was harvested, washed twice in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) by centrifugation at 300 × g for 15 minutes, and assessed for cell concentration and viability using trypan blue exclusion and automated cell counting. PBMCs were cryopreserved in fetal bovine serum containing 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), aliquoted at approximately 5 × 10⁵ cells per vial, frozen at -80°C using a controlled-rate freezing method, and transferred to liquid nitrogen storage within 3 days of processing. Cryopreserved PBMCs remained in liquid nitrogen until RNA extraction and sequencing.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

PBMCs (n = 4 per group) were processed on the Parse Biosciences Evercode WT platform. PBMCs were fixed and permeabilized with the Evercode Cell Fixation kit (ECFC3300) following the protocol of the manufacturer, then underwent split-pool combinatorial barcoding with the Evercode Whole Transcriptome kit v3 (ECWT3300) to multiplex samples 44 . Libraries were prepared as specified by the manufacturer, checked on a BioAnalyzer 2100 (Agilent) and a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher), and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus (2 × 100 bp). Reads were aligned to hg38 and demultiplexed with split-pipe v1.5.1 (Parse Biosciences).

Single-cell data processing, quality control and cell type annotation

Gene expression matrices were processed and analyzed in R with Seurat v5.4.0 45 . To exclude lowquality cells and potential multiplets, we removed cells with fewer than 300 or more than 7,000 unique genes, fewer than 1,000 or more than 50,000 unique RNA transcripts, or more than 10% mitochondrial gene expression. Expression values of the remaining cells were normalized with SCTransform 46 and clustered on a shared nearest neighbor graph, with UMAP for visualization. After quality control, 42,140 cells were carried into secondary analysis, with a mean of 94,000 reads per cell. Cell identities were assigned from the expression of canonical lineage markers, with MS4A1, CD79A and CD74 for B cells, LYZ, LST1 and FCN1 for monocytes, FCRL6, PDGFD and KLRD1 for NK cells, TRDC for γδ T cells, and CD3E, CD4, IL7R, CCR7, CD8A, CD8B and NKG7 for CD4 + and CD8 + T cells. CD8 + T cell subcluster identities were assigned from marker gene expression, with LTB, IL7R, SELL, CCR7, TCF7, MAL and NRCAM for naive and central memory CD8 + T cells, NKG7, PRF1 and GNLY for cytotoxic effector CD8 + T cells, GZMK and IFNG-AS1 for GZMK⁺ memory CD8 + T cells, and CX3CR1, FCRL6 and TBX21 for CX3CR1⁺ effector memory CD8 + T cells.

Pseudobulk differential expression

For each cell type, we summed counts across all cells from each participant (AggregateExpression) and tested differential expression with DESeq2 through the Seurat FindMarkers function 47 . This method statistically controls for differences in cell counts across samples. A Pseudobulk approach was selected because they demonstrate reasonable control of the type 1 error rates, without the computational burden and failure to converge problems associated with linear mixed models and smaller sample sizes. We computed differential expression in six PBMC populations (CD4 + T, CD8 + T, γδ T, B and NK cells and monocytes) and five CD8 + T cell subtypes (naive, central memory, GZMK⁺ memory, CX3CR1⁺ effector memory and cytotoxic effector), with four participants per group. Groups were Control (people without HIV), HIV (PWH without methamphetamine use) and Meth+HIV (PWH who use methamphetamine). We tested three contrasts, HIV vs Control (the HIV effect), Meth+HIV vs HIV (the effect of methamphetamine use within HIV) and Meth+HIV vs Control (the net effect).

Gene set enrichment analysis

Genes were ranked by sign(log2 fold change) × -log10(p), and preranked GSEA 48 was run in gseapy 49 (1,000 permutations; gene sets of 15 to 500 genes) against the MSigDB 50 Hallmark, KEGG, Reactome, PID, BioCarta, GO and ImmuneSigDB collections. For cross-contrast summaries, we used 44 Hallmark and selected KEGG pathways per cell type, after excluding Hallmark sets specific to non-immune tissues. Each pathway was classified as reversed (FDR < 0.25 in both the HIV and methamphetamine contrasts, in opposite directions), amplified (FDR < 0.25 in both, same direction), changed with HIV only, changed with methamphetamine only, or unchanged. Antigen presentation gene sets in the GO, KEGG, Reactome, BioCarta and PID collections were identified by name (antigen processing, antigen presentation, MHC, peptide antigen or cross-presentation, excluding antigen receptor sets). For candidate monocyte to CD8 + T cell contacts, canonical ligand-receptor pairs for antigen presentation, adhesion, co-stimulation, SLAM family, cytokine, inhibitory, and chemokine signaling were retained when the ligand was detected in monocytes and the receptor in CD8 + T cells (at least 75% of pseudobulk samples).

Cytokine response, pathway activity and transcription factor activity

We estimated cytokine response (CytoSig 51 ), signaling pathway (PROGENy 52 ) and transcription factor activity (DoRothEA regulons, confidence levels A to C 53 ) from the differential expression results for each cell type and contrast. Each resource provides, for every cytokine, pathway or transcription factor, a set of target genes with weights that give the direction and strength of each gene response when that signal is active. For each signature, we fit a univariate linear model 54 in which the gene-level statistic used for GSEA (sign of the log2 fold change multiplied by -log10 p) was regressed on the signature weights across all measured target genes. The activity score is the t statistic of the slope, the slope divided by its standard error, so it reflects both how strongly and how consistently the expression changes match the signature. A positive score indicates that genes induced by the signal were higher, and genes repressed by it lower, in the first group of the contrast, and a negative score indicates the reverse. Signatures with fewer than 10 measured target genes were not scored. p values from the t distribution were adjusted by the Benjamini-Hochberg method 55 within each resource, cell type and contrast, and FDR < 0.05 was considered significant. These scores infer signaling activity from transcriptional responses and do not measure cytokine or transcription factor levels directly. Cytokine responses are shown only for cell types in which the receptor genes were detected (each receptor chain in at least 75% of pseudobulk samples).

Sensitivity analyses

To test whether results were driven by ribosomal, mitochondrial or proliferation genes, we repeated GSEA and activity scoring after removing ribosomal protein and mitochondrially encoded genes, and repeated activity scoring after adjusting for membership in the Hallmark G2M checkpoint and E2F target gene sets. We also repeated pathway classification at FDR < 0.05.

Protein interaction networks

Interaction networks were built from STRING v12.5 56 . For PBMC populations, we used leading-edge genes from the top Hallmark pathways in each cell type with differential expression p < 0.1. For proteomic data, we used proteins with an HIV-or methamphetamine-associated change. The STRING combined score threshold was set for each network (0.40 to 0.85) and is shown in each panel.

Plasma proteomics using NULISAseq

Plasma proteins were measured with the NULISAseq Inflammation Panel 250 and CNS Disease Panel 120 (Alamar Biosciences, Fremont, CA, USA) on the Alamar ARGO HT platform, following the protocol of the manufacturer and as described previously 57, 58 . Plasma samples were thawed overnight at 4 °C and centrifuged at 2,000 × g for 5 minutes, and 25 µL of plasma per sample was used for each panel. Each plate included three replicates of a sample control to assess the intra-plate coefficient of variation, three replicates of an interplate control (pooled plasma) for normalization, and four negative controls to estimate the limit of detection (LOD). Target proteins in each sample were bound by a cocktail of capture and detection antibodies linked to DNA barcodes. The immunocomplexes were captured and washed on magnetic oligo-dT beads, released into a low-salt buffer and captured again on streptavidin beads, and the proximal ends of the DNA strands on each immunocomplex were ligated to generate a reporter molecule containing both target-and sample-specific barcodes. Reporter molecules were pooled, amplified by PCR and purified. The pooled library was sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 using a P2 flow cell. FASTQ files were uploaded to Argo Command Center for normalization. Target counts in each well were divided by the internal control counts of that well and then by the target-specific median of the inter-plate control replicates on the same plate, and the normalized counts were rescaled by adding 1 and log2-transformed to give NULISA Protein Quantification (NPQ) units. Differences in NPQ can therefore be interpreted as log2 fold changes. The LOD for each target was calculated as the mean plus three standard deviations of the normalized counts in the negative control wells, and detectability was defined as the percentage of samples above the LOD. The Inflammation and CNS panels quantified 249 and 131 proteins, respectively 59 . Samples came from controls without HIV (n = 12), PWH without methamphetamine use (n = 12) and PWH who use methamphetamine (n = 5). PWH who use cocaine (n = 6) were assayed alongside these groups for comparison. Values of zero were treated as missing, and principal component analysis identified no outlier samples.

Groups were compared with Welch t tests 60 , and p values were adjusted by the Benjamini-Hochberg method within each panel and comparison. The primary comparisons were HIV vs Control, Meth+HIV vs HIV and Meth+HIV vs Control, and the same comparisons were made for the cocaine group. On the basis of nominal p < 0.05, proteins were grouped by whether an HIV-associated change was amplified, reversed, persisted or was not seen with methamphetamine use, or whether they changed with methamphetamine use only; full definitions are given in the Figure 7 legend. A methamphetamine-associated change was considered shared with cocaine when the matching cocaine comparison changed in the same direction at p < 0.05.

Selected proteins were compared across Control, HIV and Meth+HIV with pairwise Welch t tests, with p values adjusted for the three comparisons by the Holm-Šídák method 61, 62.

Statistical analysis and software

Single-cell data were processed in R with Seurat 5.4.0, and downstream analyses were run in Python 3.13 with gseapy, SciPy and networkx. All tests were two-sided.

Results

Study design and analytic approach

We studied three groups, people without HIV (Control), PWH without methamphetamine use (HIV) and PWH who use methamphetamine (Meth+HIV) (Fig. 1; Table 1). PBMCs from four participants per group were profiled by single-cell RNA sequencing. The three groups were similar in age (mean 43 to 45 years) and racial composition (two White and two Black participants per group), and all participants were assigned male sex at birth (Table 1). Participants in the HIV group had been diagnosed a mean of 13.3 years earlier and had a mean current CD4 + T cell count of 706 cells/µL, Meth+HIV participants had been diagnosed a mean of 12.4 years earlier and had a mean current CD4 + T cell count of 507 cells/µL, and Meth+HIV participants reported a mean of 6.5 years of regular methamphetamine use and use on 22 of the past 30 days. Plasma from a larger set of participants (Control, n = 12; HIV, n = 12; Meth+HIV, n = 5) was profiled by NULISAseq, together with PWH who use cocaine (Coc+HIV, n = 6) as a stimulant comparison group. The cohort did not include people without HIV who use methamphetamine, so throughout we treat HIV vs Control as the HIV effect and Meth+HIV vs HIV as the effect of methamphetamine use in the context of HIV. Comparing Meth+HIV participants with controls (the Net contrast) shows the combined state of PWH who use methamphetamine.

Methamphetamine use opposes HIV-associated changes in monocytes and CD8 + T cells but not in CD4 + and γδ T cells

After quality control filtering, 42,140 cells from 12 PBMC samples (n = 4 per group) were retained for analysis. Unsupervised clustering, UMAP visualization and canonical marker gene expression identified the major immune populations expected in human PBMCs, including CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, γδ T cells, other T cell populations, natural killer (NK) cells, monocytes and other myeloid cells, and B cells (Fig. 1A, B). Cell type proportions differed modestly across groups, but none of these differences were significant, indicating that neither HIV nor methamphetamine use substantially altered the overall composition of circulating PBMC populations in this cohort (Fig. 1C).

Across the six PBMC populations, we classified 44 Hallmark and selected KEGG pathways per cell type by whether methamphetamine use reversed, had no additional effect on, or added to HIV-associated changes (FDR < 0.25; Supplementary Fig. S1). Methamphetamine use opposed HIV-associated changes most often in monocytes (12 of 19 altered pathways reversed) and CD8 + T cells (5 of 12), whereas HIV-only changes predominated in CD4 + and γδ T cells, and methamphetamine use did not amplify an HIV-associated pathway change in any cell type. These patterns held at FDR < 0.05 and after removal of ribosomal and mitochondrial genes from the ranked gene lists (Supplementary Figs. S2, S3).

HIV-associated proliferative and TNF programs persist in CD4 + and γδ T cells but are reversed in CD8 + T cells

Across T cell populations, HIV was associated with enrichment of growth and translation programs (Fig. 2A). In CD4 + T cells, HIV enriched cell cycle (E2F targets and G2M checkpoint), spliceosome, MYC target, ribosome and TNFα signaling via NF-κB programs and depleted TCR signaling through PLCγ and IP3R (2A -Left; all FDR < 0.01). γδ T cells showed HIV-associated enrichment of MYC targets, ribosome and spliceosome (2A -Middle). CD8 + T cells shared the MYC target, ribosome and spliceosome enrichment but also showed HIV-associated depletion of IFN-γ response, IFN-α response and allograft rejection programs (2A -Right).

To compare the two effects directly, we plotted each significantly changed pathway by its NES in the HIV contrast (x axis) and the methamphetamine contrast (y axis), with the dashed diagonal marking reversal (Fig. 2B). The first panel of Fig. 2B shows interpretation of this graph. Pathways on the diagonal are fully reversed, pathways between the diagonal and the x axis are partly reversed, and pathways in the gray quadrants change in the same direction with both exposures. We used this to demonstrate changes in the three T cell populations. In CD4 + T cells, the cell cycle, spliceosome, TNFα/NF-κB and TCR signaling pathways were HIV-only changes and remained altered in Meth+HIV participants relative to controls. MYC targets and ribosome were reversed but lay between the diagonal and the x axis, indicating an effect of methamphetamine use within HIV that was smaller than the HIV effect, and both remained enriched relative to controls. In CD8 + T cells, MYC targets, ribosome and spliceosome lay close to the diagonal, reflecting reversal of similar magnitude to the HIV effect (for example, MYC targets, HIV NES = 2.17; Meth in HIV NES = -1.92; both FDR < 0.001), so that these programs no longer differed from controls in Meth+HIV participants. IFN-γ response was also reversed in CD8 + T cells, whereas IFN-α response and allograft rejection changed with HIV only. In γδ T cells, all nine altered pathways were HIV-only changes.

Running enrichment scores show these patterns for individual pathways (Fig. 2C). For TNFα/NF-κB in CD4 + T cells, the HIV trace peaked among the most up-regulated genes (NES = 1.66, FDR = 0.007), whereas the methamphetamine trace remained near zero (NES = -0.95, FDR = 0.71). For MYC targets in CD4 + T cells, the methamphetamine trace was inverted relative to the HIV trace but smaller in magnitude (NES = -1.75 and 2.45). For IFN-γ response in CD8 + T cells, the two traces ran in opposite directions (HIV NES = -1.63, FDR = 0.028; Meth in HIV NES = 1.38, FDR = 0.14).

Beyond the 16 pathways in panel A, we searched the larger Reactome, KEGG, GO Biological Process and PID collections and show, for each cell type, the five most significant gene sets that did not duplicate a panel A pathway (Fig. 2D). These were dominated by translation machinery. CD4 + T cells showed HIVassociated enrichment of mRNA processing and ribosome biogenesis gene sets, and in CD8 + T cells ribosomal small subunit biogenesis was enriched with HIV and depleted with methamphetamine use. To link these pathway changes to individual genes, we examined a predefined set of effector, IFN-γ, TNF and TCR signaling genes and display the 12 most changed across the three T cell populations (Fig. 2E). CD4 + T cells from PWH expressed less of the proximal TCR signaling genes LCK, ZAP70 and LCP2 and more of the NF-κB genes NFKBIA, REL and TNFAIP3, and CD8 + T cells expressed less of the cytotoxic genes GZMB and NKG7.

Methamphetamine use reverses HIV-associated suppression of interferon and antigen presentation programs in monocytes

In B cells, NK cells and monocytes, HIV was associated with opposite patterns of pathway enrichment between lymphocytes and monocytes (Fig. 3A). In B cells, HIV was associated with strong enrichment of ribosome (NES = 2.43, FDR < 0.001) and, more modestly, MYC target, spliceosome and TNFα signaling via NF-κB programs (Fig. 3A, left). NK cells showed a similar but broader growth signature, with HIV-associated enrichment of ribosome, MYC targets and spliceosome together with the cell cycle programs E2F targets and G2M checkpoint (Fig. 3A, middle). Monocytes showed the opposite direction of change, with HIV-associated depletion of IFN-γ response, IFN-α response, allograft rejection, antigen processing and presentation, IL-2/STAT5 signaling and ribosome programs (Fig. 3A, right; all FDR < 0.05).

On the effect plane, B and NK cells and monocytes fell on opposite sides (Fig. 3B). In B and NK cells, HIV-associated changes lay in the lower right, and ribosome sat on the diagonal in both cell types (B cells, HIV NES = 2.43 and Meth in HIV NES = -2.52; NK cells, 2.37 and -2.47; all FDR < 0.001), so that ribosome programs no longer differed from controls in Meth+HIV participants. MYC targets were also reversed in both populations, although in NK cells the effect of methamphetamine use within HIV was smaller than the HIV effect and MYC targets remained enriched relative to controls. The remaining B and NK cell changes, including spliceosome and the NK cell cycle programs, were only significant in the HIV-only comparison. Monocytes showed the mirror image, with most points in the upper left. Of 19 altered monocyte pathways, 12 were reversed, including IFN-γ response, IFN-α response, allograft rejection, antigen processing and presentation, IL-6/JAK/STAT3 and IL-2/STAT5 signaling, and most of these lay close to the diagonal. Four pathways (inflammatory response, complement, DNA repair and TNFα signaling via NF-κB) changed only with methamphetamine use, all toward enrichment. Monocyte ribosome depletion was an HIV-only change and remained lower in Meth+HIV participants than in controls (NES = -1.94, FDR = 0.008).

Running enrichment scores show the monocyte interferon reversal for individual pathways (Fig. 3C). For both IFN-γ response and IFN-α response, the HIV trace dipped among the most down-regulated genes (NES = -1.87, FDR = 0.001 and NES = -1.63, FDR = 0.021), with STAT1, CD74 and IRF8 in the leading edge of the IFN-γ set, and the methamphetamine trace peaked among the most up-regulated genes to a similar degree (NES = 1.77, FDR = 0.004 and NES = 1.72, FDR = 0.005). As a result, neither interferon program differed from controls in Meth+HIV participants. For MYC targets in B cells, the two traces were smaller but likewise ran in opposite directions (HIV NES = 1.35, FDR = 0.22; Meth in HIV NES = -1.58, FDR = 0.13).

The additional gene sets again separated lymphocytes from monocytes (Fig. 3D). In B cells, HIV was associated with enrichment of mRNA decay, alternative splicing and miRNA processing gene sets, and deadenylation-dependent mRNA decay was reversed by methamphetamine use within HIV (HIV NES = 2.01; Meth in HIV NES = -2.14). NK cells showed HIV-associated enrichment of translation initiation, mRNA decay and IL-12-induced JAK-STAT gene expression sets, whereas methamphetamine use depleted platelet aggregation and RAF signaling sets. In monocytes, HIV was associated with depletion of antimicrobial peptide and phagocyte reactive oxygen and nitrogen species production sets and with enrichment of CD28 costimulation and LXR (NR1H2 and NR1H3) signaling sets, and methamphetamine use increased regulation of antigen processing and presentation (NES = 2.06, FDR = 0.007). At the gene level, monocytes from PWH expressed less STAT1 and less of the MHC class II chaperone CD74, and monocytes from Meth+HIV participants expressed more STAT1, GBP2 and IRF1 than those from HIV participants (Fig. 3E). In NK cells, HIV was associated with higher KLRK1 and IRF1 expression, and B cells from PWH expressed more of the activation marker CD69.

Cytokine and transcription factor signatures in T cells point to opposing signals from HIV and methamphetamine use

GSEA shows which programs change but not what drives them. Because T cells showed the most varied relationships between the effects of HIV and of methamphetamine use within HIV (Fig. 2), we focused on T cells to identify the upstream signals that might underlie these programs. We scored cytokine response signatures (CytoSig 51 ), signaling pathway footprints (PROGENy 52 ) and transcription factor regulons (DoRothEA 53 ), keeping only cytokines whose receptors were expressed by each cell type. Cytokine responses showed a consistent opposition between the two exposures. Of 36 cytokine responses that changed significantly in either contrast across the three T cell populations, 34 moved in opposite directions with HIV and methamphetamine use (Fig. 4A). The largest of these was the type III IFN response, which was weaker with HIV in CD4 + and CD8 + T cells (t = -8.1 and -7.9) and stronger with methamphetamine use (t = 6.9 and 4.9), with similar reversals of the IL-4, IL-10 and M-CSF responses. The TRAIL response showed the opposite pattern, rising with HIV in all three populations and falling with methamphetamine use. In CD4 + and γδ T cells, HIV was also associated with stronger TNF, IL-6 and IL-12 responses, which remained elevated relative to controls in Meth+HIV participants, and methamphetamine use raised the IL-2 response. CD8 + T cells showed the clearest reversal of interferon and IL-2 signaling. HIV was associated with weaker IFN-γ, type I IFN and IL-2 responses (t = -4.3, -2.5 and -6.8), and methamphetamine use strengthened the IFN-γ and IL-2 responses (t = 3.2 and 7.2).

Pathway and transcription factor activities showed similar opposing patterns (Fig. 4B,C). In CD8 + T cells, HIV was associated with lower JAK-STAT (t = -6.4), EGFR and p53 pathway activity and lower STAT2 activity (t = -4.9), and methamphetamine use reversed each of these (JAK-STAT, t = 5.1; STAT2, t = 3.3). MYC activity, which was higher with HIV in CD8 + T cells (t = 3.8), fell with methamphetamine use (t = -5.1), matching the reversal of MYC targets by GSEA. In CD4 + T cells, HIV was associated with higher MYC, E2F4 and MAPK activity and lower JAK-STAT and p53 activity. Methamphetamine use partially restored JAK-STAT activity, but MYC, E2F4 and MAPK activity remained higher than in controls (Net t = 4.8, 4.6 and 3.5). T-bet (TBX21) activity was lower with HIV in CD4 + and γδ T cells (t = -3.0 and -3.9) and was lower in Meth+HIV participants than in controls in all three T cell populations (Net t = -3.1, -4.0 and -3.8).

To see how the genes behind these HIV-associated programs relate to one another, we built STRING interaction networks from the leading-edge genes of the top Hallmark pathways in each T cell population (Fig. 4D). In CD4 + T cells, a large MYC target and translation cluster, which included E2F target, G2M checkpoint and unfolded protein response genes, connected to a separate NF-κB cluster of increased genes (NFKBIA, REL, RELB, TNFAIP3 and ZFP36). In CD8 + T cells, increased ribosomal and MYC target genes linked to decreased allograft rejection and interferon response genes, including ITGB2, ITGAL, FGR, TRAF2 and G3BP1, with a separate spliceosome module. The γδ T cell network centered on STAT3, connecting the IL-6/JAK/STAT3 genes IL6ST, CD44 and PTPN11 to a MYC target and translation module. The same analyses for B cells, NK cells and monocytes are shown in Supplementary Fig. S4, and all activity scores and network genes are provided in the supplementary tables.

Central memory CD8 + T cells of Meth+HIV participants lose type 1 effector programs

Because CD8 + T cells are central to HIV-associated neuroinflammation, we next asked which CD8 + T cell subsets carried these changes, separating naive, central memory, GZMK⁺ memory, CX3CR1⁺ effector memory and cytotoxic effector cells (Fig. 5). The most notable change was in central memory cells. Compared with controls, central memory CD8 + T cells from Meth+HIV participants had weaker IFN-γ response, complement, allograft rejection and cytotoxicity programs, weaker IFN-γ and TNF response signatures, and lower activity of T-bet and EOMES, the transcription factors that drive type 1 effector function (Fig. 5A-C). Part of this loss was already present with HIV, and methamphetamine use deepened it, further lowering T-bet activity and expression of the effector genes GNLY, CX3CR1, PRDM1 and ZEB2 (Fig. 5C,D). Memory subsets showed a different change, with HIV-associated MYC target programs persisting in GZMK⁺ and CX3CR1⁺ memory cells of Meth+HIV participants (Fig. 5A,B). In every CD8 + T cell subtype, the IL-2 response fell with HIV and rose with methamphetamine use, as it did in CD8 + T cells overall (Fig. 5C).

Monocyte antigen presentation programs suppressed in HIV rise with methamphetamine use and parallel interferon signatures in CD8 + T cells

Monocytes showed the clearest reversal of any population, and the reversed programs centered on interferon response and antigen presentation (Fig. 3). To ask how far this extended, we examined antigen presentation gene sets across the GO, KEGG and Reactome collections (Fig. 6A). With HIV, monocytes showed lower enrichment of MHC class II and class I presentation, peptide loading, lysosomal processing and IFN-γ signaling gene sets, including KEGG antigen processing and presentation (NES = -2.08, FDR = 0.002), MHC protein complex (NES = -1.91, FDR = 0.033) and lysosome (NES = -1.75, FDR = 0.031). Methamphetamine use shifted each of these in the opposite direction (antigen processing and presentation, NES = 1.84, FDR = 0.014; lysosome, NES = 1.87, FDR = 0.013), and regulation of antigen processing and presentation was strongly enriched with methamphetamine use (NES = 2.06, FDR = 0.007). In Meth+HIV participants, only KEGG antigen processing and presentation remained marginally lower than in controls (Net NES = -1.57, FDR = 0.24). CD28 co-stimulation showed the opposite pattern, increasing with HIV (NES = 2.08, FDR = 0.001) and decreasing with methamphetamine use (NES = -1.90, FDR = 0.23). The shift was not limited to these selected gene sets. Of all 28 antigen presentation gene sets, 25 were lower with HIV (15 at FDR < 0.25) and 24 were higher with methamphetamine use (12 at FDR < 0.25), and 24 fell in the reversal quadrants of the effect plane (Fig. 6B). Running enrichment scores showed the HIV and methamphetamine contrasts acting on the same genes in opposite directions (Fig. 6C).

At the level of individual genes, changes were modest but consistent across the antigen presentation machinery (Fig. 6D). Among 27 genes related to MHC class II presentation, MHC class I presentation and peptide loading, and lysosomal protease activity, 26 were lower in monocytes from PWH than in controls, whereas 25 were higher in Meth+HIV than in HIV participants. Significant HIV-associated decreases (p < 0.05) were in CD74, HLA-DPB1 and HLA-DMB. Transcriptional regulators of antigen presentation followed the same pattern. IRF8 and STAT1 were lower with HIV (log2 fold change = -1.06 and -0.47) and higher with methamphetamine use (0.70 and 0.59), IRF1 was higher with methamphetamine use, and the MHC class II transactivator CIITA tended to be lower with HIV (log2 fold change = -0.72, p < 0.1).

Antigen presentation by monocytes shapes CD8 + T cell activation, and IFN-γ from activated CD8 + T cells in turn induces MHC expression in monocytes. We therefore compared these programs across cell types (Fig. 6E). Monocytes and CD8 + T cells were the only populations in which HIV was associated with lower IFN-γ response, IFN-α response and antigen processing programs, together with weaker IFN-γ and type I IFN response signatures and lower STAT2 activity. For example, the IFN-γ response signature was weaker with HIV in monocytes (t = -3.8) and CD8 + T cells (t = -4.3) and stronger with methamphetamine use (t = 5.7 and 3.2). Methamphetamine use reversed these changes fully in monocytes and partly in CD8 + T cells, where the IFN-γ response and STAT2 activity rose but antigen processing programs did not change significantly. Across the 28 antigen presentation gene sets, CD8 + T cells also showed HIV-associated decreases in 26 (11 at FDR < 0.25), whereas none was significantly lower with HIV in CD4 + T, γδ T, NK or B cells. Among candidate contact molecules (Fig. 6F), the LFA-1 chains ITGAL and ITGB2 and the SLAM family receptor SLAMF7 were lower in CD8 + T cells from PWH (p < 0.05), and their monocyte partners ICAM1 and SLAMF7 were also lower with HIV, although not significantly. Together, these data point to coordinated changes in monocyte antigen presentation and CD8 + T cell interferon signaling with HIV and methamphetamine use.

Plasma from PWH shows cytotoxic lymphocyte activation that rises further with methamphetamine use

We next asked whether these cellular changes were reflected in circulating proteins, using the NULISAseq Inflammation panel in a larger set of participants (Fig. 7). HIV was associated only with increases in this panel (22 proteins higher at p < 0.05 and none lower; Fig. 7A), and the strongest were markers of cytotoxic and type 1 lymphocyte activation. The NKG2D receptor KLRK1 was the only protein to reach FDR < 0.05 (Δ NPQ = 0.74, FDR = 0.025), and granzyme B (Δ NPQ = 1.14), IFN-γ (0.94), granzyme A, CXCL9, TNF and the receptors IL12RB1 and IL18R1 were also higher in PWH than in controls (all p < 0.05). PWH also had higher levels of the T cell activation and checkpoint proteins IL2RA (CD25), CTLA4, LAG3 and PD-1 and of the B cell survival receptor BCMA (TNFRSF17).

Methamphetamine use did not reverse any of these HIV-associated changes. Of the 22 proteins increased with HIV, granzyme B, CXCL9 and TNF rose further with methamphetamine use, 17 remained higher in Meth+HIV participants than in controls and only 2 were no longer elevated (Fig. 7B, F). IFN-γ was higher still in Meth+HIV participants, although this increase was variable and did not reach significance (Fig. 7D). Methamphetamine use also brought changes that HIV alone had not (15 proteins higher and 1 lower in Meth+HIV than HIV participants), most prominently IL-6 (Δ NPQ = 0.76, p = 0.003), FASLG, the co-stimulatory receptors 4-1BB (TNFRSF9) and GITR (TNFRSF18), the lymphoid chemokines CCL19, CCL20 and CCL21 and the NKG2D ligand MICA, whereas IL-11 was lower (Fig. 7B). Pairwise comparisons showed a stepwise rise from Control to HIV to Meth+HIV for TNF and granzyme B, whereas IL-6 and FASLG were higher in Meth+HIV participants than in either other group (Fig. 7D). Taken together, Meth+HIV participants differed from controls in 43 proteins (41 higher and 2 lower), six of which reached FDR < 0.05 (CCL1, FASLG, granzyme B, IL2RA, 4-1BB and CCL20), and they also had higher CXCL13 (Δ NPQ = 0.97, p = 0.029; Fig. 7C, D).

These proteins formed a single network in which a cytotoxic and co-stimulatory module built around IFN-γ, TNF and IL-6 connected to a lymphoid chemokine module containing CXCL9 and CXCL13 (Fig. 7E). IFN-γ was the most connected protein in this network, linked directly to 18 of the 37 other proteins, including TNF, IL-6, granzyme B, FASLG, CXCL9, CXCL10 and the checkpoint receptors CTLA4, LAG3 and PD-1. Cocaine use moved 15 of the 16 proteins changed by methamphetamine use within HIV in the same direction, but none of these shifts reached p < 0.05 in Coc+HIV compared with HIV participants, and across the panel the methamphetamine and cocaine effects were only weakly correlated (r = 0.12). Relative to controls, however, Coc+HIV participants differed in 33 proteins, and 9 proteins that changed in Meth+HIV participants relative to controls, including CCL1, CD27, BCMA and CCL11, changed in the same direction with cocaine use (Fig. 7F).

Methamphetamine use is associated with higher plasma neurofilament heavy chain

The CNS panel gave a different picture (Fig. 8). HIV alone changed only five proteins, raising TNF, the neuronal adhesion protein contactin-2 (CNTN2) and IGF1R and lowering CCL2 and IL-15 (Fig. 8A), whereas methamphetamine use within HIV changed 13 (11 higher and 2 lower; Fig. 8B). The largest change in either plasma panel was a rise in neurofilament heavy chain (NEFH), a structural protein released from injured axons (Δ NPQ = 4.48 in Meth+HIV compared with HIV participants, FDR = 0.004). Methamphetamine use also increased the axon guidance protein SLIT2 (FDR = 0.042), the amyloid-β peptides Aβ40 and Aβ42 (Δ NPQ = 0.59 and 0.37; p = 0.007 and 0.008), the kallikrein KLK6, IGFBP7, TREM1 and the cytokines TNF, IL-6, IL-2 and GM-CSF, and lowered SFRP1 and SOD1.

Amyloid-β peptides did not differ significantly between HIV and Control participants (Aβ40, Δ NPQ = 0.25, p = 0.23; Aβ42, Δ NPQ = 0.21, p = 0.25), so their increase was associated with methamphetamine use rather than with HIV alone, and Aβ38 was also higher in Meth+HIV participants than in controls (p = 0.005). Compared with controls, Meth+HIV participants differed in 24 CNS panel proteins, including higher NEFH, NEFL, SLIT2, Aβ40 and TREM1 (Fig. 8C). NEFH, Aβ40 and SLIT2 were higher in Meth+HIV participants than in either other group, whereas TNF rose step by step from Control to HIV to Meth+HIV (Fig. 8D). In the interaction network, neurofilaments, Aβ40 and pTau-217 formed a neuronal module that connected through Aβ40 to a cytokine module of TNF, IL-6, IL-2 and GM-CSF (Fig. 8E).

Cocaine use also raised NEFH (Δ NPQ = 3.25 in Coc+HIV compared with HIV participants, p = 0.021) and TREM1, whereas the increases in SLIT2, amyloid-β, IL-2 and GM-CSF were not seen with cocaine use (Fig. 8D, F). Across the CNS panel, methamphetamine and cocaine effects were moderately correlated (r = 0.31), and 6 of the 13 methamphetamine-associated proteins changed in the same direction with cocaine use.

Discussion

HIV-associated cognitive impairment reflects interacting viral, inflammatory, glial, and systemic processes 63 , and stimulant use adds to this burden. In this study, we combined single-cell transcriptomics of PBMCs with targeted plasma proteomics to define how methamphetamine use shapes immune dysregulation in PWH. Three main findings emerge. First, methamphetamine use acts in a lineage-specific manner. HIVassociated proliferative and TNF signaling programs persist in CD4 + and γδ T cells of Meth+HIV participants, whereas in monocytes and CD8 + T cells methamphetamine use shifts HIV-associated programs in the opposite direction, most clearly interferon and antigen presentation programs in monocytes and IFN-γ, IL-2 and MYC programs in CD8 + T cells. Within the CD8 + T cell compartment, central memory cells of Meth+HIV participants show reduced type 1 effector programming. Second, the monocyte shift spans the antigen presentation machinery, from MHC class II and class I presentation to peptide loading, lysosomal processing and the regulators IRF8 and STAT1, and parallels interferon signatures in CD8 + T cells. Third, plasma from PWH carries a cytotoxic lymphocyte activation signature that is further elevated with methamphetamine use, together with increases in IL-6, CXCL13 and neurofilament heavy chain.

The persistence of T cell activation programs is consistent with the chronic immune activation that characterizes treated HIV infection 12, 13 . Single-cell studies of PBMCs have shown that ART restores much of the HIV-perturbed immune transcriptome but that some changes persist 64 , and that T cell dysfunction persists in PWH with incomplete immune recovery 65 . In CD4 + and γδ T cells, HIV was associated with enrichment of MYC, E2F and G2M programs, strong TNF, IL-12 and IL-6 response signatures and NF-κB activation, together with reduced expression of proximal TCR signaling genes. Reduced LCK, ZAP70 and LCP2 alongside heightened NF-κB and cytokine-driven programs suggests a shift from antigen receptor-driven toward bystander, cytokine-driven activation. Methamphetamine use did not normalize these programs and instead increased IL-2 response signatures across lymphocyte populations, in line with reports that methamphetamine alters T cell function in PWH on ART 24 and impairs T cell subset progression and polyfunctionality in chronic viral infection 22, 23.

The CD8 + T cell compartment showed two distinct effects. In CD8 + T cells as a whole, methamphetamine use opposed HIV-associated suppression of IFN-γ, IL-2 and JAK-STAT signatures and HIV-associated enrichment of MYC and translational programs. Within the compartment, however, central memory cells of Meth+HIV participants showed reduced IFN-γ and TNF response programs, reduced T-bet and EOMES activity, and lower expression of the effector differentiation genes PRDM1, ZEB2, CX3CR1 and GNLY. T-bet and EOMES drive the effector and memory differentiation of CD8 + T cells, and central memory cells provide the pool from which effector cells are regenerated 66, 67 . A loss of type 1 effector programming in this pool is in line with reports that methamphetamine impairs CD8 + T cell subset progression and polyfunctionality in chronic viral infection 22, 23 . In parallel, HIV-associated MYC programs persisted in GZMK+ and CX3CR1+ memory CD8 + T cells of Meth+HIV participants, consistent with the durable expansion of CD8 + T cells in PWH [14] [15] [16] and with methamphetamine-associated CD8 + T cell proliferation across memory subsets 24 . Cytolytic CD8 + T cells contribute to control of HIV replication in lymphoid tissue 68 , whereas IFN-γ-producing, non-cytolytic CD8 + T cells in CSF are linked to neurocognitive impairment in PWH 21 , and ongoing T cell activation in CSF tracks cognitive decline 20 . Lower interferon response signatures in circulating CD8 + T cells from PWH contrasted with higher plasma IFN-γ, which may reflect IFN-γ production at sites other than the circulating compartment or reduced sensitivity of circulating cells to chronic interferon exposure. Because the CD8 + T cells most closely linked to neurocognitive impairment in PWH are those producing IFN-γ in CSF 21 , this dissociation raises the possibility that the IFN-γ signal relevant to the CNS is compartmentalized rather than systemic. Paired blood and CSF profiling in the same participants, combined with measures of brain circuit function, will be needed to resolve where this activity resides and how the rebalancing of CD8 + T cell programs we observed in blood relates to CD8 + T cells in the CNS.

In monocytes, HIV was associated with suppression of interferon response, antigen presentation and JAK-STAT signaling, and methamphetamine use shifted each of these programs in the opposite direction, with concordant evidence from GSEA, cytokine response signatures, pathway footprints, and transcription factor activity. Interferon-stimulated programs are prominent in the earliest systemic immune response to HIV 69 , and type I interferon signaling persists in astrocytes, microglia and endothelial cells of the brain in PWH with cognitive impairment despite ART 70 . The monocyte programs shifted by methamphetamine use within HIV included both type I and IFN-γ (type II) responses. This pattern should not be read as restoration of healthy monocyte function. Methamphetamine use also enriched IL-6/JAK/STAT3 and inflammatory response programs and raised TNF response signatures, and the monocyte network for the methamphetamine contrast linked STAT1 to the Toll-like receptor genes TLR1, TLR2 and TLR6, MYD88 and NLRP3. These features are consistent with methamphetamine-induced innate immune activation superimposed on HIV-associated changes, in keeping with increased Toll-like receptor signaling and inflammatory programs reported in SIV infection and in brain myeloid cells of SIV-infected macaques exposed to methamphetamine [26] [27] [28] . Interferon and antigen presentation programs in myeloid cells are central to HIV neuropathogenesis 31, 32, 34, 37 , so methamphetamine-driven increases in these pathways could promote myeloid activation in the CNS, particularly given methamphetamine-induced disruption of the blood-brain barrier 29, 30 . Monocytes and the macrophages and dendritic cells derived from them present antigen to CD8 + T cells through MHC class I and cross-presentation and supply the co-stimulatory and adhesion signals that shape CD8 + T cell activation and effector differentiation. The breadth of the monocyte shift, which extended across MHC class II and class I presentation, peptide loading, lysosomal processing and the regulators IRF8 and STAT1, together with parallel changes in interferon signatures and in LFA-1 and SLAMF7 expression in CD8 + T cells, suggests that changes in monocyte antigen presentation may feed into the CD8 + T cell changes we observed. In this model, HIVassociated suppression of monocyte antigen presentation would limit CD8 + T cell stimulation and interferon signaling, and methamphetamine use would restore it. Functional studies of antigen presentation and CD8 + T cell activation in co-culture and in vivo will be needed to test this.

The plasma proteome extended these findings to soluble mediators. Plasma proteins can serve as accessible indicators of CNS-relevant immune activity in PWH 71 , and a previous plasma proteomic study found that proteins involved in complement, coagulation and oxidative stress changed over time in PWH with ongoing methamphetamine use 72 . Using a targeted platform with high sensitivity for low-abundance cytokines and immune receptors 59 , we found that HIV was associated with increases in KLRK1, IFN-γ, TNF, granzyme B, CXCL9, IL12RB1 and IL18R1, a profile of type 1 and cytotoxic lymphocyte activation consistent with persistent CD8 + T cell activation in treated HIV 14, 16, 17 . CXCL9 shares the receptor CXCR3 with CXCL10, which recruits activated CD8 + T cells into tissues and the CNS [73] [74] [75] [76] . Methamphetamine use did not reverse any HIVassociated plasma changes; instead, TNF, granzyme B and CXCL9 were further increased, and IL-6, FASLG and 4-1BB were higher. Meth+HIV participants also had higher CXCL13 than controls. CXCL13 recruits CXCR5+ immune cells to sites of inflammation and can act on neural circuits [39] [40] [41] [42] , and our recent work identified CXCL13-mediated CD8 + T cell recruitment as necessary for opioid self-administration 38 . The increase in plasma CXCL13 in PWH who use methamphetamine therefore nominates a candidate pathway linking stimulant use to CD8 + T cell recruitment in PWH. In the CNS panel, methamphetamine use was associated with a marked increase in NEFH, accompanied by increases in NEFL and Aβ40, suggesting ongoing neuroaxonal injury 77, 78 . Plasma Aβ40 and Aβ42 were also higher with methamphetamine use, whereas HIV alone and cocaine use were not associated with a significant change. PWH are at increased risk of cerebral amyloid-β plaque deposition 79 , so a methamphetamine-associated rise in circulating amyloid-β may add to an amyloid-related burden in a population already at elevated risk of Alzheimer-type pathology. Most methamphetamine-associated plasma changes moved in the same direction with cocaine use, and NEFH was raised by both stimulants, whereas the increases in SLIT2, Aβ40 and IL-2 were specific to methamphetamine. This study has several limitations. The design is cross-sectional, so causal relationships cannot be inferred. The cohort did not include people without HIV who use methamphetamine, so methamphetamine effects are defined within HIV and cannot be separated from interactions with HIV. Group sizes, particularly for the methamphetamine group in the proteomic analyses, were modest, meaning many of the results will need to be replicated with larger sample sizes to make these results more generalizable. PBMC profiling captures systemic immune states and may not fully represent tissue responses, such as HIV-specific CD8 + T cells concentrated in lymph nodes 80 . All participants were assigned male sex at birth, which reflects the burden of HIV among people who use methamphetamine, and all participants with HIV were on ART with suppressed viral load. These features limit confounding by sex and by ongoing viral replication, but they also mean that the findings may not extend to women or to PWH who are not virally suppressed. Nicotine, alcohol and marijuana use were permitted in all groups and are more common among people who use stimulants, so the changes we attribute to methamphetamine use may reflect a broader pattern of polysubstance use.

In summary, methamphetamine use in PWH is associated with persistent CD4 + and γδ T cell activation, a lineage-specific shift of monocyte interferon and antigen presentation programs and of CD8 + T cell interferon, IL-2 and MYC programs, reduced type 1 effector programming in central memory CD8 + T cells, and elevated plasma markers of cytotoxic lymphocyte activation, CXCL13 and neuroaxonal injury. These data identify candidate immune pathways, including TNF, IL-6, IFN-γ and CXCL13 signaling and monocyte antigen presentation to CD8 + T cells, that are detectable in blood and that can be tested mechanistically in models of HIV and methamphetamine exposure.
