Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

p = 0.5 During training, we applied random horizontal flipping (p = 0.5) and random affine transformation (rotation ±10 •, translation ±5%, scaling 0.95-1.05).

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Background: Schistosomiasis morbidity still persists in China despite extensive progress towards elimination of new and active infections. Diagnosis of liver fibrosis related to Schistosoma japonicum remains challenging due to the subjective nature of ultrasound grading and the shortage of experienced sonographers in endemic counties.

Methods: We developed a deep learning model that grades schistosomal liver fibrosis from a single B-mode ultrasound image without manual delineation of a region of interest. We used 2,921 images from 2,921 adults (aged 18-88 years, median age 54 years; 46.4% female) from a schistosomiasis hospital in Jiangxi Province, China, between 2019 and 2023. Of these, 12.9%, 34.6%, 32.4% and 20.2% had grade 0, 1, 2 and 3 fibrosis, respectively. Fibrosis grades were based on the clinical ultrasound record and re-evaluation by an experienced radiologist according to the Chinese Handbook of Schistosomiasis Control; discordant cases were reviewed by a second radiologist and resolved by consensus. Predictions from four neural networks with different architectures (ResNet-18, EfficientNet-B0, DenseNet-121 and Swin-T) were combined by weighted soft voting, with weights derived from validation performance.

Results: For the four-grade task this model achieved a macro-F1 of 0.8527 (95% CI 0.8184-0.8856), an accuracy of 0.8500 (0.8136-0.8818) and a quadratic weighted kappa of 0.9187 (0.8983-0.9373) on a fixed test set of 440 images. For detecting significant fibrosis (grade 2 or 3; test-set prevalence 0.530), sensitivity was 0.9700, specificity was 0.9179, positive predictive value was 0.9300, negative 1.

1 Introduction

Schistosomiasis-related morbidity persists in China despite substantial progress towards transmission interruption and the marked decline in active infections. Schistosomiasis affects more than 250 million people worldwide, while Schistosoma japonicum is endemic mainly in China and the Philippines, with small residual foci in Indonesia. Compared with other major human schistosomes, Schistosoma japonicum has substantially greater fecundity, resulting in a high tissue egg burden and potentially severe hepatosplenic morbidity [1]. Chronic infection can progress to hepatic fibrosis and portal hypertension and, in severe cases, to advanced schistosomiasis with massive splenomegaly, ascites, colonic proliferative lesions, severe growth retardation or gastroesophageal variceal bleeding [2]. As China moves towards schistosomiasis elimination and active infections become increasingly rare, residual morbidity is becoming concentrated among previously infected populations [3] [4] [5]. Importantly, hepatic fibrosis may continue to progress in some individuals even after antiparasitic treatment and interruption of transmission, although the mechanisms underlying this post-treatment progression remain incompletely understood [6]. By the end of 2025, 26,688 patients with advanced schistosomiasis remained registered across China's 450 historically endemic counties, even though 421 of these counties had achieved elimination and the remaining 29 had reached transmission interruption [7]. In Changshu, Jiangsu Province, a follow-up study found that 75 previously infected individuals still developed advanced schistosomiasis, although transmission had been interrupted for more than 35 years [6].

Accurate grading and longitudinal monitoring of liver fibrosis in these individuals are therefore important needs of current control programmes [8]. Two-dimensional grey-scale (B-mode) ultrasonography is non-invasive, repeatable and widely available, and can visualise the characteristic hepatic changes of schistosomiasis japonica. A distinctive feature is interseptal fibrosis, in which fibrous septa between hepatic lobules appear as echogenic bands outlining polygonal areas and producing a characteristic fishscale or network pattern. Under the Chinese ultrasound grading scheme used in endemic areas, hepatic fibrosis is classified from grade 0 to 3. Grade 0 represents a normal liver; grade 1 is characterised by scattered focal echodense areas and mildly coarsened echoes; grade 2 by stronger echogenic bands forming a fish-scale or fine network pattern; and grade 3 by a coarse, contiguous echogenic network, often accompanied by more extensive parenchymal and portal abnormalities [9]. These grades represent a continuum of increasing echogenicity, coarsening and network formation rather than categories separated by a single discrete feature. Visual grading is therefore inherently observer-dependent, particularly at the boundaries between adjacent grades [9, 10].

The grades used in this study follow the national handbook criteria in place when the images were read [11]. In 2025 the National Institute of Parasitic Diseases convened more than 30 Chinese and international experts to publish an expert consensus on the ultrasound diagnostic pathway for this condition [9]. That consensus explicitly identifies artificial intelligence-assisted image recognition, grading and follow-up monitoring as an important direction for the field. It is aligned with the WHO(World Health Organization) framework for verifying interruption of transmission and for post-interruption surveillance [9].

Artificial intelligence applied to ultrasound imaging has begun to show promise for automated assessment of schistosomal liver fibrosis. Guo et al. extracted 851 radiomic features from manually delineated regions of interest and trained support vector machines, achieving validation AUCs of 0.771-0.834 for discrimination between adjacent grades [11]; this approach requires manual delineation before feature extraction and formulates grading as six pairwise tasks rather than a single four-grade prediction.

Ockenden et al. developed SchistoTrackNet to classify Niamey liver-image patterns of Schistosoma mansoni-associated periportal fibrosis in Uganda [12]; the disease phenotype and label space differ from those of S. japonicum, and no validated mapping exists between the Niamey patterns and the Chinese four-grade scale [13]. Most directly relevant, Xu et al. trained a single ResNet-50 on 167,702 images from 9,588 participants at 20 centres to predict a 36-level ordinal refinement of the four clinical grades [26]. That study establishes that deep learning can grade S. japonicum-associated fibrosis at scale [14].

We therefore asked whether the four national grades could be predicted from a single B-mode image, without manual region-of-interest delineation, using images from one county hospital in Jiangxi Province, China (2,921 patients, one image each, 2019-2023). Because the number of images was small by the standards of deep learning, we combined four backbones from different architectural families (ResNet-18, EfficientNet-B0, DenseNet-121 and Swin-T) by weighted soft voting rather than enlarging a single network, with weights determined exclusively from validation-set performance before evaluation on the held-out test set. We also asked where the gain of the ensemble comes from, and report an activation map for each backbone so that the image evidence behind a prediction is available to the reading clinician.

2 Methods

2.1 Study population

This was a retrospective analysis of abdominal ultrasound images acquired at the Second People's Hospital of Duchang County, Jiangxi Province, China, between 2019 and 2023. Jiangxi Province is a major schistosomiasis-endemic area of China, and residents of the Poyang Lake region are at risk of exposure to infested water. The hospital is the designated local hospital for schistosomiasis diagnosis and treatment. Schistosomiasis was diagnosed according to the national Diagnostic Criteria for Schistosomiasis (WS 261-2006) [15], and Schistosoma japonicum-associated liver fibrosis was graded with reference to the Handbook of Schistosomiasis Control [16].

The inclusion criteria were evidence of current or previous Schistosoma japonicum infection, defined as a documented history of schistosomiasis, a positive serological test for anti-Schistosoma japonicum antibodies, or a positive parasitological stool examination for S. japonicum, together with availability of right subcostal and right subcostal oblique ultrasound images. Cases with conditions that could affect ultrasound assessment, such as fatty liver, hepatitis, or drug-or alcohol-induced liver injury, were excluded, as were images with severe distortion or poor quality.

Images were acquired with a Philips EPIQ 5 ultrasound system and a C5-1 convex-array transducer at 3.5-5.0 MHz, exported in DICOM format and de-identified before modelling. The ultrasound grade recorded in the medical record was retrieved, and a radiologist with more than 5 years of experience in schistosomiasis ultrasonography re-evaluated each image. A second radiologist reviewed cases with discordant grades, and disputed cases were discussed to decide on inclusion and final grade. The final consensus labels were used for model training and defined a four-class task: schistosomal liver fibrosis grades 0, 1, 2 and 3 (G0-G3). The grades are distinguished by the coarseness and spatial organisation of the parenchymal echo. Grade 0 shows a uniform liver parenchymal echo with fine or slightly thicker bright spots. In grade 1 the parenchymal echo is uneven and the bright spots become coarser. In grade 2 the echo is unevenly distributed with enhanced bright spots that form a fish-scale pattern, and a fine mesh-like echo is seen throughout the liver [11]. In grade 3 the echo is uneven with thicker, more intense bright spots and higher-pitched echoes, and the echoes form a densely packed, coarse network-like highecho band [11].

2.2 Ethics

This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethical Review Committee of the National Institute of Parasitic Diseases, Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (approval no. 2021019). All participants were informed of the study's aims, procedures, potential benefits and risks before enrolment, and each provided written informed consent; no minors were enrolled. The present analysis was performed retrospectively on de-identified ultrasound images, with no additional intervention or contact with participants.

2.3 Data partitioning and pre-processing

The 2,921 included images, with one image per patient, were split into training, validation and test sets at a ratio of approximately 70:15:15, stratified by fibrosis grade using a random seed of 42. Once defined, the training, validation and test sets were kept fixed throughout model development, with the test set held out and used only for the final evaluation. The proportion of each fibrosis grade differed by no more than 0.47 percentage points across the three subsets (Fig. 1a).

Images were stored in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format as singlechannel grey-scale arrays of 360 × 385 pixels. Each image was linearly stretched to 0-255 using its own minimum and maximum grey levels and resized to 224 × 224 pixels by bilinear interpolation. It was then converted to a tensor and normalised with a mean of 0.5 and a standard deviation of 0.5. Per-image stretching was intended to make the model rely on the relative grey-level distribution rather than on absolute echogenicity and thus reduce the effect of different gain settings. During training, we applied random horizontal flipping (p = 0.5) and random affine transformation (rotation ±10 • , translation ±5%, scaling 0.95-1.05). No augmentation was applied during validation or testing.

2.4 Models and ensemble

Models were trained and evaluated on a single workstation with a 20-core Arm CPU and one NVIDIA GB10 accelerator sharing 120 GB of unified memory (Linux kernel 6.17, NVIDIA driver 580.142). The implementation used Python 3.12.3 with PyTorch 2.11.0 and torchvision 0.26.0 built against CUDA 13.0; metrics were computed with scikit-learn and activation maps with the grad-cam package. All package versions are recorded in Supplemental materials.

We chose four backbones from different architectural families: ResNet-18 (residual convolutional network), EfficientNet-B0 (compound-scaled convolutional network with channel activation), DenseNet-121 (densely connected convolutional network) and Swin-T (hierarchical vision Transformer) [17] [18] [19] [20].

The main challenge in four-grade assessment is the discrimination of adjacent grades, which represent a continuum of ultrasonographic changes and have shown lower discriminability than more widely separated grades in previous work [11]. Different architectures encode distinct inductive biases: convolutional networks impose stronger locality and spatial-equivariance priors, whereas self-activation allows information to be integrated across more spatially distributed image regions [21]. Swin-T further combines hierarchical representations with shifted-window activation to enable information exchange across neighbouring windows [20]. These architectural differences may result in partially non-overlapping prediction errors. Probability-level averaging can therefore exploit complementary classifier outputs and reduce errors specific to individual models [22].

All four backbones were trained on identical data with an identical training procedure -loss function, optimiser, learning-rate schedule, augmentation and stopping rule -and differed only in architecture.

Each was initialised from ImageNet-1K pre-trained weights; the first convolutional (or patch-embedding) layer was adapted to single-channel input by averaging its pre-trained RGB kernels, and the whole network was then fine-tuned [23]. The loss was class-weighted cross-entropy with label smoothing [24] (coefficient 0.05), the weight of each class being proportional to the inverse of its training-sample count and normalised to sum to the number of classes. We used the AdamW optimiser (Adam with decoupled weight decay) [25] with a learning rate of 1 × 10 -4 and a weight decay of 1 × 10 -4 , linear warm-up over the first 3 epochs and cosine annealing thereafter. The batch size was 32, gradients were clipped to a global norm of 1.0, and training ran for up to 30 epochs. Training stopped early if the validation macro-F1 did not improve for 8 consecutive epochs, and the weights with the highest validation macro-F1 were retained as the final model. The random seed was fixed at 42, with cuDNN deterministic mode enabled and benchmarking disabled to improve run-to-run consistency.

Ensembling was performed at the probability level. Each backbone produced predicted class probabilities for the four grades; these were combined linearly with weights fixed before any test-set evaluation, and the grade with the highest combined probability was the final prediction: P (g) = 󰁓 a w a •P a (g). The weights were the normalised best validation macro-F1 of each backbone, w a = F1 a / 󰁓 b F1 b : 0.2453 for ResNet-18, 0.2525 for EfficientNet-B0, 0.2480 for DenseNet-121 and 0.2542 for Swin-T.

No test data were used to estimate the weights. Unlike majority voting, probability weighting preserves each model's uncertainty across grades, which suits a four-grade task in which adjacent grades are easily confused.

2.5 Model performance

The primary metric was the macro-averaged F1 score (macro-F1). Secondary metrics were accuracy, quadratically weighted kappa (QWK), mean absolute error (MAE) and, for the binary task of significant fibrosis (grade ≥2), the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), sensitivity and specificity. On the fixed test set (n = 440), 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated by non-parametric bootstrap with 1,000 resamples (random seed 42). Models were compared by paired bootstrap, in which the same resampling indices were applied to the predictions of each model and to the reference labels. Stability was assessed by five-fold cross-validation.

2.6 Activation map

Grad-CAM (gradient-weighted class activation mapping) was computed for all four backbones on their final spatial feature layer. For ResNet-18, EfficientNet-B0 and DenseNet-121, Grad-CAM was applied to the final convolutional feature maps. For Swin-T, the output of the second layer normalisation in the final transformer block was reshaped from 49 spatial tokens to its native 7 × 7 grid before Grad-CAM computation. For all backbones, the target class was the grade predicted by the ensemble so that the resulting maps referred to the same class for a given image. Maps were upsampled to 224 × 224 pixels, smoothed with a Gaussian kernel (σ = 1.5), and normalised to [0, 1] within each image. We then calculated the equal-weighted mean activation, the population standard deviation across the four maps, and the number of backbones with normalised activation above 0.5 at each pixel. The diversity index was defined as the image-wide mean of the standard-deviation map, and the agreement ratio as the proportion of pixels at which all four backbones exceeded 0.5.

3 Results

3.1 Study population

Of 3,272 patients examined between 2019 and 2023, 317 were excluded before image selection (other liver disease, n = 253; required views not acquired, n = 64) and 34 at reference grading, leaving 2,921 patients with one image each (Fig. 1a). The mean age was 51.7 years (SD 14.8), and 53.6% were male and 46.4% were female. Grades G0-G3 accounted for 376 (12.9%), 1,010 (34.6%), 945 (32.4%) and 590 (20.2%) patients, and the distribution differed by no more than 0.5 percentage points across the training, validation and test sets.

3.2 Grading performance of the individual backbones and the ensemble

Table 1 summarises the performance of the four backbones and the weighted soft-voting (WSV) ensemble on the fixed test set (n = 440; metrics are defined in Methods). The four backbones performed similarly, with test macro-F1 from 0.798 to 0.807 and overlapping 95% confidence intervals. ResNet-18 had the highest macro-F1 (0.807; 95% CI, 0.769-0.846), Swin-T the highest QWK (0.890; 95% CI, 0.863-0.912) and Swin-T had the highest QWK (0.890) and grade ≥ 2 fibrosis AUROC (0.975) among the individual models.

The WSV ensemble reached a macro-F1 of 0.853 (95% CI, 0.818-0.886), accuracy of 0.850 (0.814-0.882), QWK of 0.919 (0.898-0.937), MAE of 0.150 (0.118-0.186) and grade ≥ 2 fibrosis AUROC of 0.982, the best value on every metric. In the paired bootstrap, in which the same resampled images were scored for both models, the ensemble exceeded ResNet-18 in macro-F1 by 0.046 (95% CI, 0.019-0.073), EfficientNet-B0 by 0.047 (0.020-0.074), DenseNet-121 by 0.052 (0.021-0.084) and Swin-T by 0.055 (0.024-0.088); none of the intervals included zero. The ensemble also made smaller errors. Every one of its 66 misgraded images was assigned a grade adjacent to the reference grade, whereas the single backbones misgraded 1 to 4 images by two grades or more (Table 1).

The confusion matrix of the ensemble (Fig. 1b) shows how the errors were distributed. The proportion of images assigned their reference grade was 89.3% for G0 (50 of 56), 82.1% for G1 (124 of 151), 83.9% for G2 (120 of 143) and 88.9% for G3 (80 of 90). All six G0 errors were graded G1 and all ten G3 errors were graded G2, as the extreme grades can only be missed in one direction. For the two intermediate grades, errors went in both directions but more often upwards: of the 27 G1 errors, 17 were graded G2 and 10 were graded G0, and of the 23 G2 errors, 16 were graded G3 and 7 were graded G1.

G1 was the hardest grade for the ensemble and for three of the four backbones, with per-grade sensitivity from 0.689 (Swin-T) to 0.828 (EfficientNet-B0).

Among the single models, EfficientNet-B0 had the highest F1 for G0 (0.842) and G1 (0.836), ResNet-18 for G2 (0.788) and Swin-T for G3 (0.836). The ensemble had the highest F1 in all four grades (0.862, 0.861, 0.828 and 0.860). Its gain over the individual backbones was largest for G2, where the paired difference was significant against three of the four backbones after Benjamini-Hochberg correction across the 16 grade-by-backbone comparisons; in total, 8 of the 16 comparisons were significant. Table 2 gives the positive and negative predictive values (PPV, NPV), sensitivity and specificity of each model for each grade, computed one grade against the other three pooled. NPV exceeded 0.90 in 15 of the 20 model-by-grade combinations; the five exceptions were at G1 and G2. PPV varied more than NPV, spanning 0.699 to 0.919 against 0.855 to 0.986. Across the table, a high PPV went with a low sensitivity rather than a high one (r = -0.51, P = 0.02), the expected trade-off between assigning a grade liberally and conservatively. G1 shows this most clearly, with the highest mean PPV of any grade (0.889) and the lowest mean sensitivity (0.768). The ensemble had the best PPV for G0, G2 and G3 and the best NPV for G2 and G3. For G3 it combined a sensitivity of 0.889 with a PPV of 0.833, so advanced cases were largely detected and, when detected, usually correct. These predictive values depend on the grade mix of the test set (13% G0, 34% G1, 33% G2, 20% G3) and would differ in a population with another prevalence.

We also read the four-grade output as two clinical yes/no questions. For any fibrosis (G1-G3 versus G0; 384 versus 56 images), the ensemble detected 97.4% of fibrotic images with a specificity of 89.3% and an AUROC of 0.983; the single models ranged from 94.3% to 97.4% in sensitivity and from 82.1% to 91.1% in specificity. For significant fibrosis (G2-G3 versus G0-G1; 233 versus 207 images) the ensemble achieved an AUROC of 0.982 (Fig. 1c), above every single model (0.965-0.975). At the operating point given by the predicted grade, the ensemble's sensitivity was 97.0%, specificity 91.8%, PPV 93.0% and NPV 96.4%. Three backbones matched its sensitivity of 97.0% but with lower specificity (86.5-88.4%); EfficientNet-B0 was more specific (92.3%) but less sensitive (93.6%).

To test whether these results depended on the particular training run, we repeated the entire pipeline with 15 training seeds on the same fixed split, changing only the random seed. Ensemble macro-F1 was 0.848 ± 0.011 across seeds (range, 0.820-0.862). The single backbones varied more, with seed-to-seed SDs from 0.014 to 0.020. The ensemble exceeded the best single backbone in all 15 seeds, by 0.025 on average.

We also re-partitioned the whole pool of 2,921 images into five patient-level folds and retrained the four backbones and the ensemble in each fold (Methods). Ensemble macro-F1 on the held-out folds was 0.832 ± 0.014, against 0.811 ± 0.011 for the best single backbone of each fold. Because the folds re-partition the same pool of patients as the fixed split, this measures the stability of the pipeline rather than providing an independent validation.

3.3 Activation patterns of the backbones

Grad-CAM maps show where the evidence for a backbone's decision was concentrated. We refer to them as activation maps. We first asked whether the four backbones highlight the same part of an image. For each image we took the hottest 10% of pixels in each map and measured the overlap between every pair of backbones. Two regions of that size placed independently would overlap by 0.10; identical regions would give 1. The observed medians were 0.25 to 0.51 across the six pairs. We also measured the distance between the two maps' single hottest pixels. This was a median of 41 to 68 pixels, or 18 to 30% of the frame width, against 114 pixels for two independently placed points. The maps therefore overlap far more than chance without coinciding (Fig. 2).

The differences between maps are not an artefact of how the maps were produced. The target layer of every backbone outputs a 7 × 7 grid for a 224 × 224 input (Methods), and all four maps were upsampled to the input size by the same operator and normalised the same way, so no backbone's map is coarser or smoother than another's by construction. Nor is the ordering explained by map extent. Swin-T does mark a smaller area, with the region above half of a map's own maximum covering 9% of the frame against 29% for ResNet-18.

The disagreement followed architecture. Ranking the six pairs by overlap, the three pairs of convolutional backbones came above all three pairs containing the transformer. The most similar pair was

ResNet-18 with EfficientNet-B0, and the least similar was ResNet-18 with Swin-T. We averaged the three pairs of each kind within each image and tested the paired difference. The convolutional pairs were the more similar on all six measures we computed, three in the main text and three in the Supplemental materials, all with P < 1 × 10 -12 (Wilcoxon signed-rank, n = 420). For Swin-T, we also evaluated attention rollout and gradient-weighted rollout (Supplemental materials).

These maps show that the four backbones focus on partly different image regions. They do not prove that the models are correct, and without lesion-level annotations we do not assign the highlighted areas to specific anatomical structures.

3.4 Source of the ensemble gain

The ensemble gain did not come from correcting errors shared by all four backbones. All 35 cases (8.0%) misclassified by every backbone were also misclassified by the ensemble, with a mean posterior probability of only 0.105 for the reference grade. Conversely, no case was correct for all four backbones but wrong for the ensemble. The gain arose only when the members disagreed: in 24 cases (5.5%), at least two backbones were wrong but the ensemble was correct, including 2 cases in which three backbones were wrong (Fig. 3). In case G1_925, the errors were spread over two grades, and the reference grade prevailed after averaging with a posterior probability of 0.394. On the whole test set, the macro-F1 of the WSV ensemble (0.853) was higher than that of majority (hard) voting (0.832). Of the 20 cases on which the two methods disagreed, soft voting was correct in 14 and wrong in 6. Grading schistosomal liver fibrosis on B-mode ultrasound is the examination on which surveillance and follow-up of previously infected people in endemic areas of China depend, and it is read by eye, with disagreement concentrated at the boundaries between adjacent grades [9, 10]. Using 2,921 images, we trained four backbones of different architectural families and combined them by weighted soft voting into a single four-grade reader that takes the whole image as input, without manual delineation of a region of interest. On the fixed test set the ensemble reached a macro-F1 of 0.853 (95% CI, 0.818-0.886), and its advantage over each backbone (0.046 to 0.055 in macro-F1) had a paired 95% confidence interval that excluded zero. The advantage held in all 15 training seeds and five cross-validation folds, so it does not depend on one fortunate training run. The ensemble also reduced the size of the errors. In the reported run, every one of its errors was between adjacent grades, whereas each backbone misgraded 1 to 4 images by two grades or more.

Is the ensemble worth its cost, and how it could be deployed?

The ensemble improved macro-F1 significantly over the best single backbone, a gain that readers can weigh against the cost of running four networks. Three considerations favour the ensemble in this application. First, the gain is consistent: it appeared in every seed and in 8 of the 16 grade-by-backbone comparisons after correction for multiple testing, most reliably at G2. Second, the ensemble reduced the errors that matter most clinically, those spanning two or more grades, in the reported run and in most seeds. Third, and contrary to what the word ensemble suggests, the ensemble does not require a larger machine than a single network. The reason is that the four backbones are independent and are combined only through their output probabilities, so they can be evaluated one after another, each loaded, run and released before the next. Peak memory is then set by the largest member rather than by their sum. We measured this on the deployed configuration, at the input size the pipeline uses. Evaluated sequentially, the ensemble's peak accelerator memory was 131.4 MB, identical to that of Swin-T alone, its largest member, and below the 219.1 MB measured when all four were held in memory at once. What the ensemble costs is time, not hardware: 163.3 ms per image on four CPU threads, against 80.4 ms for the slowest single backbone and 10.8 ms for the fastest, and 10.9 ms per image with all four resident on an NVIDIA GB10 accelerator. The four deployed backbones total 49.7 million parameters, 1.8 times the 27.5 million of Swin-T alone and about twice the 23.5 million of a single ResNet-50 in the same configuration. Because images are graded one at a time rather than in a real-time stream, a few hundred milliseconds is not a clinical constraint, and no additional class of hardware is needed.

Two deployment routes therefore follow from the same set of trained weights, and the choice between them is governed by data protection rather than by computing capacity. In the first, a single backbone runs locally on the ultrasound workstation, so that no image leaves the department and no network connection is required. On these data ResNet-18 had the highest macro-F1 among the single models, while EfficientNet-B0 offers the best balance of specificity and size, at 4.0 million parameters and 10.8 ms per image. The cost is the ensemble gain reported above, and the loss of the reduction in errors spanning two or more grades. In the second, the full ensemble runs on an institutional server, which keeps one auditable copy of the model, allows it to be updated and monitored centrally, and removes any dependence on the specification of the individual workstation. This route requires images to be transmitted, so it is the more demanding of the two in terms of governance: it presupposes de-identification at source, an encrypted channel, and the data-protection approvals of the institutions involved.

The need for ensemble-based approaches

Combining classifiers at the probability level reduces the errors that are specific to individual members while leaving shared errors untouched [22], and this is what we observed. All 35 test images misgraded by every backbone were also misgraded by the ensemble; on these images the four posteriors agreed on a wrong grade and the reference grade received a mean probability of only 0.105, so averaging could not recover it. The gain came entirely from the images on which the backbones disagreed: in 24 of them, two or three backbones were wrong and the ensemble was right, either because a confident minority outweighed a less confident majority or because the errors fell on different grades and the reference grade had the highest probability after averaging. For this mechanism to work, the members must err on different images, and members of different architectural families are the natural candidates, because convolutional networks and vision transformers are known to build different internal representations of the same image [21]. The activation maps were consistent with the observed prediction diversity. Convolutional backbones showed more similar Grad-CAM patterns to one another than to Swin-T across all six similarity measures, indicating partly different spatial attribution patterns across architectures. These differences may be related to the complementary prediction errors observed in the ensemble, but Grad-CAM alone cannot establish a causal link between attribution differences and ensemble performance.

Relation to previous work

Two lines of work are relevant. Machine-learning readers for Schistosoma mansoni periportal fibrosis [12, 13] address a different pattern, the echogenic thickening along the portal tracts of the Niamey protocol, whereas S. japonicum produces a parenchymal network pattern graded by its coarseness and organisation [8, 9]. For S. japonicum itself, Xu and colleagues recently reported a single ResNet-50, trained on 167,702 images from 9,588 participants at 20 centres, that predicts a 36-level ordinal refinement of the four clinical grades [14]. Theirs rests on the scale and the granularity of the labelled data, whereas ours asks how much can be obtained from 2,921 images acquired at a single centre. That question led us to combine backbones of different architectural families rather than to enlarge a single one. It also led us to report an activation map for every backbone, so that the image evidence on which a prediction rests is available to the reading clinician alongside the grade itself. Their trained model is not released, and we did not attempt a direct comparison of the two. Additionally, the study by Xu and colleagues required 18 ultrasound images per participant, whereas the present study requires only a single, relatively easy-to-acquire cross-sectional image of the liver.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single-centre retrospective study with a relatively homogeneous sample, and the external generalisability of the model has not been validated. Second, only the clearest ultrasound image of each patient was included; information from multiple planes and time points was not used, and whether it could further improve grading remains to be evaluated. Third, the model used grey-scale images alone, without clinical variables such as serology and medical history or multimodal information such as elastography. Fourth, the study population was relatively old, with a mean age of 51.7 years, which may limit the generalisability of the model to younger populations. This age distribution likely reflects the changing epidemiology of schistosomiasis in endemic areas, where new infections have declined substantially and most newly infected individuals recover after treatment, reducing the repeated infections that historically contributed to progression to advanced disease.

Future work will address these limitations along three lines. A multicentre prospective validation study is ongoing to assess the stability and generalisability of the model across devices, populations and clinical settings. Where necessary, federated learning may support joint modelling without sharing raw images. We will also use multi-frame or multi-plane images as input and integrate clinical variables and elastography to enrich the information available to the model. Finally, we will extend the model to longitudinal monitoring to track the progression or regression of fibrosis in individual patients.

Conclusion

A weighted soft-voting ensemble of four backbones graded Schistosoma japonicum-associated liver fibrosis from a single B-mode ultrasound image on the four-grade scale, achieving a macro-F1 of 0.853 on the original test partition. Its performance was higher than that of each individual backbone, and in the reported run all misclassifications were confined to adjacent grades. The observed improvement was associated with complementary errors across the four architecturally different backbones. For detecting grade ≥2 fibrosis, the ensemble achieved an AUROC of 0.982. These findings support further evaluation of automated fibrosis grading in endemic settings, particularly through external and prospective validation.
