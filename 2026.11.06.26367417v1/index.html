How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Full text

Abstract

Bangladesh is a country with low HIV prevalence, but case detection has more than doubled in 2020-24, reaching 1,891 cases in the most recently reported year, and the number of cases is growing in the 18-24 age group. There is little qualitative evidence that describes the journey from suspected exposure to diagnosis for young people in Bangladesh, and no comparative evidence of this journey between keypopulation and general-population youth.

To explore the pathway to HIV testing and diagnosis, what motivated testing, and the life outlook of people living with HIV in Bangladesh aged 18-30 years, and to compare these between key-population and general-population participants.

This qualitative exploratory study uses semi-structured in-depth interviews, guided by the theory of emerging adulthood and the HIV Stigma Framework. Purposive maximum-variation sampling (20-30 participants) will recruit people aged 18-30, diagnosed within that age range and currently on antiretroviral therapy, balanced between key and general populations. Participants will be recruited through Shaheed Suhrawardy Medical College Hospital and Ashar Alo Society, Dhaka, and interviewed privately in Bangla. Recordings will be transcribed, translated into English, and analysed with the Framework Method in MAXQDA, with saturation judged within each group.

Introduction

Background and Rationale

HIV continues to disproportionately affect adolescents and young adults globally and is underrepresented in the population tested, as their needs are not being addressed [1, 2]. It is estimated that 370,000 young people aged 15-24 years became infected with HIV in 2024, while testing coverage for adolescents in areas where data was available has not reached 30%, which means that many young people are unaware of their HIV status [2]. Low risk perception, stigma and the absence of youthfriendly services are more important motivators for testing amongst young people than access [1]. In well-resourced systems, late diagnosis continues, with 49.6% of new diagnoses being also late in 23 EU/EEA countries in 2022-2024 and being a migrant was a risk factor [3]. Prevention coverage is also incomplete, as uptake of PrEP (Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis) among youth is hindered by stigma, low risk perception, health literacy and cost [4], and the psychological burden of being diagnosed with HIV is high, with pooled estimates of depression, anxiety and PTSD (Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder) prevalence of approximately 28%, 22% and 12% respectively among adolescents and young adults with HIV [5], and youth knowledge of and awareness of U=U is limited. (Undetectable = Untransmittable) [6]. Psychosocial issues are more important in this path than merely clinical ones as shown in high income and Asian contexts. Young PLHIV (People Living with HIV) in New York City who experienced depression, trauma, substance use and socioeconomic strain were at risk for retention, and social support and patient-friendly services were associated with retention [7]. Stigma is linked to disclosure and the nature of relationships with family, partners, friends and communities [8]; moral framing of HIV hinders post-exposure testing and distorts care even when available, and moral blame and confidentiality breaches precipitate concealment of status in Istanbul [9]. Fortunately, psychosocial intervention has a measurable impact on mental health and wellbeing of PLHIV and emotional responses to diagnosis are not irrevocable [10]. The same architecture is repeated in Asia, intensified by the issues of confidentiality and attitudes of clinicians: shock, fear and distress immediately following the diagnosis is a determinant of whether young people access services at all [11]; provider trust and social support were facilitators for accessing HIV services in Iran, and attitudes of providers and family support were barriers [12]. Stigma towards PLHIV exists at the individual, family, community, workplace and healthcare levels, and family support and counselling helps adherence to ART (Antiretroviral Therapy) in Pakistan, while service disruption and financial difficulty hinder this; in Bangladesh's immediate neighbourhood, PLHIV in rural Nepal experience delayed testing, low awareness, financial burden, confidentiality concerns and fear of judgement along the care pathway; [13] and PLHIV in India face intersectional stigma with gender inequality and domestic violence.

Historically considered a low prevalence country with a prevalence of 0.1% and below [14], this description is increasingly under pressure as HIV case detection has more than doubled since 2020, with 1,891 new cases reported in the latest year reported, a large increase in case numbers among 18-24-year-olds, including at university and among new entrants to the workforce, and young urban migrants [15, 16]. While the epidemic is still largely focused within key populations, cases among youth not in these groups challenge assumptions about where risk lies, and there is extensive labour migration, tying individual cases to migration outside the country and to non-injecting partners of PWID (People Who Inject Drugs) in Dhaka [16, 17]. It happens in a specific social and legal environment: in a Muslim dominated society marriage is sometimes a social arrangement and/or is expected and hijra identity is formally recognised, though homosexual behaviour is criminalised, resulting in some young people being visible and vulnerable. After diagnosis, PLHIV in Bangladesh continue to experience stigma from society, side effects of medication, financial problems, long distances to ART centres and lack of support from family members [18], internalised stigma around living with HIV [19], and limited uptake of testing, with many people being diagnosed late, often incidentally [16]. There is strong evidence available on prevalence, surveillance, key-population programming, ART uptake and discrete service barriers, but relatively less evidence on the experiences of young people in Bangladesh themselves around experiencing a transition from suspected exposure to a life remade around HIV. Some of what this silence might be hiding is suggested by international evidence: qualitative research in sub-Saharan Africa indicates that there is substantial initial psychological trauma and deep internalised stigma after diagnosis; that specialised counselling helps adolescents transition from suicidal thinking to selfacceptance [20]; that consent rules and poor confidentiality contribute to low uptake of services and disengagement from health clinics, particularly youth, who are targeted by informal community workers [21, 22]; and Kenyan evidence demonstrating that difficulty with disclosure is directly linked to disengagement from services [23]. Evidence in Peru indicates that adolescents experience emotional burden, misinformation about ART, side effects and suicidal thoughts as a result of the perceived endlessness of treatment [24]. Very little of this evidence is from Bangladesh, few studies focus on the age band of 18-30 years, and even fewer consider the process from exposure to diagnosis as one continuous time series. Importantly, no qualitative study has been conducted to date in Bangladesh comparing the pathway and the resulting life perspective of key-population younger youth to generalpopulation younger youth, as done in this study. A qualitative study is thus needed and appropriate for this gap; the question of "why" is raised, the process of referral is described, the emotional and social uptake of diagnosis is explored and the future imaginings of the young are discussed. This study has the potential to uncover triggers to test, missed prevention opportunities, real-world referral pathways through HIV services, and post-diagnostic experiences of hope, fear, acceptance and planning for the future; it can also determine if participants believe HIV is incurable, curable, or manageable like other chronic illnesses such as diabetes, or whether greater awareness of HIV prior to exposure could have influenced behavior. Peer-led programmes elsewhere, such as Community Adolescent Treatment Supporters in Zimbabwe, have helped young people to rebuild their hope and to stay in care [25], and meaningful youth involvement in the design of services in Kenya has led to optimisation of testing, linkage and retention [26], so for young PLHIV in Bangladesh, the question is whether such supports are sufficient. By placing key-population and general-population experiences in parallel and integrating the journey as a whole, findings can guide youth-friendly testing and counselling, more widespread PrEP awareness, and improve linkage and retention to ART, as well as psychosocial support and stigma reduction, and provide locally relevant data to inform national policy and programme planning [27].

Overarching qualitative research question

• How do young people living with HIV, aged 18-30 years in Bangladesh, experience the journey from suspected exposure to HIV diagnosis, what motivated them to test, and what is their current outlook on life?

Specific research questions

• What events, risk perceptions, or personal experiences led participants to HIV testing and diagnosis?

• How were participants referred to HIV testing, counselling, ART, and related services through health facilities, NGOs, or community networks?

• How do participants describe their current outlook on life after diagnosis, including hope, fear, acceptance, uncertainty, and future planning?

• How do participants perceive HIV, such as curable, fatal, or manageable like other chronic conditions such as diabetes mellitus?

• What awareness and perceptions of pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) did participants hold prior to diagnosis?

• How, if at all, do the exposure-to-diagnosis journey and current outlook on life differ between key-population and non-key/general-population participants?

Materials and methods

Study Design

This is a qualitative exploratory study which is based on semi-structured in-depth interviews (IDIs). This is suitable as the study seeks to explore young people's personal experiences, feelings and meanings around HIV testing, diagnosis, linkage to ART/support and life after diagnosis. The experiences are complex in nature and structured survey questions cannot adequately capture them [28].

The care continuum between suspected exposure, diagnosis, and linkage to ART and/or support will be considered a life journey in the study. The words and feelings of the participants at each phase of this journey will be described, and may be obtained from semi structured interviews. The study will be based on the emerging adulthood theory and the HIV Stigma Framework. Emerging adulthood theory can be applied to the way that young people (18-30 years) consider issues of identity, risk, relationships, disclosure and future plans [29]. The HIV Stigma Framework can help contextualize the experience of stigma, including fear of disclosure or social rejection, and internal shame/emotional distress following HIV diagnosis [30]. For the study design and reporting, the standards from the COREQ (Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research) [31] will be followed. Study Setting

The study will be carried out in 2 locations: • Shaheed Suhrawardy Medical College & Hospital (ShSMCH), Dhaka.

• Ashar Alo Society, Dhaka.

Study Population

ART users aged between 18-30 years old (YOH) from key and priority populations (sex workers, MSM, transgender and hijra people, PIDs, migrants and partners of key populations) and general population are study population. The age group 18-30 was selected intentionally, and is different from the 15-24 age group that is used for reporting HIV internationally, for four reasons. For one thing, the 15-24 is not a Bangladeshi policy or legal category. United Nations and UNAIDS have defined the youth age range as 15-24 years for reporting purpose, while the age range of youth has been defined as 18-35 years in Bangladesh's National Youth Policy 2017, and 15-29 years in the Commonwealth of Australia's definition. [32] . The selected band is thus entirely included in the national definition of youth and it starts from the point where the national definition of youth starts. Second, in terms of age, 18 years is the age of maturity in Bangladesh. The Majority Act, 1875 specifies that a person who is domiciled in Bangladesh turns major at 18 years and not earlier. [33] For minors, Bangladesh Medical Research Council guidelines call for consent to be obtained from parent/legal guardian and assent to be obtained based on the child's capacity [34]. Setting the lower age limit at 18 will allow all participants in an HIV study to consent independently and confidentially, whereas guardian consent would force disclosure of HIV status to family members, which is itself a known harm and a significant disincentive to participation. Third, there is a maximum limit of 30, based on developmental theory. The transition between adolescence and full adulthood (18-29 years) is a unique developmental phase, according to Arnett's theory of emerging adulthood, where there is identity exploration, instability in place, selffocus, a sense of "in-betweenness", and a feeling of possibilities. In this phase, majority of people delay marriage and parenthood till about 30 and use their twenties to try out relationship and career. These are the very processes that contribute to HIV exposure and the meaning a young person attributes to a diagnosis: sexual debut, partner formation, migration for study or work, and entering into marriage. A ceiling of 30 (instead of 24) thus includes university students, migrant and early career workers, and newly married people who would otherwise be among the "missing" or cut off by the 15-24 age group. Fourth, the age range of 18-30 has been used previously in HIV studies with emerging adults. In Zimbabwe, a qualitative study involving emerging adults (18-29 years) living with HIV interviewed them using a semi-structured interview, and found that they have lower adherence to ART and higher viral non-suppression rates compared to older adults. [35] A U.S. mixed methods study defined emerging adulthood as 19-28 years of age in the population of people living with HIV [36]; a qualitative study in Kenya used this emerging adult framing for 18-24-year-olds [37]. Participants will have to be diagnosed with HIV in the age group 18-30 and the age group will be within 18-30. This double criterion has the effect of establishing that the diagnosis studied is that of emerging adulthood, and not that of childhood or perinatal acquisition. This distinction is important: in the Zimbabwean sample of 18-29year-olds described above, 79% were diagnosed at birth, leaving the behaviourally acquired, adult-onset diagnosis journey, which is the focus of this study. [35].

Sample Size Determination

The number of study participants will be 20 -30 and will be recruited using purposive sampling method with the number of participants determined by data saturation, the point at which no new themes emerge from the interviews [38] and with regard to balanced representation across population groups. This target is based on the information power; that is, the richer the information of the sample, the fewer number of participants [39]. The sample will be purposefully selected from a range of key and vulnerable groups including sex workers, men who have sex with men (MSM), transgender and hijra people, people who inject drugs (PWID), migrant people, and partners of key population members, further differentiated by gender, hijra/transgender identity, migration history, marital status, time since diagnosis, and duration of ART to capture varied lived experience along the HIV care pathway. The findings of this modest sample are supported by five features: a narrow aim, a well-defined population (young people living with HIV age 18 to 30), in-depth dialogic interviews, and a case-focused thematic framework analysis [39], and analysis based on existing theory (emerging adulthood and HIV-stigma frameworks). This range is supported by empirical findings, which indicate that thematic saturation is usually reached after 12 interviews in a focused purposive sample [38], and code saturation is typically achieved after nine and meaning saturation is usually reached after 16-24 interviews [40]. 20-30 is a sample size above both thresholds and allows for the planned key-population versus general-population comparison, while other studies of YLWH have been published with 10-20 participants [21, 41].

Adequacy is determined by information power and data richness [39], and recruitment will take place until no new themes are found within each group, and saturation will be checked continuously as qualitative research does not use statistical power calculations.

Sampling Technique

Purposive sampling will be done by maximum variation in order to capture a variety of experiences and elicit common trends in those experiences [39], and to gain maximum understanding of the information, rather than statistical representation. Recruitment will be done in co-ordination with Ashar Alo Society staff and the PLHIV Network who will follow up with potentially eligible clients for recruitment.

Additionally, all participation will be completely voluntary: no client will be contacted by the research team without the organisation's facilitation and the participant's consent to be contacted. The instruments for data collection and the procedure used. Semi-structured in-depth interviews (IDIs) with youth living with HIV (YLH) aged 18-30 years will be used to gather qualitative data, which will be conducted to give participants the opportunity to describe sensitive experiences in their own words and cover the objectives uniformly [42]. The guide was developed through systematic review of the objectives, framework and literature on testing behaviour, diagnosis, stigma, disclosure, ART linkage, adherence, PrEP, U=U and youth-friendly services, and relevant HIV guidelines and prevention frameworks [30, 43]. These informed the domains and wording but will not be administered as measurement scales: the researcher, not the instrument, is the primary tool for rigour, and rigour will be sought through trustworthiness, reflexivity and information power, not psychometric validation [39, 44]. The guide will be piloted with one or two similar participants to clarify, cultural appropriateness and sensitivity, flow, and duration [44, 45]; pilot interviews may be incorporated if appropriate and no significant changes are made after the pilot interview [28, 46]. It will be translated into Bangla with back translation of meaning and tone.

Data analysis plan

Data analysis will be conducted using the Framework Method which is appropriate for qualitative health research, as it entails both deductive coding of the objectives and interview guide, and inductive coding of any unexpected meanings [28, 47]. It has five steps: Familiarisation with transcripts and as necessary with original Bangla recording materials; Development of initial thematic framework; Indexing and coding of all transcripts using MAXQDA, where 20 to 25 percent of transcripts will be coded independently by two or more investigators, with discrepancies resolved through discussion and with the assistance of a third investigator acting as adjudicator for the codebook; Charting into thematic matrices to compare the key population and general-population groups; and mapping and interpretation of themes, subthemes and relationships [47]. MAXQDA is used for data management, not data interpretation. The saturation will be measured individually for every group. To ensure credibility, dependability, confirmability and transferability as per Lincoln and Guba [48], rigour will be achieved by means of verbatim transcription, translation checking, an audit trail, and reflexive notes. Reporting will be done using COREQ [31, 49]. Discussion

Expected Outcomes

This is a qualitative exploratory study and the expected findings are likely to be thematic in-depth rather than prevalence estimates or statistical associations. The study is expected to yield a theme-based narrative of the lived sequence of exposure-to-diagnosis, including the risks and symptoms and triggers

Dissemination

Dissemination will involve translation of findings into actionable knowledge products that will inform those who design HIV services for young people in the country and support protection of participants from identification, using non-stigmatising, person-first language and leveraging COREQ for reporting.

Results will be disseminated via seminars, the technical report to the ethics committee and gatekeeper organisation, policy brief, presentations at conferences, and through at least one peer-reviewed publication, sharing with stakeholders via NASC (National AIDS/STD Control), NGOs and community groups such as Ashar Alo Society and PLHIV Network Bangladesh. Data will be used in a purely academic fashion.

Conclusion

Despite being a low prevalence HIV context, HIV diagnoses in Bangladesh are increasing at a disproportionate rate for young people, and there is little qualitative evidence understanding the pathway of suspected exposure, to diagnosis and beyond. This study seeks to leverage youth-friendly testing and expand awareness of PrEP, while supporting linkage to ART, challenging stigma, and providing locally generated evidence to inform HIV policy nationally, by understanding the experience of this journey as a lived one and by comparing key-population and general-population experiences.
