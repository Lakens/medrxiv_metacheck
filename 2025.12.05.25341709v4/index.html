Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Abstract

INTRODUCTION: Metabolic dysregulation is a hallmark of Alzheimer's disease (AD), but its temporal relationship to disease phenotypes remains unclear.

We analyzed 506 serum metabolites in 4,063 longitudinal samples from 1,430 Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative participants using cross-sectional single-timepoint analysis, joint multi-timepoint analysis, and time-interaction analysis. RESULTS: Across 15 AD-related phenotypes, 311 metabolites were significantly associated with disease. Joint multi-timepoint analysis identified 281 metabolites, 243 in cross-sectional analyses, and 19 in time-interaction analysis. Associated metabolites implicated fatty acid and energy metabolism, neurotransmitter pathways, and oxidative stress regulation. Overall, 128 metabolites showed persistent associations over time, consistent with chronic systemic metabolic disruption. Only a small subset, including betaine, choline, and few amino acids, showed steeper declines with worsening AD endophenotypes.

DISCUSSION: These findings indicate that most metabolic alterations in AD remain stable once established, while fewer continue to change during progression, clarifying the timing of metabolic dysregulation across the AD trajectory.

1. Background

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is characterized by the accumulation of amyloid-beta plaques and neurofibrillary tau tangles in the brain 1 . Further hallmarks of the disease are dysregulated glucose uptake, impaired cholesterol transport via Apolipoprotein E, and neuroinflammation, leading to neurodegeneration and ultimately dementia.

Previous studies have demonstrated that multiple metabolic pathways are altered during the onset and progression of AD, highlighting disruptions in lipid metabolism, amino acid catabolism, energy pathways, neurotransmission, and oxidative stress response, among others.

Trushina et al. 2 identified alterations in mitochondrial and lipid metabolism in plasma and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of individuals with mild cognitive impairment (MCI). Han et al. 3 demonstrated alterations in sphingolipid and sterol metabolism linked to cognitive decline.

Toledo et al. 4 showed that changes particularly in acylcarnitines, sphingomyelins, and phosphatidylcholines, are associated with AD progression and clinical outcomes.

MahmoudianDehkordi et al. 5 suggested that the gut-brain axis may contribute to AD through altered bile acid metabolism. Another study showed that 18 plasma metabolites, such as amino acids and lipids, were able to predict future dementia risk 6 . Moreover, brain metabolomics studies in humans revealed region-specific changes. Ambeskovic et al. 7 found increased levels of post-mortem hippocampal N-acetylaspartate, gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), and leucine in AD. In contrast, Batra et al. 8 showed that lower GABA levels in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) are associated with AD traits. Disruptions in neurotransmitter systems have been generally well documented in AD 9 , including neurotransmitter level changes in blood 10.

Finally, studies have suggested the role of oxidative stress as a potential driver of neurodegeneration in AD. Knörnschild et al. 11 reported disrupted glucose oxidation and elevated oxidative stress metabolites (e.g., L-cysteine) in AD mouse models, while Ahmad et al. 12 demonstrated strong associations between CSF isoprostanes and tau pathology in MCI patients, and Batra et al. 8 showed disruptions of the glutathione pathway, among others. Dronse et al. 13 found that elevated morning serum cortisol levels correlated with poorer verbal memory in AD patients. Mosconi et al. 14 found that higher serum cortisol was linked to lower total brain volume, reduced glucose metabolism in the frontal cortex and increased amyloid-ß burden in regions vulnerable to AD.

Despite the large body of consistent links between AD and metabolic dysregulation, some findings vary across tissues and disease stages. For example, decreased lysine levels have been observed in post mortem hippocampal tissue 15 and plasma samples of AD patients 16 , while elevated lysine levels have been found in DLPFC 8 and urine of AD patients 17 . Similarly, lower plasma ceramide levels were observed in patients with MCI compared to cognitively normal individuals and AD patients, whereas higher levels were predictive of greater cognitive decline and greater hippocampal atrophy in MCI patients, suggesting a dynamic evolution of metabolic changes across disease stages 18.

Metabolic signatures are unique to the individual and signature shifts can reflect disease states and underlying molecular mechanisms, especially in complex, progressive diseases such as AD. While cross-sectional metabolomics captures inter-individual differences at a single time point, it cannot track intra-individual changes over time. Longitudinal metabolomics, however, uses repeated measurements from the same individuals to study temporal trajectories of metabolites across disease progression. This dynamic insight is especially important in the prodromal stage of AD, when subtle metabolic alterations may precede clinical symptoms, and the contribution of biological pathways may shift as disease advances. Notably, Huang et al. 19 observed changes in pathways including glutathione, tryptophan, and bile acid metabolism in individuals with physio-cognitive decline compared to normal aging controls. Furthermore, contrasting cross-sectional and longitudinal data permits assessment of the temporal robustness of metabolite-phenotype associations 20.

In this study, we utilized data from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI). ADNI provides longitudinal in-depth phenotypic assessments and metabolite profiles generated by the AD metabolomics consortium collected every 6 to 12 months, enabling evaluation of metabolite associations with AD onset, severity, and progression. Our prior work in ADNI showed a significant 3-4.8% reduction in ether lipid species in individuals who progressed to AD 21 . Building on these findings, we extended our investigation to polar metabolites and adopted a broader analytical approach to evaluate the reproducibility of such associations.

To achieve this, we contrasted metabolic alterations observed in cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses. More specifically, we conducted cross-sectional analyses to identify metabolic differences in AD at single time points, complemented by two types of longitudinal analyses: a joint multi-timepoint analysis examining the persistence of metabolic differences over time; and a time-interaction model to identify metabolites that show a changing relationship to the disease over time. By combining results across analyses, we then categorized metabolites by their association patterns with disease, highlighting those that are most consistently linked to AD or are continuing to change along the disease trajectory. Our findings provide a first reference to prioritize metabolic pathways not only cross-sectionally, but longitudinally, allowing for a better understanding of metabolic disruptions in AD and a more solid basis for potential novel, metabolically-guided therapeutic strategies.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1. Study population and diagnostic classification

Data used in the preparation of this article were obtained from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) database (adni.loni.usc.edu). The ADNI was launched in 2003 as a public-private partnership, led by Principal Investigator Michael W. Weiner, MD. The primary goal of ADNI has been to determine whether serial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), positron emission tomography (PET), biological markers, and clinical and neuropsychological assessments can be combined to measure the progression of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and early Alzheimer's disease (AD). For up-to-date information, see www.adni-info.org. Written informed consent was obtained at enrollment, which included permission for analysis and data sharing. Consent forms were approved by each participating site's institutional review board.

The definition of probable AD in ADNI followed the NINDS-ADRDA criteria. Specifically, individuals with Mini-Mental State Exam (MMSE) scores between 20 and 26 (inclusive) and a Clinical Dementia Rating Scale (CDR) of 0.5 or 1.0 were classified as patients with mild AD and were followed up for a maximum of 2 years into the study 22 . Participants were defined as MCI if they had MMSE scores between 24 and 30, a subjective memory complaint, objective memory loss measured by education-adjusted scores on Wechsler Memory Scale Logical Memory II, a CDR of 0.5, absence of significant levels of impairment in other cognitive domains, and essentially preserved activities of daily living 23.

In this study, we leveraged data from 1524 ADNI-1, -GO, and -2 participants. Clinical data and biological samples were collected at baseline and every 6-12 months for a maximum of 10 years (maximum number of visits = 13). To group participants into a general longitudinally valid diagnostic group, we screened the diagnoses reported for each individual across all visits where metabolomics data was available, resulting in five longitudinal diagnostic classifications: cognitively normal (CN; n=383), stable MCI (n=423), MCI converters (CN to MCI; n=66), AD converters (CN or MCI to AD; n=294), reflecting progression to AD dementia at any time point during the course of the study, and AD (n=264). Individuals exhibiting atypical diagnostic trajectories-such as reversion from MCI or AD to CN, or fluctuation between stages-were excluded from this classification to ensure consistency in group assignment (n=72). As an additional outcome variable, the diagnostic status recorded at each visit was numerically encoded as CN = 0, MCI = 1, and AD = 2 to capture time-resolved diagnostic trajectories for all participants.

2.2. Phenotypic assessments

The available quantitative longitudinal outcomes included: (1) concentrations of amyloid-beta (Aβ 1-42 ) and tau (phosphorylated (p-)tau and total (t-)tau) in CSF, (2) measures of neurodegeneration (glucose uptake measured by Fluorodeoxyglucose-Positron Emission Tomography (FDG-PET), grey matter volume and thickness, entorhinal volume and thickness, and hippocampal volume), and (3) neurocognitive scores (cognition subscale of the 13-item AD assessment scale -ADAS-Cog. 13, ADNI composite scores on memory, executive function, visospatial functioning and language). In total, fifteen clinical phenotypes and diagnostic group comparisons were selected for association analysis, representing diverse readouts that capture various aspects of AD. These phenotypes were defined based on the ATN framework 24 -which classifies AD by amyloid, tau, and neurodegeneration biomarkers-and were extended to include cognitive assessments and diagnostic status. Comprehensive descriptions of these phenotypes and their transformations, along with the corresponding covariates specific to each phenotype, are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Aβ 1-42 , t-tau, and p-tau 181p in CSF samples were measured via a fully automated Roche Elecsys immunoassay applying a sample preparation procedure with two incubation steps 25, 26 . MRI protocol and data were accessed through LONI (https://adni.loni.usc.edu/). In brief, MRI imaging was acquired using a 1.5 T or 3 T MRI scanner with the sequences of T1 and dual echo T2-weighted imaging (ADNI-1), or a 3 T MRI scanner with fully sampled and accelerated T1weighted imaging in addition to 2D FLAIR and T2*-weighted imaging (ADNI-GO/2). To process MRI scans and extract whole brain and ROI (region of interest)-based neuroimaging measurements determined by automated segmentation and parcellation including volumes and cortical thickness, we used FreeSurfer V6 (https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/fswiki) [27] [28] [29] . After the cortical surface was reconstructed, the cortical thickness at each vertex was calculated by taking the Euclidean distance between the grey/white boundary and the grey/CSF boundary at each vertex on the surface.

For FDG-PET, we used a ROI-based measure representing the average glucose uptake across the left and right angular gyrus, left and right temporal lobes, and bilateral posterior cingulate cortex. These values were derived from preprocessed scans (including co-registration, averaging, spatial standardization, and resolution harmonization) and intensity-normalized using a pons region to calculate standard uptake value ratio (SUVR) means 30, 31.

2.3. Metabolomics profiling and data preprocessing

Serum metabolites were quantified using the MXP® Quant 500 platform (Biocrates Life Sciences AG), a targeted, kit-based metabolomics assay. Sample preparation and metabolomics profiling were conducted by the Alzheimer's Disease Metabolomics Consortium at Duke University using 10 µL aliquots resulting in 624 metabolites belonging to 26 biochemical classes. All procedures, including sample extraction, metabolite detection, identification, quantification, and initial quality control (QC), were performed in accordance with standardized protocols 4, 32 . In addition to individual metabolite measurements, the dataset included vendordefined biologically relevant sums and ratios reflecting functions such as enzyme activity. These composite measures (from the MetaboINDICATOR TM software) were derived from raw concentrations and processed in the same manner as single metabolites. Furthermore, we have aggregated metabolites from the 26 biochemical classes into 11 categories to enhance the comprehension of the associations observed across the various classes (see Supplementary Table S2)

A comprehensive quality control procedure was applied to exclude unreliably measured metabolites from the dataset. First, 98 metabolites were removed due to having greater than 30% missingness in at least one cohort (ADNI-1 and ADNI-GO/2 were profiled in two separate runs). Next, an additional 8 metabolites were excluded based on a coefficient of variation (CV) exceeding 30% in quality control reference materials. A further 12 metabolites were removed due to complete missingness across one or more assay plates. In total, 118 metabolites were removed from further analysis. Details of the quality control assessments are provided in Supplementary Table S3).

The resulting dataset comprised 506 metabolites across 26 biochemical classes and 4,884 samples from 1,524 subjects. Biological replicates were averaged (n=41), and samples with more than 30% missing data were excluded (n=10). Multivariate outlier detection using Mahalanobis distance identified outliers with D 2 >1.5 × max(D 2 expected ), leading to the exclusion of 168 samples (6 subjects). Following log transformation and removal of 284 non-fasting samples (15 subjects), missing metabolite values were imputed using random forest-based imputation 33 . Subjects with atypical diagnostic trajectories were removed (subjects = 72; samples = 270). The final three timepoints (48 samples) were excluded to minimize bias. After all filtering steps, the final dataset included 4,063 samples from 1,430 subjects, with approximately 80% of samples collected at baseline, 12 months, and 24 months (hereafter referred to as three main timepoints). The decision to limit timepoints to a maximum of 24 months here was further based on the fact that participants who entered the ADNI studies with mild AD at baseline per study protocol dropped out after 24 months.

2.4. Identification of significant medication effects

To account for the influence of medications on the metabolome, we employed the Boruta feature selection algorithm 34, 35 . The procedure was iterated 100 times to refine the set of selected and rejected features, and those exceeding 4 standard deviations above the mean importance were retained as covariates in subsequent regression models. A total of 38 and 20 significant medication-metabolite associations were identified at baseline and longitudinally at the three main timepoints, respectively. Several of these medications are commonly used in the treatment of Alzheimer's disease, although AD-specific anti-dementia drugs were excluded from the analysis (Supplementary Figure S1; Supplementary Tables S4 and S5).

2.5. Statistical analysis

Single-timepoint cross-sectional analyses were performed to examine associations between metabolite levels and disease phenotypes at each of the three main timepoints. In parallel, two mixed-effects models for repeated measures (MMRMs) were employed to assess metabolitephenotype associations longitudinally. The first model incorporated the three main timepoints, with subject and time specified as random effects (except for time-resolved diagnostic status, where we used time as a fixed effect), to evaluate the consistency of associations across time (joint multi-timepoint analysis). The second model included all timepoints, subjects as a random effect, and a time-by-phenotype interaction term to assess temporal variation in associations (time-interaction analysis). To check for potential bias from the additional timepoints, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using only the three main timepoints as well. We further performed paired t-tests, as an additional line of evidence, in individuals who transitioned from CN to MCI or from MCI to AD within six to twelve months, with analyses restricted to transitions involving ≥30 individuals to ensure adequate power. To rule out spurious associations from imputation, we performed another sensitivity analysis on non-imputed data (Supplementary Table S6).

All regression models were adjusted for sex, age, education, BMI, APOE ε4 status, relevant medications, and phenotype-specific covariates (see section 2.2 and Supplementary Table S1). For lipids, we further included log 2 -transformed levels of total cholesterol and HDL cholesterol as covariates. All analyses were performed using the MeTime R package 36.

For replication of lipid associations, we performed identical analyses on lipid species also available in independently generated lipidomics data 21 from a largely overlapping set of longitudinal ADNI samples. For this, we first mapped lipid species with significant associations from the MXP® Quant 500 platform to the lipidomics dataset. Given the greater analytical resolution of the lipidomics platform compared to the MXP® Quant 500, e.g. for phosphatidylcholines, and the ambiguity of triglyceride annotations in both platforms, this resulted in several 1:n mappings (see Supplementary Table S7). Consequently, replication was defined as follows: if any lipid species in the lipidomics dataset that mapped to a given lipid from the MXP® Quant 500 platform demonstrated a significant association with AD in the same direction, the corresponding lipid in the MXP® Quant 500 platform was considered replicated.

The Li and Ji method 37 was used for multiple hypothesis testing correction, which in a Bonferroni-like fashion adjusts for the effective number of independent metabolic features among the 506 metabolites at baseline (n=122 independent features). A similar correction method was applied to the replication analysis in the lipidomics dataset, where we adjusted for 120 unique lipid species that could be mapped to significant lipids from the MXP® Quant 500 (n= 35 independent features). This approach was chosen because both the metabolites, phenotypes and repeated measures per individual analyzed in this study exhibit strong correlation structures and the conducted association tests are therefore not mutually independent. Because of the strong correlation of the investigated phenotypes (median Spearman correlation = 0.4), no additional correction for the number of phenotypes was applied.

Furthermore, this global significance threshold facilitated comparisons across the different analysis types employed in this study. Less stringent false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted qvalues were also calculated and are reported for all analyses in the respective Supplementary

Tables.

Beyond single metabolite tests, we conducted p-gain analysis 38 to assess whether associations were driven primarily by individual metabolites or by informative sums and ratios (see Section 2.3).

2.6. Bootstrapping analysis

To assess the robustness of significant time-interaction associations, we used participant-level nonparametric bootstrapping. In 1,000 iterations, ADNI participants were sampled with replacement while retaining all longitudinal observations for each selected participant, and the original models were refitted using the same specifications and covariates. For each association, bias was evaluated from the difference between the original effect estimate and the mean bootstrap effect estimate, and its magnitude was summarized using the absolute difference. Studentized bootstrap 95% confidence intervals were calculated using the effect estimate and standard error from each iteration. Shapiro-Wilk tests were used to assess the normality of the effect estimate distribution across bootstraps. Summary p values were computed based on the mean effect estimate and mean standard error across all bootstraps.

3. Results

>Results

3.1. Cohort characteristics

In this study, we leveraged longitudinal metabolomics data generated on 4063 blood samples as well as longitudinal phenotypic data from 1430 participants of the ADNI-1, -GO and -2 cohorts. 1293 participants had metabolomics data available starting at baseline (Table 1). Blood samples were collected at annual or biannual follow-ups after study enrollment, spanning a total of 10 time points and seven years, respectively (Figure 1). The majority of the data (approximately 80%) was concentrated at three main timepoints: baseline, 12 months, and 24 months.

3.2 Identification of persistent, latent, and episodic associations

We first conducted cross-sectional analyses to identify serum metabolites associated with disease phenotypes at each of the three main timepoints separately. We obtained 138 significant associations (at a Bonferroni-adjusted p < 4.1 × 10) at baseline, 186 significant associations at 12 months, and 106 significant associations at 24 months, totaling to 243 unique metabolites (48.02%) that were significant in at least one analysis (Supplementary Table S8; Supplementary Figure S2). Cortisol was the metabolite associated with the largest number of phenotypes at baseline and at 24 months. At 12 months, triglyceride (TG) (18:1/34:4) showed the highest number of phenotype associations. Associated metabolites covered 10 of the 26 metabolite classes assessed here at baseline and 24 months, and 9 classes at 12 months (Supplementary Table S9). Among the tested phenotypes, hippocampal volume (n=98 associations) showed the highest number of associations at baseline, and ADAS-Cog. 13 at 12 months (n=119) and 24 months (n=70) (Supplementary Table S10).

Next, on top of the cross-sectional analyses, we employed linear mixed-effects models referred to as joint multi-timepoint analysis that incorporated the three main timepoints simultaneously, modeling time and subject as random effect variables to test the persistence of associations and their effect directions over time. Here, we discovered 281 out of 506 metabolites (55.53%) with significant associations. The majority of metabolites (94.31%) showed higher levels with more advanced disease phenotypes and diagnostic status, while 15 metabolites showed negative associations. This was consistent with our observations in the single timepoint cross-sectional analyses (Figure 2; Supplementary Figure S2). Cortisol was the metabolite associated with the largest number of phenotypes in the joint multi-timepoint analysis. Overall, metabolites from 20 of the 26 metabolite classes were associated with disease phenotypes in this joint multitimepoint analysis, with triglycerides being the most frequently associated in absolute numbers, and fatty acids the least (Supplementary Table S9). Among the tested phenotypes, hippocampal volume (n=203 associations), ADAS-Cog 13 score (n=109), and the composite memory score (n=129) exhibited the largest number of significant metabolite associations in the joint multi-timepoint analysis (Supplementary Table S10).

We then categorized all significant metabolite-AD associations based on their recurrence across phenotypic endpoints and the two models (Figure 3 and Supplementary Table S11).

Metabolites showing consistent cross-sectional associations at two or more single-timepoint analyses plus were also significant in the joint multi-timepoint analysis were classified as persistently associated metabolites (n=128). Those cross-sectionally significant at none or just one single timepoint but also in the joint multi-timepoint analysis were categorized as latent To further substantiate the temporal classification of metabolites, we performed replication analysis using an independent lipidomics dataset generated on largely overlapping ADNI samples (Supplementary Tables S12-S14 and Supplementary Figure S3) and compared results across platforms. Out of 265 lipids showing significant associations, 189 could be mapped to 120 unique corresponding lipid species in the lipidomics dataset (Supplementary Table S7). Of these 189 lipids, 129 (68.25%; n=81 unique lipids) could be successfully replicated. Specifically, 76 lipids were classified as persistent, of which 65 (85.53%) were replicated; 98 were classified as latent, of which 63 (64.29%) were replicated; and 15 were classified as episodic, of which 1 (6.67%) were replicated, supporting the proposed temporal classification by reproducibility based on replication success.

3.3. Identification of evolving metabolic changes over time

In the time-interaction analysis, we extended this framework by including time-by-phenotype as an interaction term, allowing effect sizes to vary across time and thus capturing whether the strength or effect direction of associations changed longitudinally (Figure 4a). As a sensitivity check we performed two additional analyses: a subset time-interaction analysis restricted to the three main timepoints, and a paired t-test analysis on subjects who transitioned from CN to MCI or MCI to AD across any time interval for each metabolite. A metabolite was considered to significantly change longitudinally with disease if it showed: (i) significance in both the full and subset time-interaction analysis; (ii) significance in the full time-interaction analysis (p < 4.1 × 10; Figure 3) and paired t-test analysis (p < 0.05); or (iii) significance in all the three analyses. In case of associations with time-resolved diagnostic status, no additional criteria were assessed.

Using the aforementioned criteria, we identified 19 metabolites (3.75%) that were significantly associated. These included aspartic acid, leucine, betaine, valine, cysteine, glutamate, phenylalanine, serine, xanthine, choline, the LPCs C16:1, C17:0, C18:0, and C18:1, PC aa C32:3, PC ae C40:4, PC ae C40:5, PC ae C42:4, and PC ae C42:5. Among these, valine, LPC C16:1, LPC C17:0, and PC aa C32:3 were supported by both the full time-interaction analysis and paired t-test analysis (Supplementary Table S15), while betaine and the remaining four PCs were significant in the full time-interaction analysis associated with the time-resolved diagnostic status. The remaining metabolites were consistently significant in both the full and subset time-interaction analysis with aspartic acid showing all three lines of evidence. From a phenotype perspective, grey matter volume and grey matter thickness (n=7 metabolites each) exhibited the largest number of metabolite associations (Supplementary Table S10; Supplementary Figure S2).

3.4. Association patterns observed across the different analyses

To directly compare cross-sectional and longitudinal results, we defined concordant effects as associations with the same effect size direction across single timepoint cross-sectional or joint multi-timepoint analysis and the time-interaction analysis, and discordant effects as associations with opposite effect size directions. Concordant effects were observed for the branched-chain amino acids valine and leucine, which showed consistently lower levels in individuals with reduced grey matter volume in both the joint multi-timepoint analysis and the time-interaction analysis. Cysteine also demonstrated a concordant positive association with AD versus CN, detectable both in the 12-month cross-sectional analysis and the time-interaction analysis.

Notably, cysteine synthesis (expressed as the ratio of cysteine over the sum of serine and methionine) was the only MetaboINDICATOR showing a significant p-gain in the timeinteraction analysis for the AD versus CN comparison (see Supplementary Tables S16-S18 and Supplementary Figure S4; p-gain = 3371.48). In contrast, discordant effects were identified for PC ae C40:4, PC ae C42:4, and PC ae C42:5, which showed a positive association with AD in the joint multi-timepoint analysis but negative associations in the timeinteraction analysis. Similarly, choline, phenylalanine, and aspartic acid were elevated at baseline in individuals with AD but declined more sharply over time in the same individuals, leading to opposite effect directions across the two analyses. Phenylalanine and choline were the only persistent metabolites to exhibit a discordant pattern, starting at higher levels and more steeply decreasing levels in AD (as visualized for aspartic acid in Figure 4b-d), while the rest of the metabolites were either latent or episodic in nature.

To assess whether these trends extended to the metabolite-class level, we quantified the proportion of metabolites in each class that were significantly associated with any AD phenotype (Figure 5). This revealed several patterns: Acylcarnitines, ceramides (including hexosylceramides), bile acids, TGs, and DGs showed frequent associations in both single timepoint cross-sectional analyses and in the joint multi-timepoint analysis, but none in the timeinteraction analysis. CEs had few associations at baseline but a greater number at later followup (24 months), but without further significant evidence from the time-interaction analysis. PCs and LPCs were elevated in disease at baseline but declined over time in relation to less favorable phenotypes. Fatty acids were reduced in individuals with less favorable phenotypes, with no significant associations detected in the time-interaction analysis. Finally, SMs, amino acids and biogenic amines, and several other metabolites (such as hexose, indoles and derivatives, choline, and cortisol; Supplementary Table S2) displayed variable or metabolite-specific associations, without a consistent class-level trend besides most metabolites within these classes (except for cysteine) declining over time along the disease trajectory in the timeinteraction analysis.

3.5. Statistical robustness of time-interaction associations

Across the 44 evaluable time-interaction associations, bootstrap estimates closely agreed with the original estimates, with a mean absolute bias of 1.10×10 -4 , a median absolute bias of 4.28×10 -5 , and a maximum absolute bias of 5.12×10 -4 . Bootstrap-t 95% confidence intervals excluded zero for all associations, and 40 of 44 bootstrap effect distributions (90.9%) showed no evidence for deviations from normality. At the study-wide significance threshold, 43 out of 44 associations were significant in more than 50% of bootstrap iterations (i.e., meeting the post-hoc power threshold to replicate associations) with same effect directions, with a median replication proportion of 81.8%. The same 43 associations remained significant according to the meanbeta/mean-SE summary test (Supplementary Table S19). The only exception was the association between PC ae C40:5 and grey matter thickness, which reached the study-wide threshold in only 48.4% of iterations and a bootstrap summary p = 5.52×10 -4.

4. Discussion

In this study, we systematically characterized temporal metabolite-AD associations in serum by distinguishing persistent, latent, and episodic associations, as well as metabolites whose associations changed over time. Across all analyses, 311 metabolites were associated with at least one AD-related phenotype. Most were detected in the joint multi-timepoint analysis (n=281), followed by single-timepoint cross-sectional analyses (n=243), whereas 19 emerged from the time-interaction analysis, which captures longitudinal shifts in disease-related level differences. All results are also accessible in an interactive web portal (https://metabolomics.helmholtz-munich.de/mxtra_ad/).

The larger number of associations in the joint multi-timepoint analysis likely reflects increased statistical power through pooling data across the three main timepoints in linear mixed-effects models. Moreover, the relatively small number of metabolites with significant time-varying effects suggests that many AD-related metabolic alterations remain comparatively stable once established. Indeed, over 40% of significant metabolites were classified as persistent, showing associations in the multi-timepoint analysis and cross-sectionally at at least two timepoints.

With respect to metabolites identified in the time-interaction analysis, 16 out of 19 were also significant in either the cross-sectional or multi-timepoint models. Eleven were latent or episodic, while phenylalanine, choline, LPC C16:1 and C18:0, and PC aa C32:3 showed persistently higher levels in more affected individuals but declined more strongly over time. Except for cysteine, all metabolites with evolving associations showed negative time interactions, indicating steeper decreases in more severely affected individuals. This is especially relevant for metabolites such as PC ae C42:4, which this and earlier work found elevated in AD blood samples 39 . If such metabolites decline faster with disease progression, studies focused on laterstage AD may report conflicting results.

These findings are broadly consistent with prior literature 4, 40, 41 . Our findings on higher levels of sphingomyelins, ceramides, and cholesteryl esters align with reports linking these lipids to poorer AD outcomes [42] [43] [44] [45] . Likewise, higher levels of secondary conjugated bile acids are replicated in earlier plasma, serum, and brain studies 5, 46, 47 . Higher levels of LPCs C16:1 and C18:1 have previously been associated with MCI and memory decline, followed by steep depletion 41, 48 . Elevated cortisol levels are consistent with systemic stress-related changes in brain, CSF, and plasma linked to brain atrophy and neuropathological burden 49, 50 . We further replicated creatinine, an indicator of muscle breakdown and early marker of frailty and dementia risk [51] [52] [53] , to be robustly associated with AD. Hippuric acid, a protective diet-and microbiotarelated metabolite 54 , showed negative associations with AD, in line with previous animal studies 55, 56 . We also extend prior evidence on the role of PCs in AD, including potentially novel findings 39, 45, 57, 58 . Finally, we replicated nearly three quarters of reported lipid associations in an independent lipidomics dataset, with replication rates highest for persistent (~90%) and lowest for episodic associations (~10%), as expected, and provide evidence for time-interaction effect stability through bootstrapping.

Together, these results both replicate and extend previous metabolic findings in AD. Below, we highlight several metabolic domains that appear especially relevant based on present and prior evidence.

4.1 Bioenergetic dysfunction as a chronic feature of AD

Our data strengthens evidence for impaired energy metabolism in AD 59 . Individuals with AD showed elevated TGs, medium-and long-chain acylcarnitines, and reduced free fatty acids in the joint multi-timepoint analysis, suggesting a compensatory yet inefficient shift toward lipidbased energy metabolism 59,60 , consistent with impaired mitochondrial β-oxidation or carnitine shuttle dysfunction 61 . This supports the hypothesis that mitochondrial bioenergetic failure in AD may result in reliance on alternative fuel sources when glucose utilization is compromised 62.

Importantly, many of the elevated TGs, DGs, and acylcarnitines, were persistent, indicating that bioenergetic dysfunction is a chronic component of AD pathophysiology in both brain and periphery. Mitochondrial dysfunction, together with elevated homocysteine, may also contribute to muscle breakdown, potentially explaining the sustained increases in creatinine and alanine.

Further, we found levels of the two branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) valine and leucine were negatively associated with grey matter volume and declined faster over time in AD. This extends earlier findings 63, 64 and is consistent with recent evidence showing lower plasma valine, leucine, and isoleucine in individuals with AD or dementia and a marked decline in BCAA levels after the age of 60 in APOE ε4 carriers 65 . In addition, a previous longitudinal study reported decreasing levels of valine over time to be associated with all-cause mortality 66 . Together these findings may reflect increased BCAA use as alternative substrates for the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. Because BCAA supplementation can promote mitochondrial biogenesis and ATP production in malnourished older adults [67] [68] [69] , their depletion in AD may worsen cellular energy supply. BCAA dysregulation is also linked to insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome, both established AD risk factors 70, 71 . Through mTORC1 signaling, chronic BCAA depletion could impair autophagy and protein turnover, increase oxidative vulnerability, and promote neurodegeneration [72] [73] [74] . Since BCAAs also contribute to glutamate and GABA synthesis, their depletion may further connect impaired bioenergetics to synaptic dysfunction 75.

4.2 Impairment of neurotransmission systems worsens with AD progression

Disrupted neurotransmission, especially in cholinergic, glutamatergic, and GABAergic systems, is central to AD pathology 76, 77 . Consistently, choline was elevated in AD but declined steeply over time with advancing disease. This peripheral depletion of choline in AD may reflect increased compensatory transport into the brain, which in rodents depends on cerebral choline demand 78.

We further found GABA levels to be negatively associated with executive function and grey matter volume and a significant decrease of glutamate levels over time in AD. As noted above, these may be linked to impaired bioenergetics, since BCAA depletion and reduced TCA cycle activity can limit production of α-ketoglutarate, a precursor for both glutamate and GABA 79, 80.

Prior longitudinal studies support a dynamic glutamate trajectory, where peripheral glutamate levels may be elevated years before dementia onset 81 , whereas lower levels closer to diagnosis are associated with faster decline and inverse brain-serum correlations [82] [83] [84] [85] [86] . In our data, lower glutamate levels do not appear to be explained by increased conversion to glutamine, as glutaminase activity in the metaboINDICATOR analysis was not significantly associated with AD.

Finally, similar to choline, phenylalanine, aspartic acid, and serine showed initially higher but steeply decreasing levels in AD. In earlier brain metabolomics work, we observed elevated glutamate, serine, and phenylalanine in AD brain tissue 8 , which could point to altered uptake or compartmental redistribution. Phenylalanine is a precursor for tyrosine and catecholamines, and its dysregulation in AD may contribute to dopaminergic and noradrenergic dysfunction 15, 85, [87] [88] [89].

Altered serine and aspartic acid may likewise affect neurotransmission through their roles as NMDA receptor agonists 15, 90.

Overall, these findings indicate that compensatory responses in neurotransmitter-related metabolism are progressively impaired as disease advances.

4.3 Oxidative stress response and antioxidant failure GABA, besides its role in neurotransmission, and AABA have antioxidant functions, additionally implicating oxidative stress, another hallmark of AD 91 . We found lower GABA and AABA levels to be associated with worse cognitive function and less favorable biomarker profiles, consistent with previous work 92.

Several other metabolites further suggest a role for diet and the gut microbiome in oxidative and inflammatory pathways. Lower tryptophan betaine, an anti-inflammatory marker linked to plantrich diets 93, 94 , was consistently associated with poorer cognitive outcomes. We also detected associations for microbially derived tryptophan metabolites. Indoxyl sulfate, a pro-inflammatory and neurotoxic co-metabolite, was persistently elevated in AD, replicating prior plasma and brain-based studies 49, 95, 96 . In contrast, lower levels of 3-indolepropionic acid (3-IPA), which has anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and neuroprotective properties [97] [98] [99] , were associated with poorer cognition 49, 100 . Because 3-IPA production can be enhanced by low-fat diets, these observations also point toward potentially modifiable aspects of disease 101.

Conversely, several markers of oxidative damage were elevated in AD. For instance, SDMA, a marker of oxidative stress 102 , and homocysteine was positively associated in the joint multitimepoint analysis. Hyperhomocysteinemia is an established dementia risk marker and may reflect subclinical vitamin B deficiency, which is itself linked to oxidative stress and AD 16, 81, [103] [104] [105].

Finally, we observed potentially compensatory increases in cysteine and glycine levels in AD.

Both metabolites are building blocks of glutathione, a key antioxidant previously linked to AD, including in brain tissue 8 . Cysteine additionally increased over time, and cysteine synthesis, approximated by the ratio of cysteine to the sum of serine and methionine, was the only MetaboINDICATOR with a significant longitudinal change. This may reflect an adaptive attempt to buffer oxidative stress or vitamin B-related deficits by increasing conversion of homocysteine via serine to cysteine [106] [107] [108] [109] [110] [111].

However, cysteine is also a precursor for coenzyme A, linking it again to mitochondrial bioenergetics, including the TCA cycle and β-oxidation 112, 113 . In general, oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction are inherently intertwined. And as we found glutamate, the third glutathione precursor, to decline with disease progression, further work is needed to fully disentangle compensation from failure in this interconnected system.

4.4 Limitations and potential for future work

This study has several limitations. First, ADNI-1/GO/2 is enriched for older adults of Caucasian descent, which limit generalizability given that age, ethnoracial background, and genetics affect both metabolism and AD. Second, statistically averaging across participants may mask heterogeneity in individual disease trajectories. Third, CSF measures were largely restricted to baseline, limiting power for longitudinal analyses on CSF amyloid and tau. Fourth, lipid classlevel trends were consistent, but species-level interpretation remains constrained by unresolved acyl chain composition and desaturation sites. In addition, the platform used here is lipidfocused, so broader untargeted approaches may capture additional biology. Finally, while in general our results are very consistent with previous reports and we performed ample analyses to provide estimates for replicability and robustness of our findings, validation in independent studies is required to assess transportability of our findings to the general population. In particular, the observed time-interaction effects require external replication, despite their strong bootstrap robustness and consistency with prior evidence. Episodic associations should likewise be interpreted with caution, as they were observed only at specific timepoints, although this does not preclude their biological relevance.

Future work should integrate longitudinal blood metabolomics paired with brain tissue-derived data along with emerging blood biomarkers while accounting for diet and gut microbiome composition 114, 115 to improve temporal mapping between central and peripheral AD metabolism.

Our findings highlight widespread and interconnected alterations to lipid, amino acid, oxidative stress, energy, and neurotransmitter metabolism. Overall, our results indicate robust but only partly dynamic metabolic abnormalities in AD and underscore the importance of temporal context in metabolomics. As the identified metabolic alterations are involved in pathways regulated across multiple organ systems, biochemical changes in AD may reflect systemic metabolic dysfunction rather than pathological processes confined to the brain. We speculate that longitudinal effects on metabolic substrates relevant across the periphery and the central nervous system as exemplified here mirror globally reduced metabolic flexibility. While circulating and brain metabolite trajectories likely differ, these peripheral alterations could nevertheless influence the metabolic environment in which neurodegeneration progresses. The predominance of persistent alterations may provide important opportunities here, as it suggests sustained windows for pathway-targeted interventions.
