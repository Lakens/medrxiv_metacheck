Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Brain metastases (BMs), the most common intracranial tumor, do not distribute uniformly throughout the brain, but instead preferentially localize to specific regions. Building on known associations with perfusion and the gray-white matter junction, we hypothesized that regional vulnerability to BMs also reflects the cellular and molecular organization of the healthy brain. Methods: In a discovery cohort of 1,406 patients, we generated a voxelwise map of BM distribution and compared it with normative atlases of brain microstructure, vasculature, metabolism, cell-type composition, and gene expression. Associations were tested for replication in an independent multicenter validation cohort of 1,209 patients. Results: In the discovery cohort, BM frequency was higher near the gray-white matter junction and in regions with greater cerebral perfusion, glucose metabolism, and mitochondrial density. At the cellular level, BM frequency was inversely related to astrocyte and microglial transcriptional signatures. Using normative gene expression maps derived from postmortem healthy brain tissue, we identified 17 genes spatially associated with BM vulnerability and specific to metastases relative to other intracranial lesion types. These associations replicated in the validation cohort. The immune checkpoint gene LAG3 ranked among the ten genes most positively associated with metastatic vulnerability across all genes tested (r = 0.40-0.45, p < 0.001). LAG3 expression was also elevated in peritumoral brain metastasis tissue relative to control brain tissue in an independent spatial transcriptomic dataset. Conclusions: These findings suggest that BM vulnerability reflects healthy brain organization and identify LAG-3, a clinically targetable immune checkpoint, as a candidate link between regional brain immune biology and metastatic colonization.

INTRODUCTION

Brain metastases (BMs) are the most common intracranial tumor, occurring in 10-26% of adults with systemic cancer and outnumbering primary brain malignancies roughly ten to one. 1, 2 Patients with BMs carry a poor prognosis, 3, 4 with survival substantially worse than that conferred by systemic disease alone. 5 Successful colonization of the brain requires cancer cells to adapt to a microenvironment unlike that of any other organ. The brain is separated from the circulation by the blood-brain barrier and contains specialized resident cells, such as astrocytes and microglia, that are not found elsewhere in the body. BMs are therefore increasingly understood as a distinct central nervous system disease rather than a simple extension of the primary tumor. 6 BMs are not uniformly distributed throughout the brain but show a well-documented predilection for posterior and infratentorial structures. [7] [8] [9] [10] This pattern is consistent with the "seed and soil" hypothesis, in which regional tissue properties act as permissive "soil" for metastatic "seeds." 11, 12 Prior work has linked this regional vulnerability to cerebral perfusion and proximity to the graywhite matter junction. 13, 14 Here, we tested whether regional vulnerability also reflects the cellular and molecular organization of the healthy brain. Large, publicly available lesion datasets and normative brain atlases now make it possible to test this across the whole brain. Using our previously developed open-source atlas of brain metastasis vulnerability 15 (n = 1,406) as a Discovery cohort and an independent multicenter Validation cohort (n = 1,209), we compared metastasis frequency maps with normative atlases of brain physiology, cell-type organization, and gene expression.

METHODS

Patient Cohorts and Brain Metastasis Atlas Construction

The Discovery cohort comprised 1,406 patients with brain metastases treated with Gamma Knife radiosurgery at NYU Langone Health (NYUMets dataset). 16, 17 Baseline T1-weighted MRI scans and radiosurgery target contours were nonlinearly normalized to the MNI152 template using EasyReg, a deep learning deformable registration algorithm in FreeSurfer, 18 and superimposed to generate a voxelwise metastasis frequency map. 15 The Validation cohort comprised 1,209 patients pooled from three independent cohorts: Brigham and Women's Hospital and Dana-Farber Cancer Institute (BWH/DFCI; n = 702; segmented via algorithm with manual correction), 19, 20 the University of Zurich (n = 193; manual expert segmentations), 21 and UCSF (n = 314; manual expert segmentation). 22 All Validation lesions were normalized to MNI152 space using EasyReg, 18 summed voxelwise across datasets, and divided by the combined sample size so each patient contributed equally. For both cohorts, we also constructed etiology-stratified atlases for lung, breast, melanoma, and other primary malignancies. Patient characteristics and lesion metrics are summarized in Supplementary Table S1; cohort inclusion and segmentation protocols are detailed in the Supplementary Methods.

Normative Brain Atlases

We assembled 14 normative volumetric atlases in MNI152 space spanning microstructural organization (synaptic density, 23 myelin, 24 gray and white matter tissue probability, 25, 26 and graywhite matter junction probability calculated as the voxelwise product of gray and white matter probabilities), vascular architecture (cerebral blood flow, 27 cerebral blood volume, 28 medium-tolarge arterial density, 29 vessel radius, 30, 31 and venous density, diameter, and torsion 32, 33 ), and metabolism (mitochondrial density 34 and glucose metabolism 35 ). Sources are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Whole-brain transcriptomic maps for 15,633 genes were obtained from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA) 36 using previously generated voxelwise expression maps 37 (N. Nolan, unpublished manuscript) processed with the abagen toolbox 38 via standard filtering, hemispheric mirroring, probe selection, and donor normalization parameters (Supplementary Methods). To estimate regional cell-type signatures across the 31 cell-type superclusters defined in the Human Brain Cell Atlas, 39 we extracted the AHBA spatial expression maps for each supercluster's canonical marker genes (median 23 genes per cell type) and computed their first principal component across voxels to capture shared spatial variance (Supplementary Methods).

Spatial Correlation and Statistical Analysis

All normative atlases and metastasis frequency maps were resampled to 2 mm isotropic resolution, and spatial associations were quantified as voxelwise Pearson correlations (Fig. S1). Significance was assessed against 5,000 null maps in which lesions were randomly relocated within the brain parenchyma while preserving lesion count and volume (Supplementary Methods). 15 Two-sided empirical P values were corrected with the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure within each biological category (microstructural, vascular, metabolic, cellular, and transcriptomic; α = 0.05). Features significant in the Discovery cohort were tested in the Validation cohort, with FDR correction across those features, and were considered reproducible if significant in both cohorts with concordant direction. Analyses were performed in Python and R (packages and versions in Supplementary Methods).

Transcriptomic, Checkpoint, and Sensitivity Analyses

Candidate genes were nominated in three steps. First, from the full set of 15,633 gene expression maps, we selected the 10 genes most positively and 10 most negatively correlated with the Discovery metastasis map. Second, we retained genes whose correlation with the Validation metastasis map fell in the same tail of the distribution of Validation correlations across all 15,633 genes (two-tailed α = 0.025, meaning above the 98.75th or below the 1.25th percentile). Third, the surviving candidates (17 of 20) were re-tested with the spatial permutation framework, with FDR correction across candidates; all 17 met FDR-corrected P < 0.001 in both the Discovery and Validation cohorts. Disease specificity was assessed by comparing normative expression averaged within patient-level metastasis masks against public lesion masks of stroke, 40 meningioma, 41 focal cortical dysplasia, 42 and multiple sclerosis, 43, 44 and matched random lesions, using FDR-corrected Mann-Whitney U tests. Independence from physiological features was assessed by re-correlating gene maps against the residuals of an ordinary least squares model predicting Discovery metastasis frequency from the reproducible microstructural, vascular, and metabolic features. Cross-cohort gene rank reproducibility was assessed with Spearman's rank correlation. Pathway enrichment was evaluated on ranked Discovery correlation lists using gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) 45 across Gene Ontology Biological Processes and cell-type marker sets 39 (FDR α = 0.05), and significant sets were re-tested in the Validation cohort (Supplementary Methods).

To verify that candidate associations were not driven solely by cerebellar-cerebral differences, spatial correlations and 5,000-permutation tests were repeated restricting all maps to a supratentorial mask (Supplementary Methods). Reproducible physiological, cellular, and transcriptomic associations were also recalculated across etiology-stratified atlases, including melanoma-specific candidate nomination and GSEA (Supplementary Methods). Finally, we compared six canonical immune checkpoint genes (PDCD1, CD274, CTLA4, LAG3, HAVCR2, and TIGIT) 46 with the Discovery and Validation maps as well as a map combining both cohorts for a single summary of spatial similarity; Fig. 6A), and confirmed LAG3 expression across human non-small cell lung cancer brain metastasis spatial RNA-sequencing data 47 (comparing peritumoral [n = 20] vs. control [n = 7] samples via Welch's t-test), healthy pig brain regions, 48 and single-cell human brain cells 39 (Supplementary Methods).

RESULTS

The Discovery cohort comprised 1,406 patients treated with Gamma Knife radiosurgery at NYU Langone Health, whose lesion locations we have described previously. 15 The Validation cohort comprised 1,209 patients pooled from three previously described cohorts at Brigham and Women's Hospital and Dana-Farber Cancer Institute (BWH/DFCI), 19 UCSF, 22 and the University of Zurich 21 (Supplementary Table S1). Discovery cohort lesions were the tumor target contours drawn for radiosurgery planning. Validation cohort lesions were segmented manually by experts (UCSF, Zurich) or by a segmentation algorithm with manual review and correction (BWH/DFCI, see Methods). In each cohort, lesions were spatially normalized to a common anatomical template and superimposed to generate a voxelwise map of metastasis frequency (Fig. 1A). Consistent with prior reports, 8, 14 metastasis frequency varied across brain regions in both cohorts.

To test whether regional properties of brain tissue act as the "soil" in the "seed and soil" model of metastasis, 11, 12 we assembled a panel of normative atlases spanning microstructural, vascular, metabolic, cell-type, and transcriptomic brain organization, each derived independently of any metastasis data (Fig. 1B; Supplementary Table S2). We spatially correlated each atlas with the metastasis frequency maps on a voxelwise basis (Fig. 1C). Significance was assessed by spatial permutation testing, in which lesion locations were randomized throughout the brain parenchyma while preserving the number and volume of observed lesions. Associations were identified in the Discovery cohort and tested for replication in the Validation cohort; only associations that remained significant after multiple-comparison correction in both cohorts, with concordant directionality, were considered reproducible.

Metastases preferentially localize to metabolically active, highly perfused regions at the gray-white junction.

Consistent with prior studies, 13, 14 BMs localized preferentially to the gray-white matter junction and to highly perfused regions, and additionally favored metabolically active regions supplied by smaller-caliber vessels (Fig. 2; all FDR-corrected P < 0.05 within category). Of five microstructural atlases, only gray-white junction probability was reproducibly associated with BM frequency (Discovery r = 0.20, Validation r = 0.12). Of seven vascular atlases, three were reproducibly associated: cerebral blood flow positively (Discovery r = 0.24, Validation r = 0.15), and vessel radius (Discovery r = -0.22, Validation r = -0.28) and intermediate-sized vessel density (Discovery r = -0.19, Validation r = -0.22) inversely. Both metabolic atlases were positively associated, including mitochondrial density (Discovery r = 0.20, Validation r = 0.21) and glucose metabolism (Discovery r = 0.28, Validation r = 0.20). Because these MRI-derived vascular atlases resolve only medium-to-large arteries, the inverse associations indicate that BMs occur less often where large vessels predominate, not that metastasis-prone regions are less vascularized overall. Together with the positive association with cerebral blood flow, this pattern suggests that BMs favor well-perfused tissue supplied predominantly by smaller vessels.

Metastasis-prone regions show low astrocyte and microglial transcriptional signatures.

To test whether metastasis distribution relates to the cellular composition of brain tissue, we compared the BM distribution maps to spatial maps of specific brain cell types. Because direct measurements of cell-type abundance are not available across the whole human brain at voxel resolution, we estimated cell-type maps from normative gene expression. For each cell-type supercluster defined in the Human Brain Cell Atlas, 39 we took the marker genes identified by single-cell sequencing and summarized their spatial expression in the Allen Human Brain Atlas as the first principal component (Fig. 3). We focused primarily on non-neuronal cell types given their established roles in vascular regulation, immune signaling, and maintenance of the brain microenvironment. Of nine non-neuronal cell types, five showed reproducible associations: BMs occurred more frequently in regions with lower astrocyte (Discovery r = -0.39, Validation r = -0.42), microglia (Discovery r = -0.36, Validation r = -0.36), oligodendrocyte precursor cell (Discovery r = -0.32, Validation r = -0.37), and fibroblast (Discovery r = -0.27, Validation r = -0.28) signatures, and in regions with higher Bergmann glia signatures (Discovery r = 0.23, Validation r = 0.35) (Fig. 4; all FDR-corrected P < 0.05). Of 22 neuronal cell types, 12 showed reproducible associations, all inverse (Fig. S2).

Bergmann glia are found primarily in the cerebellum, where they perform astrocyte-like functions; 49 their positive association may therefore largely reflect the high frequency of BMs in the cerebellum. Outside this cerebellum-specific population, BMs were more common in regions with weaker astrocyte and microglial signatures, suggesting that regional differences in glial composition relate to metastatic susceptibility.

Metastasis-prone regions show high normative expression of LAG3, FGF9, and ALDH1A3.

We next asked whether the spatial distribution of BMs resembles the expression pattern of specific genes in the healthy brain. The Allen Human Brain Atlas measured gene expression at thousands of sites in postmortem brains from six healthy donors, from which whole-brain maps of where each gene is expressed can be generated. 36, 38 We correlated the BM frequency maps with these maps for 15,633 genes. To nominate candidates, we required each gene to rank among the ten most positively or negatively correlated genes in the Discovery cohort and to replicate in the Validation cohort (see Methods). Seventeen genes (nine positive, eight negative) met both criteria (Fig. 5). These genes remained highly ranked after accounting for the gray-white junction, vascular, and metabolic features identified above (Fig. S3), indicating that their associations are not explained by regional blood flow or metabolism.

Gene rankings across all genes were highly reproducible between cohorts (Spearman's rho = 0.94). Among genes with higher expression in metastasis-prone regions, we highlight three representing distinct biological processes: LAG3, associated with immune regulation (Discovery r = 0.45, Validation r = 0.40); FGF9, associated with cellular differentiation (Discovery r = 0.46, Validation r = 0.45); and ALDH1A3, associated with metabolism (Discovery r = 0.46, Validation r = 0.41) (Fig. 5B). Genes with lower expression in metastasis-prone regions included IL13RA2 (Discovery r = -0.43, Validation r = -0.43), whose expression was highest in regions relatively spared by metastases.

To test whether these associations were specific to BMs rather than a feature of any brain lesion, we compared candidate gene expression at metastasis locations with expression at the locations of other intracranial lesions, including focal cortical dysplasia, meningioma, multiple sclerosis, stroke, and randomly generated lesions (Fig. S4). Expression of all seventeen candidate genes differed significantly at metastasis locations relative to these other lesion types (all FDRcorrected P < 0.001, Fig. 5C).

To test whether broader groups of genes showed the same pattern, we performed gene set enrichment analysis, which asks whether genes sharing a biological function are concentrated at the top or bottom of the ranked list of gene-metastasis correlations. We tested Gene Ontology biological processes and Human Brain Cell Atlas cell-type transcriptional programs. Gene sets related to immune and glial function were reproducibly concentrated among genes with lower expression in metastasis-prone regions, including myeloid leukocyte activation, leukocyte proliferation, antigen presentation, and astrocyte and microglia transcriptional programs (Fig. 5D).

Candidate associations persist within the supratentorial compartment.

BMs occur disproportionately in the posterior fossa, [7] [8] [9] [10] and the cerebellum differs from the rest of the brain in its cellular and transcriptional programs. 50 The associations above could therefore reflect features that distinguish the cerebellum from the cerebrum rather than variation within the cerebrum. To test this, we repeated the analysis using supratentorial voxels only (Fig. S5). Gene associations were modestly attenuated in the Discovery cohort (LAG3, r = 0.45 whole brain vs 0.41 supratentorial; FGF9, 0.46 vs 0.40; ALDH1A3, 0.46 vs 0.42; IL13RA2, -0.43 vs -0.38), and all 17 candidate genes remained significant in both cohorts by spatial permutation testing (all FDR-corrected P < 0.001). Within the supratentorial compartment, all cell-type associations also remained significant in both cohorts except Bergmann glia, a population restricted to the cerebellum. These results suggest that the candidate features partly reflect differences between the cerebellum and cerebrum, which may contribute to the posterior fossa predilection of BMs, but also track metastatic vulnerability outside the posterior fossa.

The immune checkpoint gene LAG3 is elevated in metastasis-vulnerable brain regions.

We next examined six canonical immune checkpoint genes (PDCD1 [PD-1], CD274 [PD-L1], CTLA4, LAG3, HAVCR2 [TIM-3], and TIGIT). LAG3 showed the strongest positive spatial correlation with metastasis vulnerability (Fig. 6A; see Fig. S6 for correlations with the Discovery and Validation maps individually) and was also among the seventeen candidate genes identified across all genes above. LAG-3 is an inhibitory immune checkpoint receptor that suppresses Tcell activation and contributes to the immunosuppressed microenvironment in cancer. 51 Gene set enrichment analysis also identified lower expression of immune-related gene sets in metastasisprone regions (Fig. 5D), suggesting that immunoregulatory pathways may contribute to regional metastatic susceptibility.

Because these findings relied on normative atlases derived from healthy brain tissue, we next tested whether LAG3 expression is also elevated in the metastasis microenvironment. In an independent spatially resolved dataset of surgically resected human brain metastases and control brain tissue, 47 LAG3 expression was higher in peritumoral metastasis tissue than in control tissue (t = 3.28, P < 0.01) (Fig. 6B), suggesting that the normative association extends to diseased brain tissue.

As a further check, we confirmed that LAG3 is expressed in healthy brain tissue in two additional datasets. In the healthy pig brain, 48 LAG3 expression was detectable across regions and peaked in the cerebellum. In a single-cell atlas of the healthy human brain, 39 LAG3 was expressed in T cells and other immune populations, as well as in populations not traditionally considered immunerelated, such as Purkinje cells (Fig. S7, Supplementary Table S3).

The metastasis vulnerability niche generalizes across carcinoma subtypes but diverges in melanoma.

To assess whether the vulnerability niche generalizes across metastasis etiologies, we recalculated all significant, reproducible associations separately for etiology-stratified distributions of primary lung, breast, other carcinomas, and melanoma. Physiological, cell-type, and gene-level associations were broadly consistent across lung, breast, and other carcinomas. Melanoma diverged: cell-type correlations were consistently attenuated or reversed, and candidate genes, including LAG3, showed weaker or divergent associations (Fig. S8); gene-level reproducibility between Discovery and Validation was also lower for melanoma than for the pooled analysis (Spearman's rho = 0.81; Fig. S9).

Applying the same candidate criteria to the melanoma-specific maps identified five reproducible genes: TNNT2 and PART1 were positively associated with melanoma metastasis frequency, and MAF, HLA-DOA, and LINC00886 were inversely associated (Fig. S9B). Gene set enrichment analysis of melanoma-specific correlations further identified reproducible enrichment for gene sets involved in axon guidance and synaptic signaling in both cohorts (Fig. S9C). Leading-edge genes of these sets included components of Wnt and Eph/ephrin signaling, such as WNT7A, EPHB2, and DVL1, pathways implicated in melanoma invasion and metastatic growth. [52] [53] [54] Overall, the features associated with brain metastasis vulnerability were largely shared across carcinoma metastases but differed for melanoma, a malignancy of neuroectodermal origin.

DISCUSSION

Across two independent cohorts, brain regions frequently colonized by metastases aligned with vascular, metabolic, cellular, and molecular features of the healthy brain. Guided by the seedand-soil hypothesis, 11 we asked whether regional properties of the "soil" contribute to where metastases form, comparing metastasis distributions with normative atlases acquired independently of disease. Metastasis-prone regions showed higher cerebral perfusion, increased metabolic activity, greater proximity to the gray-white matter junction, lower astrocyte and microglial transcriptional signatures, and elevated normative expression of several genes, most prominently the immune checkpoint gene LAG3. These findings support a model in which brain metastasis susceptibility reflects interactions between circulating tumor cells and regional properties of the host tissue organized across multiple biological scales.

These findings extend longstanding theories regarding why certain regions of the brain are preferentially colonized by metastatic cells. The seed-and-soil hypothesis proposes that successful metastasis requires compatibility between disseminated tumor cells and the tissue environment they encounter. 11, 12 Mechanical theories instead emphasize the trapping of circulating tumor cells within microvasculature, particularly at vascular transition zones such as the gray-white matter junction. 13, 55, 56 More recent studies have shown that seed-soil compatibility reflects complex interactions involving tumor cell surface markers, 57, 58 pre-metastatic niche formation, 59 metabolic adaptation, [60] [61] [62] blood-brain barrier remodeling, [63] [64] [65] glial interactions, 66 and immune evasion. 58, 67 Our analyses addressed the host-tissue side of three of these processes, metabolism, glial composition, and immune signaling, and each showed reproducible spatial associations with metastasis vulnerability. This suggests that at least some determinants of seedsoil compatibility vary regionally across the brain. The normal brain is increasingly recognized as exhibiting regional heterogeneity across multiple biological scales, including vascular, [68] [69] [70] metabolic, 71 cellular, 72 transcriptomic, 36, 39, [73] [74] [75] and immune 76, 77 processes. These intrinsic differences may collectively define region-specific niches that influence the permissiveness of the brain microenvironment to metastatic colonization.

Metastasis-prone regions showed greater perfusion, glucose metabolism, and mitochondrial density, and were supplied by smaller-caliber vessels. The perfusion and gray-white junction associations replicate prior work by Barrios et al., 14 and the additional vascular and metabolic associations suggest two complementary explanations. The first concerns delivery: regions with higher blood flow receive more circulating tumor cells, and smaller-caliber vessels favor their arrest, consistent with mechanical models of metastatic seeding. 13, 55, 56 Because cerebral blood flow and glucose metabolism are tightly coupled, 78 part of the metabolic association may reflect this shared vascular supply. The second concerns compatibility: brain metastatic cells adapt their metabolism to colonize the brain, [60] [61] [62] and regions with high baseline glucose availability and oxidative capacity may better support these demands. Our data cannot distinguish these explanations, which are not mutually exclusive.

Prior studies of brain metastasis have focused primarily on the tumor microenvironment after colonization, showing that astrocytes, 79 microglia, 80 T cells, 81, 82 and other resident brain cells can support tumor growth, immune suppression, and treatment resistance. 47, 66, 83, 84 In contrast, we found that metastases preferentially localize to regions with low astrocyte and microglial transcriptional signatures in the healthy brain. These findings are not necessarily contradictory, because the two lines of evidence address different stages of disease. Before colonization, astrocytes and microglia can contribute to tissue surveillance and anti-tumor responses; after tumor entry, they may be co-opted into tumor-supportive states that promote growth and therapeutic resistance. 80, 83 Our normative maps capture the first of these stages: the baseline cellular landscape that circulating tumor cells encounter on arrival.

Experimental evidence supports a protective role for microglia at this stage. In a breast cancer model, mice genetically lacking microglia developed more brain metastases and showed impaired natural killer and T cell responses. 85 Conversely, prophylactic microglial stimulation reduced the formation of new brain metastases. 86 Human data are consistent with this pattern: the cerebellum, one of the most frequently colonized sites, [7] [8] [9] [10] has among the lowest microglial densities in both the healthy human 77, 87 and mouse brain. 88 Together, these observations suggest that microglia, and possibly other resident glia, may restrain metastatic seeding in the healthy brain even as they support established tumors, which reconciles our findings with the pro-tumor roles reported after colonization.

Among the candidate genes, LAG3 showed the strongest association of any canonical immune checkpoint, and its expression was also elevated in peritumoral tissue from surgically resected brain metastases. 47 LAG-3 is an inhibitory immune checkpoint receptor that suppresses T-cell activation. It contributes to immunosuppressive tumor environments by inhibiting T-cell signaling and by modulating antigen-presenting cell function through MHC class II interactions. 51, [89] [90] [91] Brain metastases develop immunosuppressive niches characterized by impaired antigen presentation, 47 T-cell exhaustion, 82, 92 and immune evasion, 67, 84 and LAG3 expression has been reported within the brain metastasis microenvironment. [92] [93] [94] [95] [96] Our findings extend this observation to the healthy brain: regions with high normative LAG3 expression may provide an immunologically permissive "soil" for metastatic colonization before a tumor is established. LAG-3 may also contribute to the immunosuppressive environment that develops after colonization, through regulatory T-cell-mediated suppression and modulation of antigenpresenting cell maturation. 97, 98 LAG-3 is classically expressed by immune populations, including CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, natural killer cells, and dendritic cells; [99] [100] [101] [102] [103] expression in neurons has also been reported 51, 104 but remains contested. 105 Consistent with this, we detected LAG3 in T cells, natural killer cells, and a subset of neurons in the Human Brain Cell Atlas, 39 and expression in both the Allen Human Brain Atlas 36 and the healthy pig brain 48 was highest in the cerebellum, a region frequently colonized by metastases. Given the emergence of LAG-3 as a therapeutic target in melanoma 106 and its ongoing investigation in glioblastoma, 107 future studies should determine whether LAG-3 contributes causally to brain metastatic vulnerability and whether it represents a therapeutic opportunity in brain metastases.

Additional candidate genes provide complementary hypotheses. FGF9, whose spatial expression ranked among the most similar to that of brain metastasis vulnerability, is a fibroblast growth factor involved in cellular differentiation and tissue repair, 108 and is known to be expressed in the brain, 109 where it regulates development of the cerebellum. 110 It has also been implicated in various cancer-related processes, including stimulating human brain tumor growth, 111, 112 and cancer cell metastasis. 113, 114 ALDH1A3, another top spatial correlate of BM vulnerability, is an aldehyde dehydrogenase implicated in tumor cell metabolism. 115 Conversely, IL13RA2 was among the most negatively associated genes, meaning its normative expression could be interpreted as having a protective effect against BM colonization. Consistent with a protective role, experiments have shown that loss of IL13RA2 promotes brain metastasis. 116 While our reported spatial associations do not establish causality, they nominate LAG3, FGF9, ALDH1A3, and IL13RA2, among others listed above, as candidate regulators of the pre-metastatic brain microenvironment for future mechanistic study.

Our findings also reveal that regional metastatic vulnerability is not entirely universal across tumor origins. Associations observed across lung, breast, and other carcinomas were broadly conserved, whereas melanoma diverged from this pattern. This divergence may reflect unique biological properties of melanoma metastasis, including greater heterogeneity of circulating tumor cells. 117, 118 Notably, melanoma is unique among the tumor types examined in arising from the neuroectoderm; this shared developmental lineage with the central nervous system may partly explain why melanoma metastasis spatial vulnerability diverges from carcinomas. Consistent with this possibility, melanoma-specific spatial associations were enriched for normative brain expression patterns related to axon guidance and synaptic signaling, suggesting that regional variation in neural developmental programs may contribute to the distinct spatial distribution of melanoma metastases.

Several aspects distinguish this work from prior studies of spatial brain metastasis patterns. This represents, to our knowledge, the largest analysis testing brain metastasis vulnerability against normative brain organization, and the first to incorporate independent discovery and validation cohorts to evaluate reproducibility of associations. Prior work by Barrios et al. established important relationships between metastasis location, gray-white matter junction proximity, and cerebral perfusion, 14 but did not examine multimodal atlas-based associations spanning vascular, metabolic, cellular, and molecular organization. While Bao et al. explored relationships between metastasis distributions and normative brain features, they were limited by a smaller, singleinstitution cohort restricted to lung and breast cancer, lower-resolution gray matter parcellation approaches that excluded white matter and infratentorial regions including the cerebellum, and analytic methods designed for surface-based neuroimaging rather than volumetric lesion distributions. 119 By incorporating the entire brain volume, including infratentorial structures, comparing to a more biologically comprehensive panel of normative atlases, and validating findings across large independent patient cohorts, our approach provides the most comprehensive assessment of the biological architecture correlating with metastatic susceptibility to date. The present study also contributes to an emerging framework that uses multimodal, multiscale atlases of normative human brain organization to investigate CNS disease. [120] [121] [122] Prior studies using the same normative gene expression dataset that we use herein have demonstrated that normative gene expression patterns recapitulate functional networks, structural connectivity, neurotransmitter distributions, cortical hierarchy, and regional vulnerability across neurological and psychiatric disorders. 74, [123] [124] [125] [126] [127] [128] [129] [130] [131] [132] [133] [134] [135] [136] [137] [138] [139] More recently, studies have begun linking normative gene expression patterns directly with lesion-related phenotypes, including neurodegeneration, movement disorders, and seizures. 37, [140] [141] [142] [143] [144] We build on this technique by using Allen Human Brain Atlas expression maps to construct cell-type-resolved atlases of the brain, which we then integrate directly with lesion locations. This allows us to identify associations between lesion locations at both the cellular level and at the level of individual gene expression. We show that, as with other lesion etiologies, the spatial distribution of brain metastases can be anchored to the molecular architecture of the healthy brain.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, our analyses are inherently correlative and cannot establish causal relationships between regional brain properties and metastatic vulnerability. Second, normative atlases represent healthy brain organization and may not capture dynamic changes induced by aging, systemic cancer, prior therapies, or interactions with circulating tumor cells. Third, integration across imaging, vascular, and transcriptomic modalities is constrained by differences in spatial resolution, sampling, and biological scale. Because spatial autocorrelation, noise, and sampling density vary across modalities, correlation coefficients across biological domains should not be interpreted as directly comparable measures of effect magnitude. Fourth, transcriptomic and cell-type analyses rely on the Allen Human Brain Atlas, which is derived from six postmortem donors with variable sampling density and spatial coverage. Cell-type maps were further derived from canonical marker genes 39 rather than direct measurements of cell abundance, so a lower regional signal may reflect either fewer cells or transcriptionally distinct populations that weakly express canonical markers; our approach cannot distinguish these possibilities. Fifth, the distribution of radiographically detectable metastases reflects the cumulative outcome of tumor cell arrest, extravasation, survival, and growth; therefore, our atlas cannot distinguish whether identified regional features influence initial seeding, successful colonization, or subsequent expansion, nor how these factors interact with tumor-specific determinants of metastatic behavior. Sixth, the Discovery cohort comprised patients treated with radiosurgery, which may underrepresent large or surgically resected metastases. Segmentation methods also differed across cohorts (radiosurgery contours, manual expert segmentation, and automated segmentation with manual correction); replication despite these differences supports the generalizability of the associations, although method-specific bias cannot be fully excluded. Finally, spatial normalization of brains containing mass lesions is inherently imperfect, and despite modern deformable registration methods, residual anatomical distortion may introduce uncertainty into voxel-level localization estimates.

CONCLUSIONS

Brain metastases preferentially localize to regions of the healthy brain with distinct vascular, metabolic, cellular, and molecular features, and these associations replicated within a large independent cohort. Metastasis-prone regions were highly perfused and metabolically active, showed low astrocyte and microglial transcriptional signatures, and showed high normative expression of the immune checkpoint gene LAG3. These patterns were shared across carcinoma subtypes but diverged in melanoma. Together, these findings suggest that regional vulnerability to brain metastasis is shaped in part by the pre-existing organization of the brain itself, the "soil" that circulating tumor cells encounter on arrival. If confirmed experimentally, this framework could inform region-specific surveillance and nominate the brain's regional immune landscape, including LAG-3, as a target for preventing or treating brain metastases.

SUPPLEMENTARY METHODS

Cohort Details and Lesion Segmentation

In the Discovery cohort (n = 1,406), patients were drawn from the NYUMets dataset. 17, 146 Primary tumor etiologies included lung, breast, melanoma, genitourinary, and gastrointestinal malignancies; detailed cohort characteristics and data generation procedures have been described previously. 17 Metastasis segmentations were the tumor target contours delineated for radiosurgery planning, extracted from the corresponding DICOM RTSTRUCT files. Baseline T1-weighted MRI scans and segmentations were normalized to the MNI152 template distributed with FSL using EasyReg, a deep learning-based deformable registration tool implemented within FreeSurfer. 18, 147, 148 In the Validation cohort (n = 1,209), the Brigham and Women's Hospital (BWH) dataset included 702 patients drawn from a cohort of 1,453 patients with brain metastases. 19 Clinical data, including primary tumor type, were obtained from that study, and pretreatment MRI scans were retrieved for [inclusion criteria; CONFIRM]. Metastases were segmented using an automated deep learning algorithm [SPIRS; CITE Patel et al. 2023, CONFIRM], and each segmentation was manually reviewed and corrected as needed. The University of Zurich dataset included 193 patients, with manual lesion segmentations and primary tumor data obtained from a prior study. 21 The UCSF dataset included 314 patients with expert segmentations from a publicly available Gamma Knife radiosurgery dataset (https://imagingdatasets.ucsf.edu/dataset/1), including patients treated for lung, breast, melanoma, genitourinary, and gastrointestinal malignancies. 22, 145

Normative Atlases of Brain Physiology, Gene Expression, and Cell Types

We assembled normative volumetric atlases spanning microstructural organization, vascular architecture, and metabolism (Supplementary Table S2). Microstructural atlases included synaptic density, 23 myelin, 24 and gray and white matter probability. 25, 26 To estimate gray-white junction probability, we generated a composite atlas by multiplying voxelwise gray and white matter probability values. Vascular atlases included cerebral blood flow, 27 cerebral blood volume, 28 medium-to-large arterial structure density, 29 vessel radius, 30, 31 venous density, vessel diameter, and vessel torsion. 32, 33 Metabolic atlases included mitochondrial density 34 and glucose metabolism. 35 All atlases were distributed in MNI152 space and resampled to 2 mm isotropic resolution using default continuous interpolation in Nilearn. 149 Transcriptomic maps for 15,633 genes were derived from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA), 36 a normative dataset built from postmortem microarray profiling of six donor brains across 3,702 sampled regions. We used voxelwise expression maps from prior work, 37 (N. Nolan, unpublished manuscript) generated using the abagen toolbox 38 with standard recommended parameters: 150 0.5 intensity-based filtering, bidirectional hemispheric mirroring, differential stability probe selection, nearest-neighbor interpolation with donor-wise averaging, and cross-donor robust sigmoid normalization, followed by voxelwise z-scoring.

For each of the 31 cell-type superclusters defined by Siletti et al., 39 we retained the marker genes reported in that publication's Supplementary Table S3 that were represented in the AHBA (median 23 genes per cell type), retrieved the corresponding AHBA expression maps, and computed the first principal component across each cell type's marker genes. Principal component analysis was used instead of voxelwise averaging because normalized expression values are comparable within a gene across voxels but not across genes; the first principal component instead captures spatial structure shared across the marker set.

Simulation of Null Lesion Distributions and Spatial Permutation Testing

To address the concern that if metastases occurred randomly throughout the brain, atlas correlations may arise simply because lesions have finite size and must lie within brain anatomy, we used permutations to test this null hypothesis (Fig. S10). To generate empirical null distributions for spatial permutation testing, we simulated metastasis distributions directly within the brain volume, as previously described 15 (medRxiv, doi: 10.64898/2026.06.29.26356888). This approach was designed to approximate random spatial relocation of lesions while (1) avoiding invalid lesion placements outside brain boundaries, and (2) avoiding the central-brain bias that would otherwise be introduced by excluding such invalid placements. Because volumetric lesion maps cannot be permuted using surface-based approaches such as spherical rotation ("spin tests"), which are valid only for cortical surface data, this voxelwise simulation strategy was used in their place.

Single-lesion growth procedure.

Each simulated lesion was generated by first selecting a seed voxel from within the brain mask (excluding ventricles), with all valid voxels treated as equally likely to be selected. Beginning at this seed, the lesion was grown one voxel at a time by iteratively incorporating a neighboring, not-yet-occupied voxel, with selection weighted toward voxels proximal to the lesion origin. This produced compact, approximately convex lesions consistent in morphology with observed metastases. These simulated lesions exhibited similar sphericity (0.76 ± 0.11 vs. 0.75 ± 0.09) and convexity (1.58 ± 0.68 vs. 1.39 ± 0.55) as the observed NYUMets lesions. Growth continued until the lesion reached a predefined target volume, drawn from the empirical volume distribution of the observed cohort. This procedure was repeated independently to generate as many lesions as desired for a given simulated map.

Generation of the null ensemble and multiple comparison correction.

Using this procedure, we generated 5,000 permuted lesion maps, each matched exactly to the observed cohort in both the number of simulated lesions (e.g., n = 6,011 in the Discovery cohort) and the volume of each individual lesion. Within each permutation, all voxels within the brain mask were treated as equally probable seed and growth locations (i.e., no voxelwise prior was applied), reflecting the null hypothesis of spatially unbiased metastatic distribution. For each simulated map, all lesions were superimposed to generate a simulated frequency map, exactly as done for the observed data. Statistics of interest (e.g., spatial correlations with normative atlases) were recalculated for each of the 5,000 simulated maps to generate an empirical null distribution, against which the true observed statistic was compared. Two-sided empirical P values were calculated as the proportion of simulated statistics with a correlation magnitude equal to or exceeding the observed value and were corrected for multiple comparisons within each a priori biological category (microstructural, vascular, metabolic, cellular, and transcriptomic) using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure 151 (α = 0.05). Atlases significant in the Discovery cohort were tested in the Validation cohort using the same permutation procedure, with FDR correction applied across the atlases significant in the Discovery cohort for that category.

Validation against the observed metastasis distribution.

As a positive control, we additionally generated simulated lesion maps in which voxel selection, both for lesion seeding and lesion growth, was weighted by the observed empirical metastasis frequency map, rather than treated as spatially uniform. This analysis served as an implementation check demonstrating that the lesion-generation procedure itself does not distort an imposed spatial prior. The resulting realistic simulated frequency map closely resembled the true observed metastasis frequency map (spatial correlation r = 0.80), confirming that the simulation framework faithfully captures large-scale patterns of metastatic spatial distribution when supplied with an appropriate prior.

Candidate Gene Validation, Disease Specificity, Covariate Adjustment, and GSEA

In the two-part candidate gene selection criteria, a gene had to rank among the top 10 or bottom 10 spatial correlations with the Discovery metastasis map and exceed a two-tailed significance threshold in the Validation cohort (α = 0.025, corresponding to the 98.75th and 1.25th percentiles of the validation null distribution). Nominated candidates were then confirmed against the spatial permutation framework described above (FDR p < 0.001).

To assess disease specificity, we compared metastasis locations against lesion masks from publicly available atlases of stroke, 40 meningioma, 41 focal cortical dysplasia, 42 and multiple sclerosis, 43, 44 alongside randomly generated lesions matched by the permutation procedure used to construct null metastasis maps. For each candidate gene, normative expression values were averaged within each patient's lesion mask, and expression at metastasis locations was compared with expression at other lesion locations using the Mann-Whitney U test, with FDR correction across candidates (all FDR p < 0.001). This analysis reflects normative regional gene expression at these locations, not expression within the lesions themselves.

To confirm that candidate associations were not driven by the physiological features identified in Figure 2, we fit an ordinary least squares model (statsmodels 152 ) predicting voxelwise Discovery metastasis frequency from the reproducible microstructural, vascular, and metabolic covariates, and re-correlated all gene expression maps against the model residuals. All candidate genes remained highly ranked (top or bottom 25) in this covariate-adjusted analysis.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) 45 was performed on the full ranked list of gene-map correlations from the Discovery cohort using the Gene Ontology Biological Process database 153, 154 and cell-type marker sets, 39 with FDR correction (α = 0.05). Gene sets significant for positive or negative enrichment were re-evaluated using the corresponding ranked list from the Validation cohort.

Supratentorial Sensitivity and Etiology-Stratified Analyses

To test whether candidate associations depended on differences between the cerebellum and cerebrum, we repeated the spatial correlation analysis within a supratentorial mask based on the Harvard-Oxford atlas. 155, 156 The Discovery and Validation metastasis frequency maps, the 17 candidate gene expression maps, and the five reproducible cell-type maps were restricted to voxels within this mask, and Pearson correlations were recomputed. Significance was assessed with the spatial permutation framework using 5,000 null metastasis maps restricted to the same mask, with FDR correction applied separately within the gene and cell-type categories.

For etiology-stratified analyses, all reproducible physiological, cell-type, and gene-level associations were recalculated using the etiology-stratified atlases for lung, breast, other carcinomas, and melanoma. For melanoma, we additionally applied the candidate gene criteria and GSEA 45 to the melanoma-specific Discovery and Validation maps. Leading-edge genes were defined as those shared across multiple reproducibly enriched gene sets and ranked in the top decile of melanoma-associated genes in both cohorts.

Immune Checkpoint and LAG3 Validation

For the six canonical immune checkpoint genes (PDCD1 [PD-1], CD274 [PD-L1], CTLA4, LAG3, HAVCR2 [TIM-3], and TIGIT), 46 AHBA expression maps were spatially compared with the Discovery and Validation metastasis maps; a combined map pooling both cohorts was also used to generate a single summary statistic. We assessed LAG3 expression in an open spatial RNA-sequencing dataset of surgically resected non-small cell lung cancer brain metastases, 47 comparing peritumoral microenvironment samples (n = 20) with control tissue samples (n = 7) using Welch's two-sample t-test (α = 0.05). LAG3 expression was higher in peritumoral than control tissue (Welch's t = 3.28, df = 19.51, p = 0.0038). We additionally quantified LAG3 normalized transcripts per million across pig brain regions from an open dataset. 48 Finally, we characterized the cellular distribution of LAG3 in the Human Brain Cell Atlas 39 (approximately 3.3 million neuronal and non-neuronal cells) as the percentage of cells within each cell type expressing LAG3.
